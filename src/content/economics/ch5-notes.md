# Economics HL — 2.3 Competitive Market Equilibrium

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **Market equilibrium** is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the price has no tendency to change.
> - Above equilibrium, **excess supply (surplus)** means $Q_s > Q_d$ and pushes price down; below equilibrium, **excess demand (shortage)** means $Q_d > Q_s$ and pushes price up.
> - The **price mechanism** coordinates choices through prices: prices communicate information, motivate responses and ration goods.
> - **Marginal benefit (MB)** is the extra benefit from one more unit; **marginal cost (MC)** is the extra cost of producing it. When $MB = MC$, the competitive model is **allocatively efficient** only if the curves capture all relevant social benefits and costs.

## 1. What exam answers must show

This note covers competitive market equilibrium, disequilibrium and price adjustment, the price mechanism, allocative efficiency, and consumer, producer and social surplus.

## 2. Market equilibrium, shortage and surplus

**Equilibrium** is a balance between forces with no tendency to change. **Market equilibrium** is the price–quantity outcome where $Q_d = Q_s$; on a diagram, it is the intersection of demand and supply. The **equilibrium price** ($P^*$) clears the market and the **equilibrium quantity** ($Q^*$) is bought and sold at that price. A **market disequilibrium** is a price at which $Q_d \ne Q_s$.

- At a price **above** $P^*$, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. Unsold stock puts downward pressure on price; as price falls, $Q_d$ rises and $Q_s$ falls.
- At a price **below** $P^*$, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. A shortage puts upward pressure on price; as price rises, $Q_d$ falls and $Q_s$ rises.
- Under the competitive-model assumptions, this adjustment continues until the gap is eliminated at $Q_d = Q_s$.

Tragakes' chocolate-bar schedule (thousands of bars per week):

| Price per bar (book units) | $Q_d$ | $Q_s$ | Market position |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 5 | 4,000 | 12,000 | Excess supply: 8,000 |
| 4 | 6,000 | 10,000 | Excess supply: 4,000 |
| 3 | 8,000 | 8,000 | Equilibrium |
| 2 | 10,000 | 6,000 | Excess demand: 4,000 |
| 1 | 12,000 | 4,000 | Excess demand: 8,000 |

> [!example] Diagram 1: market equilibrium and disequilibrium
> Label the vertical axis “Price per bar” and the horizontal axis “Chocolate bars per week (thousands)”. Draw downward-sloping $D$ and upward-sloping $S$ meeting at $E$; drop dotted lines to $P^*$ and $Q^*$. Above $P^*$, mark $Q_d$ on $D$ and $Q_s$ on $S$, bracket the horizontal gap as excess supply and show a downward price arrow. Below $P^*$, bracket the horizontal $Q_d-Q_s$ gap as excess demand and show an upward price arrow. The gap is horizontal because it measures quantity.

A **free good**, in Tragakes' diagrammatic distinction, has $Q_s > Q_d$ even at a zero price; an **economic good** has $Q_d > Q_s$ at zero price and an equilibrium at a positive price. A sufficiently large rightward shift in demand or leftward shift in supply can make a formerly free good economic. Treat this as a simplified demand–supply illustration, not a complete test of scarcity.

## 3. Curve shifts and the new equilibrium

A change in a non-price determinant shifts a curve; a change in the good's own price causes a movement along a curve. For any shift, start at the initial equilibrium, hold the old price fixed to identify the imbalance, then explain the price change and movement along the curve that did not shift.

| Change | Shift | Imbalance at old price | Price adjustment | New $P^*$ | New $Q^*$ |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| Demand increases | $D_1$ right to $D_2$ | Excess demand | Rises; move up $S$ | Higher | Higher |
| Demand decreases | $D_1$ left to $D_2$ | Excess supply | Falls; move down $S$ | Lower | Lower |
| Supply increases | $S_1$ right to $S_2$ | Excess supply | Falls; move down $D$ | Lower | Higher |
| Supply decreases | $S_1$ left to $S_2$ | Excess demand | Rises; move up $D$ | Higher | Lower |

> [!example] Diagram 2: an increase in demand
> Label axes $P$ and $Q$, draw $S$, $D_1$ and a right-shifted $D_2$. Mark initial equilibrium $A=(Q_1,P_1)$. At old price $P_1$, read the larger quantity demanded on $D_2$ and label the horizontal gap from $Q_s$ as excess demand. Show the price rising and movement up unchanged $S$ to new equilibrium $C=(Q_2,P_2)$, where both $P_2>P_1$ and $Q_2>Q_1$. Add a shift arrow from $D_1$ to $D_2$ and name the cause.

For a supply decrease, reverse the logic: shift $S$ left, show excess demand at the old price, then a rise in price and movement up unchanged $D$ to higher $P^*$ and lower $Q^*$. Examples of determinants include income for a normal good (demand), technology (supply), and weather affecting a harvest (supply).

> [!note] Both curves shift
> If demand and supply shift together, the result depends on their relative sizes. For example, if both shift right, equilibrium quantity rises but equilibrium price may rise, fall or stay unchanged. State what is certain and what is ambiguous; do not invent a direction for both variables.

## 4. The price mechanism: signals, incentives and rationing

The **price mechanism** is the coordination of buyers' and sellers' decisions through prices that adjust in markets. It is the “invisible hand” idea: many separate decisions are coordinated without a central authority.

- **Signalling function:** a price conveys information. A rising price signals relative scarcity or excess demand; a falling price signals excess supply.
- **Incentive function:** a price change motivates a response. A higher output price can make production more profitable, encouraging firms to extend supply; it also encourages consumers to economise. A lower wage can encourage firms to hire more labour while discouraging some workers from supplying it.
- **Rationing function:** **price rationing** allocates a good to people willing and able to pay the market price. **Non-price rationing** allocates it by other methods, such as a first-come, first-served queue, when price cannot clear the market.
- As buyers and sellers respond, resources are reallocated toward goods whose relative prices and profitability have risen and away from those whose prices have fallen.

> [!example] Diagram 3: a demand increase sends a signal
> Label the axes “Price of strawberries” and “Quantity of strawberries per week”. Shift demand right from $D_1$ to $D_2$. At the initial price $P_1$, mark a shortage between old supply and the new $Q_d$. The resulting higher price signals scarcity and rewards producers: move up $S$ to a higher quantity supplied. At the same time consumers move up the new $D_2$ curve and buy less than they wanted at $P_1$. Mark the new intersection and show the price and quantity traded there.

A textbook labour-market example uses the same chain. Put wage on the vertical axis and quantity of labour on the horizontal. Immigration shifts labour supply right. At the old wage there is excess supply of labour, pushing the wage down. Firms move down labour demand and hire more; workers move along the new supply curve and offer less labour at the lower wage. The wage signals the surplus and provides incentives on both sides.

## 5. Allocative efficiency: marginal benefit equals marginal cost

The demand curve can be interpreted as **marginal benefit (MB)**: the maximum a consumer is willing to pay for one more unit, which represents its extra benefit. The supply curve can be interpreted as **marginal cost (MC)**: the lowest price a producer is willing to accept for one more unit, reflecting its extra production cost. In the competitive model, market equilibrium is therefore where $MB = MC$.

- If $MB > MC$, the extra benefit from the last unit exceeds its production cost; society gains by producing more.
- If $MC > MB$, the last unit costs more than its benefit; society gains by producing less.
- At $MB = MC$, the market produces the quantity with the greatest net benefit under the model's assumptions. This is **allocative efficiency**: resources are directed to the combination of output most valued by society.
- For the whole economy to be allocatively efficient, the condition must hold across all markets.

> [!example] Diagram 4: the efficient quantity
> Label the vertical axis “Price / marginal benefit / marginal cost” and the horizontal axis “Quantity”. Draw downward-sloping $D=MB$ and upward-sloping $S=MC$. Their intersection gives $P^*$ and $Q^*$. Mark output below $Q^*$ where $MB>MC$ and output above $Q^*$ where $MC>MB$. The intersection is efficient only if the curves include all relevant social benefits and costs.

This result depends on restrictive conditions. If market power, external costs or benefits, or other failures mean that private demand and supply do not reflect social marginal benefits and costs, the intersection need not be socially efficient. The textbook treats real-world market failure and intervention as later material; do not claim that every observed market equilibrium is efficient.

## 6. Consumer, producer and social surplus

- **Consumer surplus (CS)** is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay. With a straight-line demand curve, it is the area below $D$ and above $P^*$, up to $Q^*$.
- **Producer surplus (PS)** is the difference between the price producers receive and the lowest price they would accept. It is the area above $S$ and below $P^*$, up to $Q^*$.
- **Social (community) surplus (SS)** is $CS+PS$. In the competitive model, it is maximised at the equilibrium quantity, where $MB=MC$.
- “Surplus” by itself usually means excess supply. “Consumer surplus”, “producer surplus” and “social surplus” mean benefits; name the type to avoid ambiguity.

> [!example] Diagram 5: surplus at equilibrium
> Draw $D=MB$ and $S=MC$ intersecting at $E=(Q^*,P^*)$. Shade CS between $D$ and the horizontal $P^*$ line, from the price axis to $Q^*$. Shade PS between the $P^*$ line and $S$ over the same traded quantity. Label $CS$, $PS$, and $SS=CS+PS$. With linear curves, these shaded regions may be triangles; if a curve has a quantity-axis intercept, check whether the area is a trapezium instead.

## 7. HL calculations: equilibrium and surplus

**Method**
1. Set $Q_d=Q_s$ and solve for $P^*$; substitute to find $Q^*$.
2. Find the relevant price intercepts by setting quantity equal to zero. For a supply curve with a positive quantity-axis intercept, identify that intercept too.
3. For straight-line demand, $CS=\frac12(P_{D\text{-intercept}}-P^*)Q^*$.
4. If supply intercepts the price axis, $PS=\frac12(P^*-P_{S\text{-intercept}})Q^*$. For the textbook's supply curve starting on the quantity axis, use its trapezium construction: $PS=\frac12(Q_{S\text{-intercept}}+Q^*)P^*$.
5. Add the two areas for $SS$ and state units. An area measured in (price per unit) times (units per time) is money per time.

**Textbook figures, Tragakes Fig. 2.18 (pp. 68–69):**

| Diagram | Consumer surplus | Producer surplus | Social surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) $P^*=3$, $Q^*=4.5$; demand price intercept $6.5$, supply price intercept $1$ | $\frac12(6.5-3)(4.5)=7.875\approx7.88$ | $\frac12(3-1)(4.5)=4.50$ | $7.875+4.50=12.375\approx12.38$ |
| (b) $P^*=3$, $Q^*=4$; demand price intercept $6.5$, supply quantity intercept $2$ | $\frac12(6.5-3)(4)=7.00$ | $\frac12(2+4)(3)=9.00$ | $7.00+9.00=16.00$ |

> [!warning] OCR correction, not a textbook arithmetic error
> The OCR extract renders Fig. 2.18(a)'s printed total as 12.18. I checked the scan: the book prints 12.38. The arithmetic is also 7.875 + 4.50 = 12.375, which rounds to 12.38. Fig. 2.18(b)'s trapezium result is 9.00 and its total is 16.00; both check out.

**Worked example** (illustrative, not empirical): $Q_d=60-4P$ and $Q_s=-12+8P$, where $P$ is INR per kg and $Q$ is thousand kg per week.

- Equilibrium: $60-4P=-12+8P$, so $P^*=6$ and $Q^*=36$.
- At $P=4$, $Q_d=44$ and $Q_s=20$: shortage $=24$ thousand kg per week. At $P=8$, $Q_d=28$ and $Q_s=52$: surplus $=24$ thousand kg per week.
- Price intercepts: demand $P=60/4=15$; supply $P=12/8=1.5$.
- $CS=\frac12(15-6)(36)=162$ and $PS=\frac12(6-1.5)(36)=81$. Therefore $SS=243$ INR thousand per week.
- If demand shifts to $Q_d=72-4P$, at the old price $P=6$ there is a shortage of $48-36=12$. New equilibrium is $P^*=7$, $Q^*=44$. The demand price intercept is $18$, so $CS=\frac12(18-7)(44)=242$, $PS=\frac12(7-1.5)(44)=121$, and $SS=363$ INR thousand per week.

## 8. Case: India's 2023 non-basmati rice export restriction

**Verified policy facts.** On 20 July 2023, India's Gazette published Notification 20/2023, changing the export policy for non-basmati white rice under HS code 1006 30 90 from “Free” to “Prohibited”, with immediate effect. The measure was for this specified category, not all rice; parboiled and basmati rice were not included in this specific change.[1][2][3] The government stated that its aims were adequate domestic availability and lower domestic prices. It said retail rice prices had risen 11.5% over the year and 3% in the preceding month.[1][3]

**Apply the model, then test it against evidence.**

1. **World market:** removing some Indian white rice exports shifts world supply left, all else equal. At the old world price this creates excess demand; price rises and quantity traded falls. Diagram: world price of rice (USD per tonne) vertically, quantity traded horizontally; show $S_1$ shifting left to $S_2$, the old-price shortage and a new intersection with higher $P^*$ and lower $Q^*$.
2. **Domestic market:** rice retained for domestic sale shifts domestic supply right relative to the no-ban counterfactual, all else equal. That creates excess supply at the old price and downward pressure on domestic price. Diagram: domestic price per kg against quantity; shift $S_1$ right to $S_2$, mark the old-price surplus and the new lower-price, higher-quantity equilibrium.
3. **Observed international evidence:** FAO reported that its All Rice Price Index averaged 142.4 points in August 2023, up 9.8% from July and a 15-year nominal high. FAO linked the increase largely to trade disruptions after India's July ban, alongside seasonally tight availability and uncertainty that led market participants to hold stocks, renegotiate contracts or stop making offers.[4] This is consistent with the world-supply analysis, but it does not show that the ban was the only cause.
4. **Domestic outcome was mixed:** USDA FAS reported that prices eased by about 10% in major Indian states after the announcement, while noting that improved planting progress could partly explain the change; it also reported that retail prices had not shown signs of weakening at that point.[3] A later panel-GARCH study of Indian daily retail prices found export restrictions were not, by themselves, sufficient to lower domestic prices.[5] So distinguish the government's stated aim from proof of its success.

The case illustrates how a policy can improve availability for domestic buyers while reducing supply to foreign buyers. Actual consumer and producer effects depend on price pass-through, other supply and demand changes, and the rice category in question; avoid claiming that every Indian consumer gained or every producer lost by the same amount.

## 9. Limits of the efficiency result

Competitive equilibrium's maximum $CS+PS$ is a statement about efficient use of resources under the model, not a judgement about fairness. Ability to pay influences who receives goods; efficiency alone does not answer “for whom to produce”. The same efficient output can coexist with very unequal or more equal income distributions. Also, if relevant external costs or benefits are absent from private $D$ and $S$, the measured private surplus is not total social welfare. Keep **efficiency** and **equity** distinct.

## 10. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Why it loses marks | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Calling a demand increase “an increase in quantity demanded” | A non-price determinant shifts the whole curve | Say demand shifts right; reserve movement along $D$ for a price change |
| Drawing a shortage or surplus as a vertical gap | The imbalance is a quantity | Bracket the horizontal $Q_d-Q_s$ or $Q_s-Q_d$ gap at a stated price |
| Jumping directly from a shift to the new equilibrium | It omits the market adjustment mechanism | Show the old-price imbalance, price pressure, movement along the unchanged curve, then the new intersection |
| Shifting both curves when only one determinant changed | It misidentifies the cause | Shift only the affected curve; explain the movement along the other |
| Saying “surplus” when you mean consumer surplus | Excess supply and benefit surplus are different concepts | Write the full term: excess supply, CS, PS or SS |
| Shading CS or PS beyond $Q^*$ | Only units actually traded count | Shade the correct area to the equilibrium quantity |
| Using the triangle formula without checking intercepts | Producer surplus may be a trapezium | Identify the supply intercept and inspect the shaded geometry first |
| Equating maximum surplus with fairness | Efficiency says nothing about distribution | Explain the “for whom” limitation separately |
| Treating the India policy aim or index movement as proof of a single cause | The case has other influences and different price measures | Attribute carefully; distinguish model prediction, reported outcome and inference |

## 11. Self-test

1. Define market equilibrium and explain what happens to price when there is excess supply or excess demand.
2. Using a demand-and-supply diagram, explain the effect of an increase in demand on equilibrium price and quantity.
3. Distinguish the signalling function from the incentive function of prices, and distinguish price rationing from non-price rationing.
4. (HL) Given $Q_d=60-4P$ and $Q_s=-12+8P$, calculate $P^*$, $Q^*$, the shortage at $P=4$, consumer surplus, producer surplus and social surplus.
5. Using a world rice-market diagram, explain the expected effect of India's July 2023 non-basmati white rice export restriction and assess what the reported evidence does and does not establish.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Market equilibrium is where $Q_d=Q_s$, so there is no tendency for price to change. If $Q_s>Q_d$, excess supply puts downward pressure on price; if $Q_d>Q_s$, excess demand puts upward pressure on price. Price changes alter quantities along the curves until the gap closes.
> 2. Demand shifts right from $D_1$ to $D_2$. At the old price there is excess demand, so price rises. Quantity demanded contracts along $D_2$ and quantity supplied extends along unchanged $S$ until a new equilibrium is reached, with higher $P^*$ and higher $Q^*$. Diagram: labelled $P$ and $Q$ axes, both demand curves, $S$, old and new equilibria, old-price shortage and price-adjustment direction.
> 3. Signalling is information conveyed by a price change; incentive is the motivation it creates to change behaviour. Price rationing allocates according to willingness and ability to pay the market price; non-price rationing uses other rules such as queues.
> 4. Set $60-4P=-12+8P$: $P^*=6$, $Q^*=36$. At $P=4$, $Q_d=44$ and $Q_s=20$, so shortage is 24 thousand kg per week. Demand and supply price intercepts are 15 and 1.5. $CS=\frac12(15-6)(36)=162$, $PS=\frac12(6-1.5)(36)=81$, and $SS=243$ INR thousand per week.
> 5. The ban reduces Indian exports of the specified rice category; ceteris paribus, world supply shifts left, creating excess demand at the old price and raising equilibrium price while reducing quantity traded. FAO reported a 142.4-point All Rice Price Index for August 2023, 9.8% above July and a 15-year nominal high, and linked the increase largely to trade disruptions after the ban.[4] FAO also described tight seasonal availability and uncertainty; the index therefore supports, but does not isolate, the ban's effect. The Indian government's stated aim was lower domestic prices, but USDA's report described mixed domestic price evidence.[1][3]
