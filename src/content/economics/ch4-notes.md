# Economics HL — 2.2 Supply

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **Supply** is the quantities firms are willing and able to produce and sell at different prices over a time period, **ceteris paribus**; **quantity supplied** is the amount at one price.
> - The **law of supply** says price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, other things equal. It gives an upward-sloping **supply curve**.
> - A good's own-price change moves along S; a change in another determinant shifts the whole curve.
> - **Market supply** is the horizontal sum of individual firms' quantities at each price.
> - **HL:** **diminishing marginal returns** eventually raise **marginal cost**, helping explain why a higher price is needed to make greater output worthwhile.

> [!note] Numbering and scope
> Tragakes labels this textbook section **2.3 Supply**; the IB guide for first assessment 2022 labels the syllabus point **2.2 Supply**. The guide lists the law and curve, individual-to-market supply, non-price determinants, movements versus shifts, and—at HL only—the assumptions of diminishing marginal returns and increasing marginal costs. It does **not** list linear supply functions under 2.2, so they are left out here.[1]

## 2. Supply and the law of supply

**Supply (of an individual firm)** is the various quantities of a good or service that the firm is willing and able to produce and supply for sale at different possible prices, during a particular time period, **ceteris paribus**. *Ceteris paribus* means that other relevant influences are held constant. A **supply schedule** lists the quantities offered at different prices; a **supply curve** graphs those price–quantity combinations.

The **law of supply** states that, over a particular time period and ceteris paribus, price and quantity supplied have a positive relationship: as price rises, quantity supplied rises; as price falls, quantity supplied falls. A price change causes a change in quantity supplied, not a change in the whole supply curve.

Tragakes gives two points for a chocolate-bar firm: at USD 3 it supplies 400 bars per week; at USD 4 it supplies 500 bars per week. These are stated schedule points, not enough to establish a supply equation. (Tragakes, printed p. 50.)

> [!example] Diagram 1: an individual supply curve
> - Vertical axis: **Price (USD per unit)**. Horizontal axis: **Quantity (units per week)**.
> - Draw an upward-sloping curve labelled **S**.
> - Mark A at (Q1, P1) and B at (Q2, P2), where P2 > P1 and Q2 > Q1. Add dotted guides to the axes and an arrow **along S** from A to B.
> - Caption: a rise in the good's own price increases quantity supplied, ceteris paribus.

> [!note] Textbook extra: vertical supply
> Tragakes also describes a vertical supply curve where quantity is fixed—for example, the seats for one theatre performance, or an original painting that cannot be reproduced as an original. This is a textbook extension, not one of the diagrams named in the current guide's 2.2 checklist.[1]

## 3. From individual supply to market supply

**Market supply** is the total quantities that all firms in a market are willing and able to supply at each possible price during a period. It is the sum of the individual firms' supplies.

**Horizontal summation** means adding quantities at the same price; do not add prices. In Tragakes' chocolate-bar example, at USD 3 firm A supplies 400 bars per week and firm B supplies 300. Adding these and the quantities from the other firms gives market supply of 8,000 bars per week at USD 3 (Fig. 2.6, printed pp. 50–51).

> [!example] Diagram 2: market supply by horizontal addition
> Draw three panels side by side: firm A's S, firm B's S, then market S. Use the same vertical price axis in all panels. At one chosen price, read Q from each firm's curve and add the quantities horizontally to locate the market-supply point. Repeat at other prices. Label the market quantity in the units and time period given by the question.

## 4. Non-price determinants shift supply

A **non-price determinant of supply** is a factor other than the good's own price that changes how much firms are willing and able to supply. A change in one of these determinants shifts the whole curve:

- **Increase in supply**: rightward shift; more is supplied at every given price.
- **Decrease in supply**: leftward shift; less is supplied at every given price.

| Determinant | Change | Why and direction of shift |
|---|---|---|
| **Costs of factors of production** (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) | A factor price, such as a wage or input price, rises | Costs rise, making production less profitable; supply shifts left. A fall in factor prices shifts supply right. |
| **Prices of related goods: competitive supply** | The price of the alternative product rises | The goods compete for the same resources. Firms switch toward the now more profitable alternative, so supply of this good shifts left. |
| **Prices of related goods: joint supply** | The price of one joint product rises | Joint products come from the same production process. More production of the higher-priced product also increases supply of its joint product(s), shifting their supply right. |
| **Indirect taxes and subsidies** | A tax rises / a subsidy rises | A tax raises production costs and shifts supply left. A subsidy lowers effective costs and shifts supply right. |
| **Future price expectations** | Firms expect their product's price to rise / fall | If a higher future price is expected, firms may hold back current supply: left now. If a lower future price is expected, firms may sell more now: right now. |
| **Technology** | Production technology improves | Lower costs or greater productivity make production more profitable; supply shifts right. Less productive technology can shift it left. |
| **Number of firms** | More firms enter / firms leave | More firms add to market supply, shifting it right; fewer firms shift it left. |

Tragakes additionally discusses sudden **shocks**, including weather and disasters, as influences on supply. Treat these as examples of a non-price change, not as an extra named item in the guide's 2.2 list.[1]

> [!warning] Joint versus competitive supply
> A higher price for a **competitive** product draws shared resources away from this product, shifting its supply left. A higher price for a **joint** product can increase supply of this product too, shifting it right.

## 5. Movement along versus shift of supply

| | Movement along S | Shift of S |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Change in the good's own price | Change in a non-price determinant |
| Name | **Change in quantity supplied** | **Change in supply** (increase or decrease) |
| Diagram | One curve; a point moves along it | The whole curve moves, e.g. S1 to S2 or S3 |
| Comparison | Different price and quantity | At the same price, quantity differs between curves |

> [!example] Diagram 3a: movement along S
> Draw one upward-sloping S with P vertical and Q horizontal. Mark A at (Q1, P1) and B at (Q2, P2), with P2 > P1 and Q2 > Q1. Draw dotted guides and an arrow **along S** from A to B. Label it “increase in quantity supplied: own price rose”.

> [!example] Diagram 3b: shift of S
> Draw the original S1, S2 to its right (increase in supply), and S3 to its left (decrease in supply). Hold price P1 fixed. Read Q1 on S1, Q2 on S2 and Q3 on S3, with Q2 > Q1 > Q3. Use horizontal arrows between curves and name the determinant, such as “improved technology”.

For marks, label both axes and units, every curve, the fixed price in a shift diagram, quantities, direction arrows, and the cause in a sentence that refers to the diagram.

## 6. HL: diminishing returns and increasing marginal cost

### 6.1 Short run and long run

- The **short run** is a period in which at least one input is fixed in quantity and quality; other inputs can vary.
- The **long run** is a period in which all inputs can be changed. Neither term refers to a fixed number of days or months; timing varies by industry.
- The HL explanation of diminishing marginal returns is a short-run explanation because it relies on at least one fixed input. (Tragakes, printed p. 54.)

### 6.2 Product and diminishing marginal returns

**Total product (TP)** is the total output produced. **Marginal product (MP)** is the additional output from one more unit of a variable input. With one-worker increments, MP is the change in TP between adjacent worker counts.

The **law of diminishing marginal returns** states that as more units of a variable input (such as labour) are added to one or more fixed inputs (such as land), MP may first rise but eventually begins to fall, assuming the fixed input(s) and technology remain unchanged.

| Workers | Total product (kg of potatoes) | MP recomputed from TP (kg) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 20 | 20 |
| 2 | 50 | 30 |
| 3 | 90 | 40 |
| 4 | 120 | 30 |
| 5 | 140 | 20 |
| 6 | 150 | 10 |

In the displayed total-product figures, MP is greatest with worker 3 and falls from worker 4 onward. The table illustrates diminishing marginal returns even though the last row contains a textbook inconsistency.

> [!warning] Textbook arithmetic error: Table 2.4
> The printed table and prose say that the sixth worker adds 0 kg, but the displayed total product rises from 140 kg with five workers to 150 kg with six. The difference is 10 kg, so MP6 recomputed from the printed TP figures is **10 kg**, not 0. The source scan confirms that “150” and “0” are printed. Either the TP figure or the MP entry/prose is wrong; the extract does not establish which value was intended. Use the recomputed 10 kg when calculating from the displayed TP data, and do not repeat the contradiction as if both figures agree. (Tragakes, printed p. 55.)

### 6.3 Marginal cost and the supply curve

**Total cost (TC)** is all production cost incurred by a firm. **Marginal cost (MC)** is the additional cost of producing one more unit of output: MC is the change in TC divided by the change in output.

| Output (units) | Total cost (USD) | MC (USD per extra unit) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 12 | 12 |
| 2 | 20 | 8 |
| 3 | 26 | 6 |
| 4 | 34 | 8 |
| 5 | 46 | 12 |
| 6 | 62 | 16 |

The MC figures are the successive increases in TC: 12, 8, 6, 8, 12 and 16. This table shows MC falling at first and then rising. In the short-run explanation, when MP rises, MC falls; when MP is at its maximum, MC is at its minimum; when MP falls, MC rises. This inverse relationship assumes other relevant costs, including the price of the variable input, are unchanged. (Tragakes, printed p. 56.)

**HL explanation chain:** at least one input is fixed in the short run → adding more of a variable input eventually causes diminishing marginal returns, so MP falls → with input prices otherwise unchanged, producing extra output becomes more costly, so MC rises → a higher product price is needed to make the additional output worthwhile → the supply curve slopes upward.

> [!example] Diagram 4: the HL relationship
> Use separate panels so the axes remain clear. Panel A: MP vertically against units of the variable input horizontally; show MP rising at first, peaking, then falling. Panel B: MC vertically against output horizontally; show MC falling at first, reaching a minimum, then rising. Label the assumptions “fixed input” and “technology unchanged”, and connect falling MP to rising MC. Do not imply that the tables' worker counts and output units share the same numerical scale.

## 7. Case application: cocoa production estimates, 2023/24

The ICCO's February 2025 production table labels the 2023/24 figures as estimates. It reports world production falling from 5.043 million tonnes in 2022/23 to 4.489 million tonnes in 2023/24. The difference is 554,000 tonnes, or an 11.0% decline. Côte d’Ivoire fell from 2.241 million to 1.674 million tonnes (567,000 tonnes; 25.3%); Ghana fell from 654,000 to 530,000 tonnes (124,000 tonnes; 19.0%). The differences and percentages are recomputed from the ICCO table.[2]

The ICCO Annual Report attributes the production decline primarily to adverse weather and pests/diseases affecting Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana; it names unfavourable weather, black pod disease and Cocoa Swollen Shoot Virus Disease.[3]

**Supply-model application:** treat adverse weather and disease as a non-price supply influence. Draw the market supply of cocoa beans shifting left from S1 to S2. Use price (USD per tonne) on the vertical axis and quantity (million tonnes per year) on the horizontal axis. At any one price, show a lower quantity on S2. The ICCO output figures are seasonal estimates, not observations of quantity supplied at an identical fixed price, so use the diagram as a model application—not as a direct measurement of the curve shift or a way to infer the new price. Price determination belongs in [[Economics HL — Competitive Market Equilibrium — Notes]].

## 8. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Why it loses marks | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| “Price rose, so supply rose” | Own-price changes affect quantity supplied, not the whole curve. | Show a movement along S and call it a change in quantity supplied. |
| Shift S when the good's own price changes | A shift needs a non-price cause. | Own price: move along. Other determinant: shift. |
| Draw a cost increase as a rightward shift | Higher costs reduce supply at each price. | Costs/tax up: shift S left. |
| Reverse joint and competitive supply | Their resource/production relationships differ. | Competitive alternative price up: this S left; joint product price up: this S right. |
| Add prices to obtain market supply | Market supply is horizontal summation. | Add firms' quantities at the same price. |
| Say diminishing returns mean total output falls | MP can fall while TP continues to rise. | Explain that each additional worker adds less output; distinguish MP from TP. |
| Copy the sixth-worker MP as 0 without checking TP | It conflicts with the printed change from 140 to 150. | State the source inconsistency and calculate 10 kg from the displayed TP values. |
| Import linear supply equations into 2.2 | They are not listed in the first-assessment-2022 guide's 2.2 scope. | Focus on the listed concepts and diagrams.[1] |
| Claim a supply shift alone proves a price change | A market price depends on both demand and supply. | Keep 2.2 to the supply shift; analyse the new equilibrium in 2.3. |

## 9. Self-test

1. Define supply and state the law of supply.
2. How is market supply built from individual firms? At USD 3, what does Tragakes' example state for firm A, firm B and the market?
3. A product's own price rises; then its input price rises. Distinguish the effect of each change on the supply curve.
4. Using the total-product sequence in Table 2.4, calculate the MP of worker 6 and explain the conflict in the printed table.
5. Use the ICCO world cocoa output estimates for 2022/23 and 2023/24 to illustrate a supply shift, and state one limitation of the diagram interpretation.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Supply is the quantities producers are willing and able to offer at different prices over a time period, ceteris paribus. The law of supply states that price and quantity supplied are positively related, ceteris paribus.
> 2. Add quantities horizontally at each price. At USD 3, firm A supplies 400 bars per week and firm B supplies 300; after adding the other firms, the textbook gives market supply as 8,000 bars per week.
> 3. The own-price rise causes a movement up along the same S curve and an increase in quantity supplied. The input-price rise increases costs and shifts S left, decreasing supply at each price.
> 4. MP6 = 150 − 140 = 10 kg. The printed MP cell and prose say 0 kg, which conflicts with the displayed TP figures; the source does not establish which printed value was intended.
> 5. ICCO estimates fell from 5.043 million tonnes to 4.489 million tonnes, a 554,000-tonne or 11.0% decline. Weather/disease is consistent with a leftward supply shift. These are seasonal estimates, not a same-price supply schedule, so they do not measure the exact curve shift or establish the resulting price.[2][3]
