# Economics HL — Elasticity of Demand and Supply — Notes

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **Elasticity** measures percentage responsiveness: compare a percentage change with a percentage change, not absolute units.
> - **PED** is usually negative because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions; when classifying its size, IB questions often use its absolute value, written $\lvert PED\rvert$.
> - **YED** keeps its sign: positive means normal, negative means inferior; for normal goods, $0<YED<1$ is income-inelastic and $YED>1$ is income-elastic.
> - **PES** measures how responsive quantity supplied is to price; time, spare capacity, stocks and the ease of switching resources matter.
> - Elasticity is not the same as slope. On a straight-line demand curve, slope is constant but PED varies along the curve.

## 1. Scope and command terms

This note covers **IB Economics 2.5, elasticity of demand** (price elasticity of demand, PED, and income elasticity of demand, YED) and **2.6, elasticity of supply** (price elasticity of supply, PES). Typical command terms include **define**, **calculate**, **state**, **comment**, **explain**, **draw** and **analyse**. For a calculation, show the formula, substitution, answer and interpretation. For an explanation, link the coefficient to the size and direction of the response.

| Syllabus concept | Strong answer includes |
|---|---|
| PED / YED / PES | Definition, correct formula, units-free coefficient and interpretation |
| Calculation | Correct percentage-change method; show sign and/or absolute value as required |
| Determinants | Name the factor, explain its mechanism, predict elastic or inelastic response |
| Diagram | Axes, labelled curves, relevant points and the direction of the response |
| Application | Use the stated context and distinguish observed evidence from a model prediction |

## 2. Percentage-change convention

For a change from an initial value to a new value, the ordinary percentage change is:

$$\%\Delta X=\frac{X_{new}-X_{initial}}{X_{initial}}\times100$$

Some elasticity questions use the **midpoint (arc) method** to reduce the dependence of the answer on which endpoint is chosen:

$$\%\Delta X=\frac{X_2-X_1}{(X_1+X_2)/2}\times100$$

Use the method specified by the question or taught for that dataset. Do not mix the midpoint numerator with an initial-value denominator. A percentage change is dimensionless; the coefficient has no unit.

## 3. Price elasticity of demand (PED)

**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures the responsiveness of the **quantity demanded** of a good to a change in its own price, other things equal.

$$PED=\frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P}$$

Because price and quantity demanded usually move in opposite directions, PED is usually negative. For the size categories below, compare the **absolute value** $\lvert PED\rvert$ unless a question explicitly asks for the signed coefficient.

| Classification | Condition | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Price inelastic | $0\leq\lvert PED\rvert<1$ | Quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price |
| Unitary elastic | $\lvert PED\rvert=1$ | Quantity demanded changes by the same percentage as price |
| Price elastic | $\lvert PED\rvert>1$ | Quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than price |
| Perfectly inelastic | $PED=0$ | Quantity demanded does not respond to price; vertical demand curve |
| Perfectly elastic | $\lvert PED\rvert\to\infty$ | An arbitrarily small price rise reduces quantity demanded to zero; horizontal demand curve in the model |

### 3.1 What determines PED?

| Determinant | Mechanism | Tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Availability and closeness of substitutes | Buyers can switch readily when the price rises; closer substitutes make switching easier. | More/closer substitutes → more elastic demand. |
| Necessity versus luxury | Buyers have less scope to stop buying an essential item; luxury purchases can more readily be postponed or forgone. This depends on the consumer and context. | Necessities tend to be less elastic; luxuries tend to be more elastic. These are tendencies, not rules. |
| Proportion of income spent | A price change on an item taking a large share of a budget has a greater effect on real purchasing power. | Larger budget share → more elastic demand. |
| Time to adjust | Over time, buyers can change habits, find alternatives, or replace durable goods. | PED often becomes more elastic over a longer period. |
| Definition of the market | A narrowly defined product usually has more close substitutes than a broad category. | Narrow market definition tends to produce more elastic demand. |

### 3.2 PED and total revenue

**Total revenue (TR)** is the money received from sales: $TR=P\times Q$.

| Demand at the current point | If price rises | If price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic ($\lvert PED\rvert>1$) | TR falls: the percentage fall in quantity outweighs the percentage rise in price. | TR rises. |
| Inelastic ($\lvert PED\rvert<1$) | TR rises: the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price. | TR falls. |
| Unitary elastic ($\lvert PED\rvert=1$) | TR is unchanged for a small movement / at the unit-elastic point. | TR is unchanged for a small movement / at the unit-elastic point. |

On a straight-line demand curve, PED is elastic in the upper section, unit elastic at the midpoint, and inelastic in the lower section. The midpoint maximizes total revenue **for a linear demand curve**. Do not infer elasticity from steepness alone without checking axes and scales: slope and elasticity are different concepts.

> [!example] Workbook calculation: platinum earrings
> The workbook gives a price fall from $400 to $350 and a quantity rise from 25 to 30 per month (Unit 2, PDF pp. 23–24). Using the midpoint method:
> $$PED=\frac{(30-25)/27.5}{(350-400)/375}= -1.36\text{ (approximately)}$$
> Thus $\lvert PED\rvert\approx1.36$: demand is price elastic over this interval. The calculation uses the supplied exercise data, not an observed estimate for all jewellery buyers.

> [!example] India application: petrol demand
> A 2020 study by Charles Shaw estimates Indian petrol-demand price elasticities of **−0.189 in the short run** and **−0.418 in the long run**, using data for 1966–2019. Both estimates are price inelastic in absolute value; the larger long-run response is consistent with consumers having more time to adjust. These are estimates from one study and period, not a universal constant for every household or every fuel market.[1]

## 4. Income elasticity of demand (YED)

**Income elasticity of demand (YED)** measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in consumer income, other things equal.

$$YED=\frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta Y}$$

Unlike PED, the sign is essential because it identifies the relationship between income and demand.

| Result | Good type | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| $YED<0$ | Inferior good | Income rises and demand falls; income falls and demand rises, other things equal. |
| $0<YED<1$ | Normal, income-inelastic (often called a necessity) | Demand rises proportionately less than income. |
| $YED=1$ | Normal, unit income-elastic | Demand changes by the same percentage as income. |
| $YED>1$ | Normal, income-elastic (often called a luxury) | Demand rises proportionately more than income. |

“Necessity” and “luxury” here describe how demand responds to income in the model; they are not moral judgements or fixed labels. A good’s YED may differ between countries, income groups and time periods.

> [!example] Workbook calculation: tea
> A 3% rise in real household income increases tea sales from 100 million to 101 million units (Workbook, Unit 2, PDF p. 28). Quantity rises by 1%, so $YED=1\%/3\%\approx+0.33$. This is positive but below one: tea is normal and income-inelastic in this example.

### 4.1 Why firms and governments use YED

- A firm can forecast which product lines are likely to grow in a boom or contract in a downturn, then adjust output, stock, investment and marketing.
- A firm can compare product portfolios: an income-elastic product may have strong growth potential in a boom but greater exposure to recession.
- Governments can use YED estimates to anticipate how demand for taxed goods may change as incomes change and to inform revenue forecasts. YED alone does not determine tax revenue; tax rates, prices, quantities and policy changes also matter.

## 5. Price elasticity of supply (PES)

**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures the responsiveness of the **quantity supplied** of a good to a change in its own price, other things equal.

$$PES=\frac{\%\Delta Q_s}{\%\Delta P}$$

PES is normally positive because a higher price creates an incentive for firms to supply more. Use the absolute-value categories as a comparison of size; unlike PED, there is usually no negative sign to remove.

| Classification | Condition | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Price inelastic supply | $0\leq PES<1$ | Quantity supplied changes by a smaller percentage than price |
| Unitary elastic supply | $PES=1$ | Quantity supplied changes by the same percentage as price |
| Price elastic supply | $PES>1$ | Quantity supplied changes by a larger percentage than price |
| Perfectly inelastic supply | $PES=0$ | Quantity supplied cannot respond over the period; vertical supply curve |
| Perfectly elastic supply | $PES\to\infty$ | Suppliers will supply any amount at one price in the model; horizontal supply curve |

### 5.1 What determines PES?

| Determinant | Mechanism | Tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Time period | Firms need time to recruit, train, build capacity or alter production. | Supply is usually more elastic in the long run. |
| Spare capacity | Idle machines, workers or premises can be brought into use quickly. | More spare capacity → more elastic supply. |
| Stocks and inventories | Stored goods can be released when price rises. | Larger usable stocks → more elastic short-run supply. |
| Factor mobility and production flexibility | Easily available inputs and adaptable processes allow output to change. | Greater mobility/flexibility → more elastic supply. |
| Production time and perishability | Crops, housing or specialist goods may take a long time to produce or cannot be stored. | Longer production lag / limited storage → more inelastic supply in the short run. |

> [!example] Workbook calculation: Toyota output diagram
> The workbook diagram shows quantity supplied rising from 20,000 to 50,000 vehicles while price rises from $25,000 to $30,000 (Unit 2, PDF p. 31). Using the midpoint method, $PES\approx4.71$, so supply is elastic over the interval in the diagram. It is a diagram-based exercise, not a measured estimate for Toyota’s actual production.

## 6. Calculation routine and exam method

1. Identify what changes: $Q_d$, $Q_s$, price or income.
2. Write the correct ratio: PED, YED or PES. Do not use quantity supplied in PED or quantity demanded in PES.
3. Calculate the percentage changes using the question’s required base or midpoint method.
4. Divide the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in the relevant variable.
5. Keep the PED sign unless instructed to report its absolute value; keep the YED sign.
6. Interpret the answer in words: e.g. “a 1% price rise is associated with an approximately 1.36% fall in quantity demanded over this interval.”
7. For a comment, classify the coefficient and connect it to the context. Do not simply repeat the number.

For a linear demand diagram, put **price** on the vertical axis and **quantity demanded per stated period** on the horizontal axis. Label the demand curve $D$; show a price/quantity change along the same curve. For a linear supply diagram, put **price** vertically and **quantity supplied per stated period** horizontally; label the curve $S$. When an exercise supplies two endpoints, mark both and make clear that the coefficient is measured over that interval.

## 7. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Using absolute changes, such as “quantity fell by 5,” instead of percentage changes | Elasticity compares percentage changes. |
| Reporting PED as positive without saying the absolute value is being used | Calculate the signed value first; then classify using $\lvert PED\rvert$ where appropriate. |
| Treating every good as elastic if it is a luxury or inelastic if it is a necessity | These are tendencies; explain substitutes, budget share, time and context. |
| Saying “demand increased” because the good’s own price fell | Say “quantity demanded increased”: this is movement along D. |
| Assuming a steep curve means inelastic demand in all diagrams | Slope depends on axis units and scale; elasticity uses percentage changes and varies along a straight-line demand curve. |
| Classifying a normal good as a luxury whenever YED is positive | A normal good with $0<YED<1$ is income-inelastic; it is income-elastic when $YED>1$. |
| Dropping the negative sign from YED | Negative YED identifies an inferior good; do not convert it to an absolute value. |
| Calling YED=1 “the same percentage change in price” | It is the same percentage change in quantity demanded and income. |
| Claiming any real-world elasticity estimate applies everywhere | State the study, place, period and estimate; note context and limits. |
| Applying a PED estimate as an exact prediction after a large change | Elasticity may vary along a curve and estimates are context/interval-specific; describe large-change predictions as approximate unless a model specifies otherwise. |

## 8. Self-test

1. Define PED and explain why its usual sign is negative.
2. A product’s price rises by 10% and its quantity demanded falls by 15%. Calculate PED and classify demand.
3. A 4% rise in real income increases demand for a product by 2%. Calculate YED and classify the good.
4. Explain why supply for a crop can be more inelastic in the short run than in the long run.
5. Explain how a firm could use YED during an expected recession, and give one limitation of its estimate.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. PED measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good’s own price, ceteris paribus: $PED=\%\Delta Q_d/\%\Delta P$. Its usual sign is negative because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions.
> 2. $PED=-15\%/10\%=-1.5$. $\lvert PED\rvert=1.5>1$, so demand is price elastic.
> 3. $YED=2\%/4\%=+0.5$. It is positive and below one, so the good is normal and income-inelastic in this example.
> 4. In the short run, crops already planted cannot be increased immediately; land, growing time and seasonal conditions constrain output. Over a longer period, producers may change planting, inputs or capacity, making supply more responsive.
> 5. The firm can use YED to forecast which products may lose demand as incomes fall and adjust stock or production. An estimate may not apply to every customer or recession because the product, income group, time period and substitute options can differ.
