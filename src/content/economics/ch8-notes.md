# 2.7 — Role of Government in Microeconomics

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - Governments intervene to address market failure, improve equity, protect consumers, and pursue social or macroeconomic objectives; intervention can also create unintended costs.
> - An **indirect tax** creates a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive, reduces equilibrium quantity, and generates government revenue.
> - The relative **PED** and **PES** determine how the economic burden is shared; the party legally remitting a tax does not necessarily bear most of its cost.
> - A specific tax shifts supply vertically by a fixed amount; an ad valorem tax creates a percentage wedge that grows with the market price.
> - Taxes, subsidies, and price controls each have trade-offs. Explain the diagram and evaluate the outcome for different stakeholders.

## 1. Why governments intervene

**Government intervention** is a policy action that changes market outcomes or the allocation of resources. In microeconomics, governments may intervene to:

- **Correct market failure:** change incentives where private decisions ignore external costs or benefits, or where markets fail to provide socially desirable outcomes.
- **Improve equity:** redistribute income or make essential goods and services more accessible. Equity is about fairness, not necessarily identical outcomes.
- **Protect consumers and workers:** set safety, information, or employment rules where private incentives alone may not protect them.
- **Influence consumption or production:** discourage harmful activities or encourage activities with wider social benefits.
- **Raise revenue:** collect funds for public services and other government spending.

Intervention is not automatically successful. It can involve administrative and enforcement costs, imperfect information, unintended responses, or government failure. A good evaluation compares the likely market failure with the costs, distributional effects, and practical limits of the policy.

**Command terms:** *Explain* the market problem and the policy transmission mechanism. *Evaluate* by weighing efficiency, equity, stakeholder effects, information, time, and implementation.

## 2. Indirect taxes

An **indirect tax** is a tax on expenditure on a good or service. A **specific tax** is a fixed amount charged per unit. An **ad valorem tax** is a percentage of the selling price or value of the transaction. These are different from direct taxes on income or wealth.

### How the market changes

A tax raises the cost of supplying each unit. In a standard competitive-market diagram:

1. Put **price/cost** on the vertical axis and **quantity** on the horizontal axis.
2. Draw the original demand curve **D** and supply curve **S**, meeting at **E₁ (P₁, Q₁)**.
3. Add the post-tax supply curve **S + tax** above/left of S. For a specific tax of *t* per unit, the vertical gap is *t* at every quantity. For an ad valorem tax, the tax is a proportion of price, so the absolute gap is larger at higher prices; show a widening wedge rather than a constant vertical gap.
4. Mark the new quantity **Q₂ < Q₁**, the buyer price **Pᶜ**, and the seller price **Pᵖ**. The vertical distance **Pᶜ − Pᵖ** is the tax per unit at Q₂.
5. Label government tax revenue as the rectangle with height **Pᶜ − Pᵖ** and width **Q₂**.

The tax reduces the quantity traded. Consumers generally pay more per unit than before, while producers receive less per unit net of tax. The government receives revenue, but consumer and producer surplus fall. The lost trades between Q₂ and Q₁ create a deadweight loss in the standard model.

**Tax revenue:** for a specific tax, `tax per unit × quantity sold after tax`. Do not multiply the tax by the original quantity. With an ad valorem tax, calculate the tax per unit at the post-tax selling price, then multiply by the post-tax quantity.

**Deadweight loss (DWL)** is the combined consumer and producer surplus lost because mutually beneficial trades no longer occur after the tax. In a linear supply-and-demand diagram, it is the triangle between the pre-tax and post-tax quantities. Government revenue is a transfer to the government, not itself DWL.

### Who bears the tax?

**Tax incidence** is the division of the economic burden of a tax between consumers and producers. It depends on relative PED and PES, not simply on which party sends the payment to the government.

- If demand is relatively more price-inelastic than supply, consumers have fewer effective alternatives; the buyer price tends to rise by a larger share of the tax.
- If supply is relatively more price-inelastic than demand, producers have fewer alternatives for reallocating resources; the net price they receive tends to fall by a larger share.
- With perfectly inelastic demand, buyers bear the full per-unit burden in the standard model. With perfectly inelastic supply, sellers bear the full burden.

Use the diagram to compare the original price P₁ with Pᶜ and Pᵖ:

- Consumer burden per unit: **Pᶜ − P₁**.
- Producer burden per unit: **P₁ − Pᵖ**.
- These add to the per-unit tax: **(Pᶜ − P₁) + (P₁ − Pᵖ) = Pᶜ − Pᵖ**.

### Specific versus ad valorem

| Feature | Specific tax | Ad valorem tax |
|---|---|---|
| Tax basis | Fixed amount per unit | Fixed percentage of value/price |
| Supply-diagram effect | Parallel upward shift by the tax amount | Tax wedge widens as price rises; often shown as a rotation |
| Revenue calculation | Tax per unit × post-tax quantity | Percentage × relevant taxable value, or per-unit tax at the new price × quantity |
| Example form | ₹x per litre or ₹x per packet | y% of the sale price |

## 3. Worked example — specific tax

The Economics Workbook gives a market schedule with original equilibrium price **$4** and quantity **6,000 units**. The schedule is linear: demand is `Qd = 10,000 − 1,000Pc`; supply is `Qs = 2,000 + 1,000Pp`. A **$2-per-unit specific tax** means buyers pay `Pc = Pp + 2`.

At the new equilibrium:

- Set demand equal to supply: `10,000 − 1,000(Pp + 2) = 2,000 + 1,000Pp`.
- Solve for the price sellers receive: **Pp = $3**; buyers pay **Pc = $5**.
- Quantity traded is **5,000 units**.
- Government revenue is **$2 × 5,000 = $10,000**.
- Consumers bear **$1 per unit** (price rises from $4 to $5); producers bear **$1 per unit** (net price falls from $4 to $3). Each bears **$5,000** in total.
- Under the linear-curve model, **DWL = ½ × $2 × (6,000 − 5,000) = $1,000**.

The equal split occurs because of this particular schedule; it is not a universal rule. In an exam, draw and label the diagram as well as showing the calculation.

## 4. India application — excise duty on fuel

On 7 April 2025, India announced a **₹2-per-litre increase** in central excise duty on petrol and diesel, effective 8 April. The Oil Ministry said public-sector oil marketing companies would not increase retail prices following the duty change.[1] This is a useful specific-tax example and a reminder to distinguish the legal tax from the observed consumer price. Do not infer the full economic incidence from the announcement alone: the article also reports that the duty change coincided with falling international crude prices, which could offset the tax in retail prices.[1]

**Exam use:** explain the tax wedge and stakeholder effects, then evaluate the policy’s stated objective (revenue) against its effect on prices, output, firms’ margins, and the distribution of the burden. Avoid claiming that all fuel consumers are completely unaffected over time; the evidence describes the announced immediate price response.

## 5. Subsidies and price controls — brief comparison

A **subsidy** is a government payment or equivalent support that lowers the effective cost of production or consumption. In a competitive-market diagram, a producer subsidy shifts supply down/right; equilibrium price paid by consumers tends to fall, quantity tends to rise, and government expenditure is approximately **subsidy per unit × post-subsidy quantity**. It can encourage merit-good consumption or support producers, but costs taxpayers and may cause overproduction, opportunity cost, or benefits captured disproportionately by firms or higher-income consumers.

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum price. If set below equilibrium, it is binding and creates excess demand (a shortage). A **price floor** is a legal minimum price. If set above equilibrium, it is binding and creates excess supply (a surplus). For each diagram, mark the original equilibrium, controlled price, quantity demanded, quantity supplied, and the shortage/surplus. Evaluation should consider affordability or producer income alongside rationing, queues, quality changes, black markets, and any government cost of purchasing or supplying the surplus.

> These are the three common microeconomic policy forms covered in the class materials. The current SA2 tracker specifically targets indirect taxation within 2.7; this note gives the other two only as a short comparison, not as a claim that their full applications have been practised.

## 6. Stakeholder and evaluation checklist

| Stakeholder | Possible benefit | Possible cost / evaluation question |
|---|---|---|
| Government | Tax revenue; policy may support a stated objective | Is revenue worth the administration cost and opportunity cost? Is the policy enforceable? |
| Consumers | Potential long-run benefit if the tax corrects external costs; public spending may benefit households | Higher buyer price, lower consumption, reduced choice; is the tax regressive relative to income? |
| Producers | A subsidy or protected price may support output/income | Indirect tax can reduce sales or net price; can firms absorb the tax, or will they cut output/jobs? |
| Workers | Public spending or supported industries may sustain employment | Lower taxed-market output may reduce labour demand; can workers move to other jobs? |
| Wider society | Potential improvement in equity or correction of external costs | DWL, administrative cost, unintended effects, and possible government failure |

Evaluate a tax by asking: **What problem is it addressing? How large is the change in quantity? Who bears the burden? What happens to revenue and surplus? Is the policy fair and workable? Are there less costly alternatives?**

## 7. Common mistakes / examiner traps

- Saying consumers or producers pay the full tax just because one side remits it. Separate legal payment from economic incidence.
- Claiming that inelastic demand automatically means consumers bear all the tax. The burden depends on both PED and PES; only the limiting elasticity cases give a full burden to one side.
- Shifting demand instead of supply for the standard indirect-tax diagram, or drawing a specific tax as a shift by the wrong amount.
- Drawing an ad valorem tax with a constant per-unit wedge. Its absolute value varies with price.
- Calculating revenue from the pre-tax quantity rather than the quantity actually sold after tax.
- Calling the entire consumer/producer-surplus reduction deadweight loss. Part becomes government revenue; DWL is the lost-surplus triangle.
- Treating a policy objective (for example, lower consumption) as proof that the policy achieved it. Explain the mechanism and consider elasticities, time, and unintended effects.
- Treating a price ceiling above equilibrium or a price floor below equilibrium as binding; those controls do not change the market outcome in the basic model.
