## Section 1.1 — Key Concepts: What is Economics?

### 1.1.1 Definition

**Economics** is a **social science** that studies the relationship between humans and the economy — using past experiences, data, and observation to understand how people, firms, and governments make decisions with **limited resources** to satisfy **unlimited wants and needs**.

Two definitions worth remembering:

- **Lionel Robbins (1932)** — *"Economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses."* Every word matters:
  - *Ends* = wants/needs (unlimited)
  - *Scarce means* = resources (limited)
  - *Alternative uses* = choices must be made
- **Modern IB definition** — Economics studies **how societies allocate scarce resources** to meet the material needs and wants of their populations.

**Why is Economics a *social* science, not a natural science?**
- It studies human behaviour, which is unpredictable (unlike gravity or chemical reactions).
- Economists use **models** and **assumptions** to simplify reality — they can never fully control variables the way a chemist can.
- Example: A physicist can predict exactly where a ball will land. An economist cannot predict *exactly* how consumers will react to a 5% rise in petrol prices — only the general direction.

---

### 1.1.2 The Nine Central Concepts — WISE CHOICES

The IB Economics syllabus (post-2022) is built around **9 interlinked central concepts**, remembered by the mnemonic **WISE CHOICES**: **W**ell-being, **I**nterdependence, **S**carcity, **E**fficiency, **CH**oice, Intervention, **C**hange, **E**quity, **S**ustainability.

#### 1. Scarcity — The Root Problem

**Definition:** Limited availability of resources relative to unlimited human wants. Scarcity is **relative**, not absolute — a resource is scarce if quantity demanded exceeds quantity freely available.

**Examples:**
- Fresh drinking water in Mumbai during May–June: scarce
- Sand in the Sahara: not scarce
- Rotten tomatoes: not scarce (no demand)
- Time: universally scarce
- Rare-earth metals (lithium, cobalt): increasingly scarce as EV demand explodes

Scarcity forces every society to answer three questions: **What** to produce? **How** to produce it? **For whom** to produce it?

#### 2. Choice — The Consequence of Scarcity

Because we can't have everything, we must choose. Every choice involves giving up the next-best alternative — this is **opportunity cost**.

**Example:** Every ₹1,000 crore India spends on Rafale jets is ₹1,000 crore NOT spent on the PM Poshan mid-day meal scheme.

#### 3. Efficiency — Getting the Most from What We Have

**Definition:** Using resources such that output is maximised for a given input.

- **Productive efficiency:** Producing at the lowest possible cost (on the PPC).
- **Allocative efficiency:** Producing the *right combination* of goods.

**Example:** A factory that makes 1,000 shirts with 100 workers is more productively efficient than one making 800 shirts with the same workers. But if society wanted 500 shirts and 500 trousers, producing 1,000 shirts is allocatively inefficient.

#### 4. Equity — Fairness in Distribution

**Equity ≠ Equality.** Equity is *fairness*; equality is *sameness*. A progressive income tax is **equitable** (not equal, but fair). Equity is a **normative** concept.

#### 5. Well-being — The Ultimate Goal

A multidimensional concept: financial security, ability to meet basic needs, freedom of economic choice, and non-material factors (mental health, environment).

**Beyond GDP:** HDI (Human Development Index), OECD Better Life Index, Bhutan's Gross National Happiness.

**Example:** Norway and Qatar both have high GDP per capita, but Norway has higher HDI due to better healthcare, education, and civil liberties.

#### 6. Sustainability — Not Compromising the Future

**Brundtland (1987):** Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Three dimensions: **Environmental**, **Economic**, **Social**.

**Examples:** Overfishing in the North Sea (unsustainable); Norway's sovereign wealth fund (sustainable); India's push for solar over coal.

#### 7. Change — Economies are Dynamic

- **Technological:** AI, EV batteries, UPI
- **Structural:** India shifting from agriculture (55% of workforce in 1991) to services (over 50% of GDP today)
- **Institutional:** GST replacing 17 indirect taxes in 2017
- **Social:** Rising female labour force participation
- **Economic:** Business cycles, inflation, growth

#### 8. Interdependence — No Actor is an Island

**Micro:** A Mumbai coffee shop needs beans (Karnataka), milk (Maharashtra), electricity, staff, and customers — all interlinked.

**Macro:** US Fed hikes rates → dollar strengthens → INR weakens → India's oil bill rises → domestic inflation.

**Trade** is the classic expression of interdependence.

#### 9. Intervention — When Markets Don't Deliver

Governments intervene through taxes, subsidies, regulation, direct provision, and trade barriers when markets fail on equity, public goods, environment, or access to essentials.

**Example:** India's electricity subsidy for farmers — correcting the market outcome of unaffordable power. (Whether it's *good* intervention is a separate debate — it has caused groundwater depletion.)

### 1.1.3 Basic Economic Terms — The Vocabulary

| Term | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Consumer** | Buys goods/services for satisfaction of wants | You buying a shirt |
| **Producer** | Makes goods for sale; aim = profit maximisation | Nike, TCS, kirana |
| **Economic good** | Has a price, has opportunity cost | Cars, haircuts |
| **Free good** | Unlimited supply, no price | Sunlight, air |
| **Price** | Amount consumer pays per unit | ₹5,000 for a shirt |
| **Cost** | Total paid to factors of production | Wages + rent + materials + interest |
| **Profit** | Price − Cost | ₹5,000 sale − ₹3,500 cost = ₹1,500 |
| **Revenue** | Price × Quantity | ₹5,000 × 100 = ₹5,00,000 |
| **Purchasing power** | How much money can buy | ₹100 in 2000 vs 2026 |
| **Investment** | Money committed for future returns | Buying a machine |
| **Economy** | System of production, distribution, consumption | Indian economy |

---

### 1.1.4 Positive vs Normative Economics

- **Positive statements** — Factual, testable.
  - *"Cutting India's GST from 18% to 12% will increase consumer demand."*
  - *"Unemployment in India was 8.3% in December 2023."*
- **Normative statements** — Value-based, opinion.
  - *"The government should raise the minimum wage."*
  - *"India should prioritise growth over inequality reduction."*

Watch for *"should, ought to, fair, better"* → likely normative.

---

### 1.1.5 Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Individual units | Whole economy |
| **Focus** | Households, firms, markets, prices | GDP, inflation, unemployment |
| **Questions** | Why did tomato prices rise 200%? | Why is India growing at 6.5%? |
| **Tools** | Demand & supply, elasticity | AD/AS, fiscal, monetary policy |

**Analogy:** Micro is individual trees; Macro is the forest. Both matter, and they interact.

---

### 1.1.6 Economics as a Model-Based Science

Economists build **models** — simplified representations of reality.

- **Diagrammatic** — demand/supply, PPC, circular flow
- **Mathematical** — Y = C + I + G + (X − M)

**Ceteris paribus** ("all other things being equal") — we change one variable at a time to isolate its effect. *"If coffee price rises, ceteris paribus, quantity demanded falls."*

## Section 1.2 — The Central Economic Problem: Scarcity

### 1.2.1 The Problem Stated

**The fundamental economic problem:** How to allocate limited resources among unlimited wants.

- **Wants** = unlimited desires
- **Needs** = essentials for survival (food, water, shelter, clothing)
- **Resources** = LIMITED factors of production

The mismatch → **scarcity** → **choices** → **opportunity cost**.

---

### 1.2.2 The Four Factors of Production

| Factor | What it is | Payment | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|---------|
| **Land** | Natural resources — soil, minerals, oil | **Rent** / royalty | Farmland in Punjab, offshore oil |
| **Labour** | Physical and mental human effort | **Wages / Salaries** | Software engineer, doctor |
| **Capital** | Man-made aids to production | **Interest** | Machinery, buildings, servers |
| **Entrepreneurship** | Combines factors, takes risks, innovates | **Profit** | Ratan Tata, Nithin Kamath |

**Important distinctions:**
- **Capital** in economics ≠ money. Economists mean *physical capital*.
- **Entrepreneurship** is what turns land + labour + capital into a business.

**Example — Making a cup of coffee at Blue Tokai:**
- Land: coffee beans grown in Coorg
- Labour: barista, farmer, roaster
- Capital: espresso machine, café furniture, delivery van
- Entrepreneurship: Matt Chitharanjan organising it into a profitable business

---

### 1.2.3 The Three Basic Economic Questions

**1. What to produce (and how much)?** Cars or bicycles? Wheat or rice? In market economies, decided by consumer demand; in planned economies, by government.

**2. How to produce?**
- **Labour-intensive** — handloom weaving in Varanasi
- **Capital-intensive** — Maruti Suzuki assembly line
- India (labour-abundant) tends labour-intensive; Germany (capital-abundant) tends capital-intensive.

**3. For whom to produce?**
- **Pure market:** Highest bidder wins
- **Planned:** Government decides based on need
- **Mixed:** Market allocates most, government redistributes via taxes/welfare

---

### 1.2.4 Types of Economic Systems

| System | How allocated | Example |
|--------|---------------|---------|
| **Planned (Command)** | Government decides everything | Former USSR, North Korea, Cuba |
| **Free Market** | Invisible hand of prices | No pure example — Hong Kong closest |
| **Mixed** | Market + government intervention | **India, USA, UK, Germany** |

**The historical pendulum:** Post-1991 (Soviet collapse + India liberalisation), the world moved toward markets. Post-2008 crisis and COVID-19, the pendulum swung back toward more intervention.

---

### 1.2.5 Opportunity Cost — The Most Important Idea in Economics

**Definition:** The value of the **next best alternative** foregone when a choice is made.

**Examples:**

1. **The farmer's field:** 5 acres growing wheat (₹80,000 profit) or sugarcane (₹1,00,000 profit). If she chooses wheat, opportunity cost = ₹1,00,000.

2. **The student's evening:** 3 hours — study Physics or watch a movie. Choosing one means losing the value of the other.

3. **The government's budget:** ₹1 lakh crore on Central Vista = ₹1 lakh crore NOT on rural healthcare.

4. **The trader's allocation:** ₹10 lakh in gold futures instead of Nifty futures → opportunity cost is the Nifty return foregone. Every asset allocation decision is an opportunity cost decision.

**Economic goods have opportunity cost; free goods do not.**

| Feature | Economic goods | Free goods |
|---------|---------------|-----------|
| Requires resources | Yes | No |
| Has economic value | Yes | No |
| Opportunity cost | Yes | No |
| Examples | Cars, food, medicine | Air, sunlight |

**Note:** Clean air in Delhi may no longer be a "free good" — it's now scarce (pollution). *Tragedy of the commons* in action.

## Section 1.3 — The Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)

### 1.3.1 What the PPC Shows

The **PPC** (or PPF) is a graphical model showing the **maximum combinations of two goods** an economy can produce when:
- All resources are fully employed
- Resources are used efficiently
- Technology is fixed
- Time period is fixed

It captures **four fundamental economic concepts simultaneously**: Scarcity, Choice, Opportunity cost, Efficiency.

---

### 1.3.2 Interpreting Points

A country producing only two goods: **Wheat (Y-axis)** and **Cars (X-axis)**.

- **ON the curve (A, B, C, D):** All resources fully employed. **Efficient.**
- **INSIDE the curve (E):** Resources idle or misused. Unemployment. **Inefficient.**
- **OUTSIDE the curve (F):** Impossible given current resources and technology. **Unattainable** — unless curve shifts.

---

### 1.3.3 Shape of the PPC

**a) Straight-line (Linear) — Constant Opportunity Cost**
When resources are equally suited to producing both goods. *Example:* A farmer growing potatoes and onions — similar inputs.

**b) Concave (Bowed-Outward) — Increasing Opportunity Cost**
The typical shape. As you specialise in one good, you transfer resources that were better suited to the other — opportunity cost rises.

*Example:* Wheat and cars. Initially easy to shift resources. But at extremes, you convert prime farmland to factories and retrain farmers as engineers — opportunity cost per additional car keeps rising.

**Rule of thumb:** Most real economies have concave PPCs.

---

### 1.3.4 Assumptions of the PPC

1. **Fixed resources** — quantity of factors constant
2. **Fixed technology** — no innovation during the period
3. **Only two goods** — simplification for 2D graphing
4. **Full employment and efficiency** — no waste

---

### 1.3.5 Movements Along vs Shifts

**A) MOVEMENT ALONG the PPC** — reallocation of existing resources between the two goods. E.g., from (100 wheat, 20 cars) to (80 wheat, 40 cars). This is where **opportunity cost is visible** — 20 wheat lost to gain 20 cars.

**B) SHIFTS of the PPC** — the whole curve moves.

**Outward shift (growth) — curve moves RIGHT/UP:**
- Increase in **quantity** of factors (population, immigration, discovery of oil reserves)
- Increase in **quality** of factors (education, healthcare)
- **Technological progress** (AI, automation)
- Improved efficiency (better management, infrastructure)

*Example:* India's PPC has shifted outward dramatically since 1991 due to liberalisation, tech adoption, rising productivity.

**Inward shift (contraction) — curve moves LEFT/DOWN:**
- Natural disasters, pandemics
- War (destruction of capital, deaths)
- Brain drain
- Depletion of natural resources

*Example:* Ukraine's PPC has shifted sharply inward since 2022 due to war destruction and displacement.

---

### 1.3.6 Actual vs Potential Growth (Important IB Distinction)

**Actual growth:** Economy moves from *inside* the PPC toward the curve. The curve itself doesn't shift; the country just uses existing resources better.
- *Example:* Post-COVID recovery 2021–22 — factories reopened, workers rehired. Same PPC, closer to frontier.

**Potential growth:** The PPC itself shifts outward. The economy's *capacity* to produce increases.
- *Example:* India building highways, metros, semiconductor fabs — expanding future productive capacity.

**Actual growth = using what you have better. Potential growth = increasing what you can produce.**

---

### 1.3.7 What the PPC Illustrates — Summary

| Concept | How PPC shows it |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Scarcity** | The curve itself — you cannot produce beyond it |
| **Choice** | The many possible points along the curve |
| **Opportunity cost** | The slope — how much Y is lost to gain X |
| **Efficiency** | On the curve = efficient; inside = inefficient |
| **Economic growth** | Outward shift |
| **Recession / Underemployment** | Points inside |
| **Unattainability** | Points outside (given current resources) |

## Section 1.4 — The Circular Flow of Income Model

### 1.4.1 Purpose

The **Circular Flow of Income** shows how money, resources, and goods **flow** between actors in an economy. It illustrates **interdependence** — no economic actor operates in isolation.

**Fundamental principle:** In a market economy, exchanges are **voluntary** and **mutually beneficial** — every actor gains. Markets are **not zero-sum**.

---

### 1.4.2 The Two-Sector Model

Two actors:

**1. Households**
- Own all factors of production
- Sell/rent factors to firms → earn income (wages, rent, interest, profit)
- Spend income on goods → generate revenue for firms

**2. Firms**
- Hire factors from households → pay income
- Produce goods and services → sell them
- Receive revenue from household spending

**Two markets connect them:**

**a) Factor Market** — Households sell factors; firms buy. Money flows from firms → households.

**b) Product Market** — Firms sell goods; households buy. Money flows from households → firms.

**Two flows moving in opposite directions:**
- **Real flow:** Goods, services, and factors
- **Money flow:** Income, spending, revenue

Total money flowing in the model = **Gross National Income (GNI)**. Rising GNI → economy growing. Falling GNI → recession.

---

### 1.4.3 Assumptions of the Two-Sector Model

1. Firms produce all goods and services
2. Households own all factors of production
3. **Closed economy** — no foreign trade
4. **No government** — no taxes, no government spending
5. **No financial sector** — no banks, savings, investment
6. All income is spent (no savings)

Unrealistic — hence the five-sector model.

---

### 1.4.4 The Five-Sector Model (Realistic)

Adds: **Government, Financial Sector, Foreign Sector**.

**INJECTIONS (money flowing IN — increases GNI):**

| Injection | Source | Example |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| **Investment (I)** | Financial sector | Banks lending to firms |
| **Government Spending (G)** | Government | Highways, salaries, welfare |
| **Exports (X)** | Foreign sector | Indian pharma exports to US |

**LEAKAGES (money flowing OUT — decreases GNI):**

| Leakage | Destination | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Savings (S)** | Financial sector | Households deposit in banks |
| **Taxes (T)** | Government | Income tax, GST |
| **Imports (M)** | Foreign sector | Chinese electronics — money leaves India |

**The equilibrium condition:**

> **S + T + M = I + G + X**

If **injections > leakages**, national income grows. If **leakages > injections**, national income falls.

---

### 1.4.5 Real-World Example

India in a given year (illustrative):

- **Consumption (C):** ~₹150 lakh crore
- **Investment (I):** ~₹80 lakh crore
- **Government spending (G):** ~₹45 lakh crore
- **Exports (X):** ~₹40 lakh crore
- **Imports (M):** ~₹55 lakh crore

**GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)** = 150 + 80 + 45 + (40 − 55) = **₹260 lakh crore**

If government cuts spending by ₹10 lakh crore or exports collapse 20%, the circular flow shrinks — GDP falls unless offset elsewhere.

## Supplementary: The Ten Principles of Economics (Mankiw)

Useful for framing essay arguments.

### How People Make Decisions

**1. People face trade-offs** — "There's no such thing as a free lunch." Guns vs butter, work vs leisure.

**2. The cost of something is what you give up to get it** — Every decision has opportunity cost. The cost of college isn't just fees — it's the salary you could have earned.

**3. Rational people think at the margin** — Decisions compare *marginal* benefit vs *marginal* cost, not total. An airline should sell a last-minute ticket at ₹2,000 even if "average cost" is ₹5,000 — because marginal cost of one more passenger is only ₹500.

**4. People respond to incentives** — Higher fuel taxes → more efficient cars. GST discount on EVs → more EVs sold. Free school meals → higher attendance.

### How People Interact

**5. Trade can make everyone better off** — Voluntary trade benefits both sides. India specialises in IT, imports oil from Saudi Arabia. Both gain.

**6. Markets are usually a good way to organise economic activity** — Adam Smith's "invisible hand." Prices coordinate millions of decentralised decisions.

**7. Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes** — Markets fail with: **externalities** (pollution, education), **market power** (monopolies), **public goods** (defence), **information asymmetry** (used cars). But government failure is also possible.

### How the Economy Works

**8. A country's standard of living depends on its productivity** — Productivity = output per worker per hour. Why do US workers earn 20x more than Indian workers? Not 20x more effort — more capital, better tech, better institutions.

**9. Prices rise when the government prints too much money** — Inflation is fundamentally monetary (Friedman). Weimar Germany 1923, Zimbabwe 2008, Venezuela. Recent: US M2 grew 40% during COVID → contributed to 2022 inflation spike.

**10. Society faces a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment** — The Phillips Curve. Lower unemployment historically correlates with higher inflation. Short-run only — long-run breaks down (1970s stagflation). Central banks navigate this constantly.

## Section Summary — Quick Revision

1. **Economics = choice under scarcity.** Every choice has an **opportunity cost**.
2. **Nine central concepts (WISE CHOICES)** frame the entire IB syllabus.
3. **Four factors of production** (Land, Labour, Capital, Entrepreneurship) — each earns a distinct payment (Rent, Wages, Interest, Profit).
4. **The PPC** shows scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency, growth. On = efficient; inside = inefficient; outside = unattainable. Outward shifts = growth.
5. **The Circular Flow** shows household-firm interdependence. In the 5-sector model, injections (I + G + X) = leakages (S + T + M) at equilibrium.

## Exam-Ready Definitions (Memorise Verbatim)

- **Economics:** A social science studying how humans allocate scarce resources with alternative uses to satisfy unlimited wants and needs.
- **Scarcity:** Limited resources relative to unlimited wants.
- **Opportunity cost:** The value of the next-best alternative foregone when a choice is made.
- **Factors of production:** Inputs used to produce goods/services — Land, Labour, Capital, Entrepreneurship.
- **PPC:** A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with available resources and technology when all resources are fully and efficiently employed.
- **Circular flow of income:** A model showing the flow of money, resources, and goods between households and firms (and government, financial, foreign sectors in extended versions).
- **Economic efficiency:** Producing maximum output from given inputs, with no waste.
- **Ceteris paribus:** "All other things being equal."
- **Positive statement:** Factual, testable claim about the economy.
- **Normative statement:** Value-based claim about what *should* be.
