# Chapter 2: Demand and Supply — Competitive Markets

> **Textbook base:** Tragakes, *Economics for the IB Diploma*, 3rd Ed. (2022), Chapter 2 — "Competitive Markets: Demand and Supply."
>
> **Why this chapter matters.** Everything in microeconomics — market failure, government intervention, elasticities, welfare analysis — is built on the demand-supply model. If you're fluent here, the rest of Paper 1 becomes bookkeeping.
>
> **How to use these notes.** Every section has (1) the theory, (2) the diagram you should be able to redraw from memory, (3) a real-world example (usually Indian markets, since that's what you'll see on the news), and (4) the exam traps that cost easy marks. ## Section 2.1 — Introduction to Competitive Markets

A **market** is any arrangement — physical, digital, or purely conceptual — that allows buyers and sellers to interact and exchange goods, services, or resources at a price. It doesn't require a building. The Zerodha app is a market. Amazon India is a market. So is the mandi in Vashi.

A **competitive market** has three properties:

1. **Many buyers and many sellers** — so no single participant can dictate the price.
2. **A standardised product** — buyers see the offerings as roughly interchangeable (one 1-kg bag of Basmati rice from Kohinoor is close-enough to one from India Gate).
3. **Free entry and exit** — firms can start or stop selling without significant barriers.

Perfect competition is the textbook ideal — no real market meets all three conditions strictly. But wholesale agricultural markets, foreign exchange spot markets, and stock exchanges come very close. These are the markets the model describes best.

**Why the model matters even when markets are imperfect.** The demand-supply framework gives you a benchmark. When a real market misbehaves — one giant firm controls it, or the government sets the price, or pollution isn't priced in — you compare the outcome to what a competitive market *would* produce, and the gap is the source of policy analysis. This is the connection to market failure, monopoly, and government intervention in later chapters.

**Two forces, one equilibrium.** The rest of this chapter builds up demand (buyers' side), then supply (sellers' side), then puts them together at market equilibrium, then extracts the welfare and efficiency implications. Everything else in micro is decoration on this skeleton.

## Section 2.2 — Demand

**Definition.** Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are *willing and able* to purchase at each possible price, over a specified period, ceteris paribus.

Three words matter:

- **Willing** — you have to want it. (Wanting a Ferrari doesn't create demand for it.)
- **Able** — you have to be able to pay. (Purchasing power matters.)
- **Ceteris paribus** — all other factors held constant, so we can isolate the effect of price.

### The law of demand

**As price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus.** The demand curve slopes downward.

Three independent reasons underlie this:

1. **The income effect.** When the price of a good falls, your real income (purchasing power) rises — a given nominal income now buys more. You can afford more of the good.

2. **The substitution effect.** When the price of good A falls relative to good B, A becomes relatively cheaper, so consumers substitute towards A.

3. **The law of diminishing marginal utility.** Each additional unit of a good gives you less extra satisfaction than the one before. So you'll only buy more if the price falls enough to compensate for that lower marginal utility.

**Diagram (draw this from memory).** Vertical axis: price (P). Horizontal axis: quantity demanded per period (Q). Downward-sloping straight line labelled D. Movement *along* the curve = change in quantity demanded caused by a price change. Shift of the whole curve = change in demand caused by a non-price determinant.

### Movement along vs shift of the demand curve — the single biggest mark-losing confusion

- **Change in quantity demanded (movement along):** the price of THIS good changed. You move up or down the same curve.
- **Change in demand (shift of curve):** something OTHER than the price of this good changed. The whole curve shifts left (decrease in demand) or right (increase in demand).

**Never write "demand went up because the price fell."** That's a movement, not a change in demand. Write "quantity demanded went up because the price fell."

### The non-price determinants of demand — memorise as INTREP

| Letter | Determinant | How it shifts the curve |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | **Income** (for normal goods) | Income rises → demand rises → curve shifts right. Reverse for inferior goods (bus travel, second-hand clothing) — income rises, demand falls, curve shifts left. |
| **N** | **Number of consumers** | More consumers in the market → demand rises → curve shifts right. India's urbanising middle class shifts demand for cars, EMI-financed durables, private schools all rightward. |
| **T** | **Tastes and preferences** | Tastes shift for many reasons — trends, health awareness, advertising, celebrity endorsement, cultural change. Post-COVID demand for home fitness equipment: rightward shift driven by taste change. |
| **R** | **Related goods** — prices of | Two cases: (a) **substitutes** — good A's substitute rises in price → demand for A rises. Petrol price rises → demand for CNG cars rises. (b) **complements** — good A's complement rises in price → demand for A falls. Printer price rises → demand for ink cartridges falls. |
| **E** | **Expectations** of future price/income | Consumers expecting a price rise next month bring purchases forward → demand rises now. Expected income rise (a bonus, a promotion) → demand for durables and luxuries rises. |
| **P** | **Population characteristics** (age, tax, government policy) | Ageing population → demand for healthcare rises. Higher direct tax → disposable income falls → demand falls for most goods. |

**Note on the acronym.** Textbooks vary — Tragakes uses a slightly different mnemonic. The individual factors are what matters, not the word.

### Worked example — reading a shift

*Question style:* "The Reserve Bank of India cuts the repo rate. Show and explain the effect on the market for two-wheelers."

*Answer structure:*
- Repo rate cut → banks lower loan rates → EMI on a two-wheeler falls → real cost of buying now is lower → this is essentially an income/purchasing-power boost for two-wheeler consumers.
- Non-price determinant is *changes in the cost of borrowing* (falls under "income" for durables).
- Demand curve for two-wheelers shifts *right* (D → D').
- At the original price, there's now excess demand → upward pressure on price → new equilibrium at higher P and higher Q.

## Section 2.3 — Supply

**Definition.** Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are *willing and able* to produce and sell at each possible price, over a specified period, ceteris paribus.

### The law of supply

**As price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.** The supply curve slopes upward.

Why:

1. **Higher prices → higher profit margins → incentive to produce more.** Firms already in the market expand output.
2. **Higher prices attract new firms into the market** (in the long run). More firms means more supply.
3. **Rising marginal cost.** As output rises, firms eventually face rising marginal cost (overtime wages, less efficient machinery being brought online, more expensive inputs). They'll only produce those extra units if the price covers that higher marginal cost.

**Diagram.** Same axes as demand. Upward-sloping straight line labelled S. Movement *along* = change in quantity supplied (caused by a change in this good's own price). Shift = change in supply (caused by a non-price determinant).

### Non-price determinants of supply — memorise as COSTNET

| Letter | Determinant | How it shifts the curve |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | **Costs of factors of production** | Wages rise, raw material prices rise, oil prices rise → costs rise → supply falls → curve shifts left. |
| **O** | **Others' prices** (related goods in production) | Two cases: (a) **joint supply** — beef and leather from cattle. Beef price rises → more cattle raised → more leather supplied. (b) **competitive supply** — the same land can grow wheat OR rice. Wheat price rises → farmers switch to wheat → rice supply falls. |
| **S** | **Subsidies and taxes** | Indirect tax (GST, excise) → cost of production rises → supply shifts left. Subsidy → cost falls → supply shifts right. This is directly why India's Minimum Support Price (MSP) system, fertiliser subsidies, and PLI schemes exist. |
| **T** | **Technology** | Better technology → productivity rises → cost per unit falls → supply shifts right. Post-2020 solar panel cost has collapsed >80% due to technology; supply of solar power curve shifted massively right. |
| **N** | **Number of firms** | Entry of new firms → market supply rises → curve shifts right. Exit → curve shifts left. |
| **E** | **Expectations of future prices** | If producers expect prices to rise soon, they may withhold supply now (hoarding) → current supply falls. Or they may build up capacity → future supply rises. |
| **T** | **Turbulence** (natural events, shocks) | Cyclones, floods, geopolitical shocks. Russia-Ukraine war disrupted global wheat and sunflower oil supply — leftward shift, prices spiked worldwide. |

Again — textbook mnemonics vary. The determinants are what matter.

### Movement along vs shift — same trap as demand

Doubling down: **the good's OWN price does not shift its supply curve.** It moves you along the curve. Only OTHER factors shift the curve.

## Section 2.4 — Market Equilibrium

**Definition.** Market equilibrium is the price and quantity at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. There is no tendency for either price or quantity to change unless a non-price determinant shifts one of the curves.

Notation: equilibrium price = P\*, equilibrium quantity = Q\*.

**Diagram.** Draw D and S curves crossing. The intersection is (Q\*, P\*). Mark them.

### How the market self-corrects — the price mechanism at work

**Case A — price is above equilibrium (P > P\*):**
- Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded → **surplus** (excess supply).
- Unsold inventory piles up. Firms compete for buyers by cutting prices.
- Price falls until surplus is eliminated at P\*.

**Case B — price is below equilibrium (P < P\*):**
- Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied → **shortage** (excess demand).
- Buyers compete for scarce goods. Firms notice they can raise prices and still sell everything.
- Price rises until shortage is eliminated at P\*.

The market clears itself without central direction. This self-adjustment is what makes free markets efficient in the pure model.

### Changes in equilibrium — the four cases

Every exam question that says "what happens to the market for X when Y happens" boils down to one of these:

| Shift | Effect on P\* | Effect on Q\* |
|---|---|---|
| Demand rises (D shifts right) | rises | rises |
| Demand falls (D shifts left) | falls | falls |
| Supply rises (S shifts right) | falls | rises |
| Supply falls (S shifts left) | rises | falls |

**Simultaneous shifts** (harder — Paper 1 Part b territory). When both curves shift, one of P\* or Q\* is unambiguous and the other is ambiguous.

- Both curves shift right: Q\* definitely rises; P\* depends on which shifts more.
- Both shift left: Q\* definitely falls; P\* depends.
- D right, S left: P\* definitely rises; Q\* depends.
- D left, S right: P\* definitely falls; Q\* depends.

For a full-marks answer, state the definite direction, then explain the ambiguity with a diagram showing both cases.

### Real-world worked example — India's onion market, 2024

Onion prices in India are a political indicator — when they spike, governments lose state elections. Trace the 2024 chain:

1. **September 2023–May 2024:** the central government imposed an export ban, then a 40% export duty, then a $550/tonne minimum export price, to keep domestic prices low. Effect on the *domestic supply of onions*: shifted right (more onions kept in India). Effect on domestic price: fell.
2. **May–September 2024:** partial removal of restrictions. Domestic supply left slightly. Prices rose.
3. **April 2025:** 20% export duty removed after months of low farm-gate prices. Farmers had been squeezed and needed higher prices. Effect: domestic supply shifts left (some exports resume), farm-gate prices should rise.

This chain shows the **policy loop** — every intervention shifts one curve, which changes equilibrium, which creates political pressure, which triggers the next intervention. Perfect exam fodder.

## Section 2.5 — Efficiency in Competitive Markets

Once you have market equilibrium, the next question is: is this outcome *efficient*? The demand-supply model gives a clean answer using three concepts.

### Consumer surplus (CS)

The difference between what consumers were *willing to pay* (their maximum reservation price, read off the demand curve) and what they *actually paid* (the equilibrium price). Graphically: the triangle above the price line and below the demand curve, up to Q\*.

CS represents net benefit to consumers.

### Producer surplus (PS)

The difference between the price producers *actually received* and their minimum acceptable price (their marginal cost, read off the supply curve). Graphically: the triangle below the price line and above the supply curve, up to Q\*.

PS represents net benefit to producers.

### Social (community) surplus

**Social surplus = CS + PS.** Total welfare generated by the market.

### The efficiency claim

At equilibrium in a competitive market:
- Marginal social benefit (MSB — from the demand curve) equals marginal social cost (MSC — from the supply curve).
- Social surplus is maximised. Producing one more unit would cost more than the extra benefit; producing one less would sacrifice a unit whose benefit exceeded its cost.
- This condition (MSB = MSC) is **allocative efficiency**. Society's resources are being used to produce the right amount of this good — no more, no less.

Whenever price is prevented from reaching equilibrium (by a price ceiling, price floor, tax, subsidy, or monopoly), the market produces the wrong quantity and social surplus falls. The lost surplus is called **welfare loss** (or **deadweight loss**). This is the analytical heart of Chapters 4–5 on government intervention and market failure.

## Section 2.6 — The Three Roles of the Price Mechanism

Prices in a competitive market do three jobs simultaneously — the trio Adam Smith called the "invisible hand."

**1. Signalling.** Prices tell producers what consumers want more of (rising prices signal excess demand) and what they want less of (falling prices signal excess supply). No committee has to decide — the price does the communicating.

*Example.* When lithium prices tripled in 2022 on the back of EV demand, mining firms globally read the signal and expanded exploration. When prices halved in 2024 on oversupply, some mines cut back. No central plan; just prices talking.

**2. Incentive.** Rising prices motivate producers to supply more (there's profit to be made). Falling prices push them to cut costs, exit, or switch to other products.

**3. Rationing.** Prices allocate scarce goods to buyers who value them most (or who can pay most). At the equilibrium price, the only buyers who miss out are those who value the good less than the price.

The critique — and this is where the HL section goes — is that "rationing by willingness and ability to pay" has an *equity* problem. It allocates lifesaving insulin the same way it allocates concert tickets. Whether that's fair is a normative question. Chapter 5 on market failure and Chapter 8 on income distribution take this up.

## Section 2.7 — HL Extension: Critique of the Consumer/Producer Model

The whole demand-supply model rests on two big assumptions that behavioural economics has challenged.

### Assumption 1: rational, utility-maximising consumers

The classical model assumes consumers:
- Have well-defined preferences.
- Know their preferences.
- Have full information about prices and product quality.
- Choose the option that maximises their utility given their budget.

Real people don't. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's research (Nobel 2002 to Kahneman for prospect theory), and later Richard Thaler's work (Nobel 2017), showed consumers systematically deviate from rationality.

Key deviations you should be able to name for a HL Part b:

- **Bounded rationality** (Herbert Simon). Cognitive limits mean consumers *satisfice* — pick a good-enough option — rather than fully optimise.
- **Bounded self-control.** People know they should save for retirement / eat less sugar / not day-trade meme stocks, and do it anyway. Present bias.
- **Loss aversion.** Losses feel about twice as painful as equivalent gains. Consumers cling to bad investments, subscriptions, jobs.
- **Framing.** The way a choice is presented changes decisions. "5% off" vs "save ₹500" can prompt different responses even when identical.
- **Anchoring.** The first number seen affects subsequent judgments — the classic "was ₹4,999, now ₹2,999" trick.
- **Herding.** Consumers follow crowds. Explains fashion cycles, IPO frenzies, and the 2021 crypto boom.
- **Choice architecture** (Thaler & Sunstein's *Nudge*). Small design choices — defaults, ordering, salience — have large effects. The UK's automatic enrolment in workplace pensions is the textbook policy example, raising participation from ~40% to >90% without changing the underlying financial incentive.

### Assumption 2: rational, profit-maximising firms

Firms in the classical model:
- Know their costs and revenues.
- Choose output where marginal revenue = marginal cost.
- Pursue profit maximisation as their single objective.

Real firms:
- **Satisfice** on profits and pursue market share, brand prestige, executive prestige, or ESG goals.
- Have **incomplete information** about future demand, costs, or competitor behaviour.
- Suffer from **principal-agent problems** — managers pursue their own bonuses, not shareholder value.

### What this critique does and does not mean

**It does not mean the demand-supply model is wrong.** It means it's a simplification — useful for many purposes, misleading for others. When you see a real-market outcome that the classical model can't explain (persistent hoarding, sticky prices, herd bubbles), behavioural economics offers the missing piece.

For IB HL Paper 1, the expected structure is: state the classical assumption, name one or two behavioural deviations, give an example, conclude on when the model works and when it doesn't.

## Section 2.9 — Chapter Summary

The competitive market model has four moving parts:

1. **Demand** — quantity buyers will and can purchase at each price, downward-sloping. Shifted by non-price determinants (INTREP).
2. **Supply** — quantity producers will and can offer at each price, upward-sloping. Shifted by non-price determinants (COSTNET).
3. **Equilibrium** — where demand and supply meet. The price mechanism drives the market there automatically via surplus/shortage adjustments.
4. **Efficiency** — at equilibrium, MSB = MSC, social surplus (CS + PS) is maximised, allocative efficiency is achieved.

The **price mechanism** signals, incentivises, and rations without central direction — but only reaches an efficient outcome under the idealised assumptions of the model. When those break down (behavioural biases at HL; market failure, monopolies, externalities in later chapters), intervention becomes the policy question.

## Section 2.10 — Exam-Ready Definitions

- **Market** — any arrangement allowing buyers and sellers to interact and exchange at a price.
- **Competitive market** — a market with many buyers, many sellers, a standardised product, and free entry/exit; no individual participant has price-setting power.
- **Demand** — quantity of a good buyers are willing and able to purchase at each price over a specified period, ceteris paribus.
- **Law of demand** — as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus.
- **Change in quantity demanded** — movement along the demand curve caused by a change in the good's own price.
- **Change in demand** — shift of the whole demand curve caused by a change in a non-price determinant.
- **Substitute** — a good that can replace another in consumption; prices move demand for the other in the same direction.
- **Complement** — a good used with another in consumption; prices move demand for the other in the opposite direction.
- **Normal good** — a good for which demand rises when income rises.
- **Inferior good** — a good for which demand falls when income rises.
- **Supply** — quantity of a good producers are willing and able to offer at each price, ceteris paribus.
- **Law of supply** — as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.
- **Joint supply** — two goods produced together from the same process (beef and leather).
- **Competitive supply** — two goods that a producer must choose between using the same resources (wheat vs rice on the same land).
- **Market equilibrium** — the price-quantity combination at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- **Shortage / excess demand** — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price.
- **Surplus / excess supply** — quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the current price.
- **Consumer surplus** — difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay; the area above price and below demand up to Q\*.
- **Producer surplus** — difference between the price received and the minimum producers were willing to accept; the area below price and above supply up to Q\*.
- **Social (community) surplus** — CS + PS; total welfare from the market.
- **Allocative efficiency** — MSB = MSC; society's resources produce the right combination of goods.
- **Welfare (deadweight) loss** — the reduction in social surplus when the market is prevented from reaching equilibrium.
- **Price mechanism** — the process by which prices signal, incentivise, and ration in a free market.
- **Bounded rationality** (HL) — decision-making constrained by cognitive limits and time; consumers satisfice rather than fully optimise.
- **Loss aversion** (HL) — the psychological phenomenon that losses feel more painful than equivalent gains.
- **Choice architecture** (HL) — the design of the environment in which decisions are made; changes in defaults and framing can substantially change outcomes.

## Section 2.11 — Common Exam Traps

1. **Confusing "change in demand" with "change in quantity demanded."** The single most common mark loss on this chapter. Reserve "demand fell" for a leftward shift. Use "quantity demanded fell" for a movement along the curve caused by a rising price.
2. **Assuming a firm's own product price shifts its supply curve.** It doesn't. Own price causes movement along S. Only other factors shift it.
3. **Forgetting to label axes, curves, or equilibrium points on diagrams.** Automatic mark loss. Every diagram gets P, Q, D, S, P\*, Q\*.
4. **Drawing demand upward-sloping.** Yes, it happens. Slow down and label your axes so you catch it.
5. **Saying "both curves shift, so equilibrium is unchanged."** Rarely true. Usually one variable is unambiguous, the other depends on which shifts more. Show both scenarios.
6. **Confusing substitutes and complements.** Substitutes: price of one *rises*, demand for the other *rises* (they compete). Complements: price of one *rises*, demand for the other *falls* (they go together).
7. **Mixing up joint and competitive supply.** Joint: same production process yields both (beef & leather). Competitive: producer must choose between them (wheat vs rice).
8. **Writing "supply and demand meet at equilibrium" without saying WHY the market GOES there.** The mechanism — surplus/shortage forces — is the point. Always describe the adjustment process.
9. **HL only: naming a behavioural bias without linking it back to the model's assumption.** State the assumption first, name the deviation, give an example, THEN evaluate.
10. **Ignoring the equity critique of the price mechanism.** Efficient rationing by willingness to pay is not the same as fair rationing. HL Part b questions often reward flagging this.
