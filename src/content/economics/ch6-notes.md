# Economics HL — 2.4 Critique of the Maximising Behaviour of Consumers and Producers

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **Rational consumer choice** assumes complete and consistent preferences, perfect information, and utility maximisation within a budget.
> - **Behavioural economics** uses psychology and observed decisions to explain why these assumptions do not always hold.
> - A **nudge** steers choices without financial incentives or sanctions and without removing options; **choice architecture** is the design of the decision setting.
> - Firms may seek profit, but also social responsibility, market share, growth, revenue, or satisfactory results across several goals.

## 2. Rational consumer choice

**Rational economic decision-making** means individuals are assumed to act in their best self-interest and try to maximise the satisfaction expected from their economic decisions. In the standard model, consumers maximise utility; firms maximise profit; workers seek high wages; investors seek high returns (Tragakes, p. 72).

The assumptions about preferences are:

| Assumption | Examiner-acceptable meaning | Coursebook label |
|---|---|---|
| **Completeness** | A consumer can rank any two alternatives: prefer A, prefer B, or be indifferent. | Complete preferences |
| **Transitivity** | If A is preferred to B and B to C, then A is preferred to C. Rankings are consistent. | Transitive preferences |
| **Non-satiation** | More of a good is preferred to less, other things equal. | More is preferred to less |

The model also assumes **perfect information**: consumers know the relevant products, qualities and prices, so they face no uncertainty. **Utility** is the satisfaction a consumer derives from consumption. **Utility maximisation** means choosing the combination of goods and services that gives the greatest total utility for a given budget or income (Tragakes, pp. 72–73).

Marginal utility and diminishing marginal utility are building blocks of the standard consumer model; their explanation of the demand curve belongs in [[Economics HL — Demand — Notes]]. The formal derivation is beyond this section (Tragakes, p. 73).

**Homo economicus** (“economic man”) is the simplified model of an agent who continually maximises self-interest. A challenge is whether every motive should be re-described as personal utility: if altruism is always counted as self-interest because it gives the giver satisfaction, the theory risks explaining every action and therefore explaining little. Tragakes presents Simon's argument that altruism should be treated as a motive in its own right, and notes that Smith and Ricardo allowed for mixed motives (pp. 74–75).

## 3. Behavioural economics and limits to choice

**Behavioural economics** studies how people actually make decisions, drawing especially on psychology and also on sociology and neuroscience. It uses experiments to observe decisions rather than relying only on assumptions about behaviour (Tragakes, p. 75).

### 3.1 Biases

A **bias** is a systematic error in thinking or judgement. Unlike random mistakes, systematic patterns can sometimes be anticipated (Tragakes, p. 73).

| Bias | Definition | Tragakes example | Why it challenges the standard model |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rule of thumb** | A simple guideline based on experience or common sense that reduces the effort of a complex decision. | Estimating a fruit serving as about the size of a fist. | The consumer uses a shortcut rather than comparing every option; it may be useful, but need not identify the optimum. |
| **Anchoring** | Giving undue weight to an irrelevant reference point, often the first information encountered. | Jeans first seen at USD 150 make a similar pair at USD 100 seem cheap, even if the same item is available for USD 50 elsewhere. | The initial price is not reliable evidence of the product's value, but it shapes the judgement. |
| **Framing** | A choice is affected by how equivalent information is presented. | Beef described as “80% lean” may be preferred to “20% fat”. | The information is equivalent, but the presentation changes the response. |
| **Availability** | Relying more heavily on information that is recent or readily recalled. | Recent advertising makes one brand easier to remember and therefore more likely to be selected. | Recent or memorable information is not necessarily more reliable than other information. |

### 3.2 Bounded behaviour and imperfect information

| Concept | Examiner-acceptable meaning | Illustration | Assumption challenged |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bounded rationality** | Consumers are rational only within limits: information is incomplete and costly to obtain, and the mind cannot process unlimited information. They often **satisfice**—seek a satisfactory rather than optimal outcome. | Choosing a service after comparing a few plausible options instead of researching every available plan. | Utility maximisation and perfect information. |
| **Bounded self-control** | People exercise self-control only within limits and may not follow through on choices that would serve their longer-term interests. | Eating or spending more than intended, saving too little, or working less than planned. | The assumption that choices consistently maximise the consumer's own welfare. |
| **Bounded selfishness** | People are selfish only within limits; self-interest alone does not explain all concern for others or contributions to public welfare. | Anonymous donations, volunteering, or a firm choosing not to dismiss workers during a recession despite a cost to profit. | The assumption that decisions are motivated only by private gain. |
| **Imperfect information** | Consumers cannot access all relevant information about prices, products and quality, so decisions may be based on incomplete or faulty information. | Buying a used car without knowing its full repair history. | Perfect information. |

Bounded rationality does not mean people are wholly irrational: satisficing can be a sensible response to the cost of searching and limited mental capacity. Likewise, altruism may be compatible with a broad definition of utility, but that broad definition can make the self-interest assumption hard to test (Tragakes, pp. 74–75).

## 4. Choice architecture and nudges

A **nudge** is a method designed to influence choices predictably without offering financial incentives, imposing sanctions, or limiting choice (Tragakes, p. 75). Thaler and Sunstein coined the term in their 2008 book, as reported by Tragakes. **Choice architecture** is the design of the setting in which people make choices; a **choice architect** arranges that setting. Framing is one element of choice architecture (p. 76).

| Type | Definition | How the choice is shaped |
|---|---|---|
| **Default choice** | The option that applies if a person does nothing. | Inertia or habit can keep people with the default, so the architect selects which outcome follows from inaction. England's deemed-consent organ-donation system took effect on 20 May 2020; people can still record a different decision.[5] |
| **Restricted choice** | A choice limited by government or another authority. | The set of available options is narrowed, for example by a rule that excludes some options. |
| **Mandated choice** | A choice that must be made, while the decision among the available alternatives remains with the person. | A form or process requires an answer rather than allowing the person to leave the choice blank. |

> [!warning] Nudge or regulation?
> Tragakes groups default, restricted and mandated choice under choice architecture. But a restriction removes options, which conflicts with the strict definition of a nudge as not limiting choice. In an evaluation, distinguish a genuine nudge that preserves options from regulation or a required decision.

> [!note] Date organ-donation examples
> Do not treat “the UK” as one timeless default system. NHS Organ Donation states that Wales changed its law in December 2015; England's system came into effect on 20 May 2020.[5] The Netherlands' amended Donor Act came into force on 1 July 2020, so distinguish the timing of a law's passage from its implementation.[6]

## 5. Evaluating behavioural policy

| Potential advantages | Potential disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Can be relatively simple and low-cost compared with a large financial intervention. | Findings may depend on the specific situation and may not generalise across time, income groups or cultures. |
| Can preserve freedom of choice when the person can freely reject the default or ignore the message. | Choice architecture can manipulate people, particularly if it is hidden or works through framing they do not notice. |
| Can be tested and adjusted through trials, and can address behaviour that the standard model struggles to explain. | Government may use nudges instead of a more effective tax, subsidy or regulation; the choice architect's idea of a “socially desirable” choice may not match people's own preferences. |
| May complement information campaigns or other policies. | Evidence of a response in one context does not establish a universal behavioural theory. Transparency and public discussion matter. |

### Evidence: HMRC tax letters and social norms

A UK field experiment by Hallsworth, List, Metcalfe and Vlaev varied short messages in tax reminder letters sent to 101,471 Self Assessment taxpayers who had declared tax but had not yet paid it. The “minority norm” message—stating that nine out of ten people paid on time and that the recipient was in a small minority—was associated with a 5.1 percentage-point increase in payment within 23 days in an adjusted model. Gain- and loss-framed public-service messages each had an estimated 1.6 percentage-point effect.[4]

The authors estimated that the messages accelerated about GBP 4.9 million in payments during the first 23 days. This is not evidence that the same amount was new tax revenue: the experiment measured earlier payment of declared liabilities, and the authors cautioned that the study could not establish how much was new revenue rather than accelerated revenue. The sample also consisted of people who had already filed but not paid, so do not assume the same effect for all taxpayers.[4]

### Evidence: UK workplace pension automatic enrolment

The Department for Work and Pensions reported that eligible employees participating in a workplace pension rose from 10.7 million (55%) in 2012 to 18.7 million (87%) in 2018, a 32-percentage-point increase as automatic-enrolment duties were phased in from October 2012 to February 2018. Workers could opt out.[2] The more recent DWP series reports that 89% of eligible employees in Great Britain—21.7 million people—were saving in 2024; among eligible private-sector employees at micro employers, participation was around 59%.[3]

This illustrates the power of defaults, but automatic enrolment is not a pure nudge: employers have legal duties and the scheme includes employer pension contributions. A before-and-after rise therefore cannot isolate the effect of the default alone. The difference between the overall rate and the micro-employer figure also cautions against treating results as uniform.[2][3]

**Mainstream response.** Rational-choice theory can still be useful even if its assumptions do not describe every individual perfectly. Tragakes notes that consumers often respond to monetary incentives in the predicted direction—for example, a higher petrol tax is expected to reduce quantity demanded—and that aggregate behaviour may fit the model better than every individual decision (p. 78). A balanced answer weighs individual deviations against the model's predictive usefulness and the context of the evidence.

## 6. Producer behaviour and business objectives

### 6.1 Profit maximisation and its limits

**Profit maximisation** is the objective of making the difference between a firm's total revenue and total costs as large as possible:

**Profit = total revenue − total cost.**

Tragakes' pizza example (p. 79): a restaurant sells 400 pizzas a day at USD 7 each, giving revenue of USD 2,800. With daily costs of USD 2,650, profit is USD 150. The arithmetic is 400 × 7 = 2,800 and 2,800 − 2,650 = 150.

This section defines the objective but does not require a marginal-cost/marginal-revenue diagram. The book says it develops profit maximisation later in Chapter 7 (HL).

Tragakes questions the realism of profit-maximisation models (ToK 2.3, p. 82): firms may not know their demand curves or rivals' responses; marginal costs can be difficult to identify; short-run profit may conflict with long-run goals; and market conditions may change before a decision takes effect. Evidence that firms pursue other objectives also challenges profit maximisation as a complete description.

**Evaluation of assumptions:** Friedman argues that unrealistic assumptions can still be useful if a theory predicts well; Samuelson argues that predictions cannot be separated from the assumptions and theory that produce them. Use the disagreement to frame a balanced judgement rather than claiming that an unrealistic assumption automatically makes a model useless (Tragakes, p. 82).

### 6.2 Alternative business objectives

| Objective | Meaning | Why a firm may pursue it / trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** | Taking socially beneficial action, such as avoiding pollution, using sound environmental practices, respecting human and labour rights, or supporting community activities. | Consumer concern, boycotts and employee productivity may protect revenue and reputation; responsible conduct may reduce pressure for government regulation. Costs may rise, and Tragakes notes that studies of the overall profit effect are inconclusive (p. 80). |
| **Market share** | A firm's share of total sales in its market. | It can indicate performance relative to competitors and may help the firm gain economies of scale. A firm might lower prices or spend more on advertising or research, reducing profit in the short run. The book's example is USD 10 million of sales in a USD 50 million market: 10 ÷ 50 × 100 = **20%** (p. 80). |
| **Growth maximisation** | Prioritising the expansion of the firm rather than the highest immediate profit. | Growth can support economies of scale, diversification and market power, reduce reliance on one product or market, and make owners' and managers' interests more alike. Rapid growth can still sacrifice short-run profit and bring management challenges (p. 81). |
| **Revenue maximisation** *(coursebook extension)* | Prioritising sales revenue rather than profit. | Managers may find sales easier to measure and use as targets; rewards may be linked to sales. Owners who prioritise profit may disagree with managers. Revenue maximisation is discussed in the coursebook but is not named in its p. 72 list of example alternative objectives (p. 81). |
| **Satisficing** | Seeking satisfactory levels across several objectives instead of the optimal result for any single objective. | A large firm contains groups with overlapping or conflicting goals, so it may compromise and rank objectives. This is a response to bounded rationality, not simply laziness (p. 81). |

## 7. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Treating rationality or perfect information as a fact about every consumer. | Call them assumptions of the standard model, then evaluate their usefulness and limits. |
| Defining a bias but not showing how it affects a decision. | Give the mechanism and link it to the assumption challenged. A shortcut is not automatically a systematic error. |
| Calling every policy that affects behaviour a nudge. | Check the definition: no financial incentive, sanction or loss of choice. Distinguish nudges from restrictions and mandates. |
| Confusing default, restricted and mandated choice. | Default: inaction selects. Restricted: options are removed. Mandated: an answer is required, but the person selects it. |
| Claiming that a rise in pension participation proves the default alone caused it. | Note the legal employer duties, contributions and other parts of the policy package; describe the evidence and its limits. |
| Treating tax paid sooner as new revenue, or one trial as proof of universal behaviour. | Distinguish accelerated from additional payments and state who was studied. |
| Equating market share, growth, revenue and profit. | Define each objective and compare which outcome the firm prioritises. |
| Saying CSR must lower profit or that satisficing is laziness. | Discuss revenue, productivity and regulation as well as costs; explain satisficing as compromise under limits. |
| Adding MC = MR diagrams or a full firm-cost analysis to this section. | Stay with the objective definitions here; the coursebook develops the formal profit theory in Chapter 7 (HL). |

## 8. Self-test

> [!question]- Questions
> 1. Define rational consumer choice and explain completeness, transitivity and non-satiation.
> 2. Explain how two of the textbook's biases can lead a consumer away from the standard rational-choice model.
> 3. Distinguish default, restricted and mandated choice, and explain why restricted choice creates a tension with the strict definition of a nudge.
> 4. Evaluate the HMRC letter experiment as evidence for behavioural policy, including one result and one limitation.
> 5. Distinguish profit maximisation from two alternative business objectives, and define satisficing.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Rational consumer choice assumes decisions reflect the consumer's best self-interest and maximise utility. **Completeness** means any two alternatives can be ranked; **transitivity** means rankings are consistent; **non-satiation** means more of a good is preferred to less. The model also assumes perfect information and utility maximisation within a budget.
> 2. Any two. **Anchoring:** an irrelevant first price becomes a reference point, so a price can seem attractive despite a better comparable price elsewhere. **Framing:** equivalent information such as “80% lean” and “20% fat” can produce different choices. **Availability:** recent or easy-to-recall information is over-weighted. **Rule of thumb:** a shortcut replaces the costly comparison of all options.
> 3. A **default** applies if the person does nothing. A **restricted choice** removes or limits options. A **mandated choice** requires a selection but leaves the selection to the person. Restricted choice conflicts with the strict nudge definition because it limits choice; the textbook still groups these approaches under choice architecture.
> 4. In a UK randomized field experiment involving tax reminder letters, the minority-norm message had an adjusted 5.1-percentage-point effect on payments within 23 days; the authors estimated about GBP 4.9 million in payments was accelerated.[4] The sample was taxpayers who had filed but not yet paid, and accelerated payments are not necessarily new revenue.
> 5. **Profit maximisation** seeks the largest total revenue minus total cost. **CSR** prioritises socially beneficial conduct; **market share** prioritises a firm's proportion of market sales; **growth maximisation** prioritises expansion; **revenue maximisation** prioritises sales revenue. **Satisficing** seeks satisfactory results across multiple ranked objectives rather than the optimum for one.
