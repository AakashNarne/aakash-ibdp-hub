# English Paper 1 — Non-Literary Text Types

**Syllabus:** Analyse the choices made in the listed non-literary text types, responding to the guiding question. See [[_Syllabus Index]]. For examiner-commented sample scores and task-specific pitfalls, see [[English A — Assessed Work Mark Lessons]].

> [!info] Why this matters
> Paper 1 is not a device hunt. You earn marks by explaining how a writer's or designer's choices guide a particular audience toward a purpose.

## If you remember nothing else

- Make a thesis that answers the guiding question and names **choice + effect + audience/purpose**.
- Every paragraph moves from a precise detail to its meaning and effect; do not stop at naming a technique.
- Treat layout, image, typography, wording, omissions and source as deliberate choices.
- Text-type conventions are clues, not rules: analyse what this text actually does.

## A repeatable method

**Command terms:** *analyse* and *evaluate*. Analyse how choices create meaning; evaluate how effectively they serve the purpose for this audience.

1. Identify text type, producer/publication, date/context, audience and purpose.
2. Read the guiding question; use it to select evidence rather than list everything.
3. Annotate verbal and visual choices: framing, hierarchy, diction, tone, colour, scale, juxtaposition, captions and omissions.
4. Plan a thesis and 2–3 distinct claims.
5. Write each paragraph as **choice → evidence → mechanism → audience effect → link to question**.

## Text-type conventions to test against the page

| Type | Typical purpose and conventions | Useful analytical questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Print advertisement** | Sell or position a product; brand, product image, slogan, visual hierarchy, testimonial, price/claim, colour and white space | What desire or need is constructed? How do image and copy reinforce or complicate one another? |
| **Public service announcement (PSA)** | Encourage public awareness or behaviour; sponsor identity, urgent framing, imperative/call to action, warning, emotional or factual appeal | Does it persuade through fear, empathy, authority or efficacy? Is the action clear and achievable? |
| **Political cartoon** | Comment or criticise; caricature, symbols, labels, exaggeration, irony and juxtaposition | Who or what is ridiculed? What shared context must the reader know? What is the cartoonist's position? |
| **Comic strip** | Tell a brief story or make social commentary; panels, gutters, speech/thought balloons, pacing, repetition and punchline | What changes between panels? What does the gutter make the reader infer? How does humour carry criticism? |
| **Infographic** | Explain or persuade with data; headline, chart type, scale, legend, icons, colour coding, source and visual hierarchy | What is counted and omitted? Is scale proportionate? How does design make the data seem urgent, simple or authoritative? |
| **Magazine cover page** | Sell the issue and establish identity; masthead, cover image, cover lines, issue/date/price, typography, colour palette and placement | Which cover line dominates and why? What identity or aspiration is attached to the subject? What does the cover promise but withhold? |
| **Feature article** | Explore a subject in depth for a broad readership; scene-setting lead, anecdote, human detail, subheadings, quotes and a developed narrative | How does the opening draw the reader in? How are individual stories linked to a wider issue? |
| **Opinion article** | Argue a position; identifiable thesis/voice, evaluative diction, reasons, evidence, counterargument and conclusion | How does the writer establish credibility and handle opposing views? Is the tone measured, polemical or ironic? |
| **News article** | Report a recent event; headline, byline/date, lead with key facts, inverted-pyramid structure, attribution and quotations | What is prioritised in the lead? Whose voices are included or absent? Does apparently neutral wording frame the event? |

These conventions overlap. A feature may argue; news may contain vivid narrative; an advertisement may look like editorial content. Name the likely type, then qualify your claim with evidence.

## Class focus: artists and infographic source

| Creator / source | Useful context | What to analyse (always verify against the actual text) |
|---|---|---|
| **Uğur Gallenkuş** (Ugur Gallenkus) | Turkish digital-collage artist; his “Parallel Universes” works juxtapose photojournalistic imagery with scenes of peaceful everyday life. | Examine the join/seam, crop, scale, gaze, colour and visual bridge between the two scenes. Ask what unequal realities are placed side by side, how the contrast positions the viewer, whose perspective is visible or missing, and what source/context information the work gives. Do not assume every collage uses the same structure or that juxtaposition alone proves a specific political claim. |
| **Liza Donnelly** | American cartoonist and writer; her official site identifies her as a New Yorker cartoonist and visual journalist. | For cartoons, inspect line, gesture, expression, scale, symbol, framing, caption/dialogue and any use of blank space. Explain how understatement, incongruity or irony shapes the comment on culture or politics. A cartoon may be one-panel or use another form; analyse the one in front of you, not a memorised “Donnelly style.” |
| **Fountain Ink — Infographics** | The publisher maintains an infographic archive. A class-practice example is [“Doped and out”](https://fountainink.in/infographic/doped-and-out), about sports doping and bans. | Track headline → lead claim → visual hierarchy → evidence/figures → source/credit. In “Doped and out,” the cyclist and podium anchor the topic, while contrasting coloured bands and small text blocks organise claims and cases. Check legibility, attribution, date, denominator and whether each visual actually supports the headline. Treat the graphic as a text to analyse—not as independently verified data. |

> [!tip] Use class exemplars without forcing a formula
> Gallenkuş is a useful comparison for **juxtaposition and photo-collage**, Donnelly for **cartooning and visual commentary**, and Fountain Ink for **editorial infographics**. Start from what is visible, then explain its effect in relation to that particular text's audience and purpose.

## From observation to analysis

> [!example] Worked micro-analysis (illustrative, not a source text)
> Suppose a road-safety PSA places a cracked helmet in the foreground and a small line, “One ride. One choice.” beneath it. Do not write “the image is effective.” Explain that the oversized damage makes consequence visually immediate, while the compressed parallel slogan presents safety as a simple personal decision. The contrast narrows the viewer's attention from general danger to an actionable choice. The effect depends on the PSA's intended audience and context.

## Exam-ready terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Visual hierarchy** | The order in which design elements attract a viewer's attention. |
| **Framing** | The selection and presentation of details that shape interpretation. |
| **Juxtaposition** | Placing elements together so their contrast or relationship creates meaning. |
| **Call to action** | A direct instruction intended to prompt a specific response. |
| **Inverted pyramid** | News organisation that places the most consequential information first, followed by less essential detail. |
| **Byline** | The line identifying the author of an article. |
| **Connotation** | Associations a word or image carries beyond its literal meaning. |

## Common mistakes / examiner traps

> [!warning] Spotting is not analysis
> Naming “alliteration”, “pathos” or “close-up” earns little by itself. Explain what the choice makes this audience think, feel or do.

> [!warning] Do not assume neutrality
> Data displays and news writing still select, order and omit information. Analyse those choices without alleging bias unless the text supports it.

> [!warning] Keep the guiding question in control
> A paragraph that is accurate but irrelevant does not build a focused response. Link each claim back to the question.

> [!warning] Avoid universal colour/genre rules
> Red does not always mean danger; a headline does not prove an article is news. Use the text's context and evidence.

## Self-test

1. What three-part thesis formula helps answer a guiding question?
2. Name two conventions of a political cartoon and explain one analytical question they raise.
3. How does an inverted-pyramid news story differ from a feature lead?
4. Give two ways an infographic can shape interpretation without changing its numbers.
5. In a magazine cover analysis, why should you discuss the relationship between the image and cover lines?

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Choice + effect/mechanism + audience or purpose, directly answering the question.
> 2. Caricature and labels, for example; ask which figure or idea is criticised and how the exaggeration makes that judgement legible.
> 3. News leads with the most consequential facts; a feature often opens with a scene, anecdote or human detail before widening the focus.
> 4. Chart selection, axis/scale, colour, ordering, icons, headline and omitted comparisons can frame unchanged data.
> 5. The image establishes associations while cover lines anchor or redirect them; their interaction constructs the issue's promise and intended reader.
