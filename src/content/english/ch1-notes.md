# Chapter 1: SA1 Focus — Paper 1 Non-Literary Analysis

> **English A: Language & Literature SL — Summative Assessment 1.**
> The paper: one unseen non-literary text, one guiding question, 75 minutes, 20 marks. Your job is to build a thesis around the guiding question and prove it with the text's own choices.
>
> This chapter covers everything you need for SA1: the analytical framework, the five non-literary text types on the syllabus, essay structure, and the traps that quietly bleed marks. Flashcards drill the definitions; these notes drill the *reasoning*.

---

## Section 1.1 — Paper 1 Task and Marking Criteria

At SL you receive **one unseen non-literary text** with a single **guiding question** printed under it. The question always points at how the author has shaped the reader's response — through structure, language, image, layout, tone — for a particular purpose. You do not choose between texts; there is only one.

**Duration:** 1 hour 15 minutes. **Total marks:** 20.

### The four marking criteria — SL

| Criterion | Marks | What earns them |
|---|---|---|
| **A · Understanding & interpretation** | 5 | Reading the text beyond the literal — inferences supported by evidence. |
| **B · Analysis & evaluation** | 5 | Explaining *how* specific authorial choices shape meaning; not just naming devices. |
| **C · Focus & organization** | 5 | A thesis-led argument that stays anchored to the guiding question. |
| **D · Language** | 5 | Precise vocabulary, controlled register, subject-specific terminology used correctly. |

### The time split that actually works

- **Annotate the text: 15 minutes.** Read twice. Circle choices; write "why" next to each.
- **Plan: 5 minutes.** Thesis + three body paragraph angles.
- **Write: 50 minutes.** Aim ~600–800 words.
- **Review: 5 minutes.** Fix grammar; check thesis appears in intro *and* conclusion.

Do not skip the plan. Criterion C collapses without it.

## Section 1.2 — The SPACECAT Analytical Framework

Not a checklist to work through in order — a set of lenses to interrogate any non-literary text. Run through it during annotation; use the two or three lenses that give you the most material.

| Lens | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **S — Subject** | What is the text literally about? |
| **P — Purpose** | To persuade, inform, entertain, warn, provoke, sell, mobilise? |
| **A — Audience** | Who is being addressed? Age, class, ideology, insider/outsider status. |
| **C — Context** | When and where was this produced? What historical/cultural moment does it respond to? |
| **E — Exigence** | What prompted the text — the specific event, controversy, or gap it fills? |
| **C — Choices** | Diction, syntax, image, colour, layout, font, sound, structure, register. |
| **A — Appeals** | Ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), logos (logic), kairos (timeliness). |
| **T — Tone** | The attitude the text takes toward its subject *and* its audience. |

### Example — how this reads as a thesis

> "By juxtaposing a stark black-and-white photograph with a hopeful serif tagline, this PSA reframes homelessness from an abstract social problem into a personal ethical claim on the middle-class Mumbai reader, exploiting *kairos* around the monsoon to compress emotional and civic appeals."

That single sentence names purpose, audience, context, choices, appeal, and tone. Every body paragraph then evidences one of those claims.

## Section 1.3 — Print Advertisements

**Purpose:** commercial persuasion. Every design choice serves the sale.

### What to look for

- **Visual hierarchy** — what the eye lands on first (usually the product, a face, or a headline). Directs attention deliberately.
- **Copy vs image ratio** — luxury ads are image-heavy with sparse copy (aspirational); mass-market ads carry more copy (practical persuasion).
- **Brand positioning** — the ad places the product on a spectrum (premium/mass, traditional/modern, safe/rebellious).
- **Slogan & tagline** — condense the brand promise; often use rhyme, alliteration, or imperatives ("Just do it.").
- **Colour psychology** — red urgency/desire, blue trust, green natural/health, black premium, white minimalism/purity.
- **Typography** — serif = tradition/authority; sans-serif = modern/clean; handwritten = artisanal/personal.
- **Semiotics** — signs and symbols carrying cultural meaning (a wedding ring, a national flag, a lotus, a stopwatch).
- **Model / spokesperson** — celebrity capital (Virat Kohli for a brand), aspirational body types, cultural coding.
- **White space** — luxury brands use it; discount brands crowd every pixel.

### The three classical appeals — with concrete markers

| Appeal | How it shows up in an ad |
|---|---|
| **Ethos** | Celebrity endorsement, "since 1892", awards, expert testimony, doctor imagery. |
| **Pathos** | Faces in emotional states, family imagery, aspirational lifestyle, fear (before/after). |
| **Logos** | Statistics, comparisons, ingredient lists, price/value claims, technical diagrams. |

### Common trap

Do not just *name* the appeal. Name it, quote the evidence, then explain the effect on the target audience.

- Weak: "Uses pathos."
- Strong: "The child's downcast eyes exploit *pathos* to induce guilt in the parental reader, priming them for the product's rescue narrative."

The second sentence earns marks under Criterion B; the first earns nothing.

## Section 1.4 — Public Service Announcements (PSAs)

The commercial ad's civic cousin. Same techniques, different transaction. A PSA is a non-commercial message from a government body, NGO, or civil society organisation aiming to change behaviour or awareness. The "product" is a social action (wear a helmet, vaccinate, save water). The reader gives attention and behaviour; the maker gives no goods in return.

### Distinguishing features

- **Explicit call to action** — often a hotline, a QR code, a hashtag, a directive verb ("Report", "Stop", "Ask").
- **Sponsor identification** — logo of Ministry of X, WHO, UNICEF, a state road-safety body. Establishes ethos through institutional authority.
- **Fear appeal (shock advertising)** — graphic imagery of consequences (smokers' lungs, drink-driving crashes). Ethical debate: paternalism vs efficacy.
- **Cognitive dissonance** — pairs a normal image with a jarring caption to force reconsideration.
- **Statistical hook** — one arresting number that carries the argument ("One drink. One life.").

### Commercial ad vs PSA — quick contrast

| Dimension | Commercial ad | PSA |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Sell a product / service | Change behaviour or awareness |
| Beneficiary | Company (profit) | Society or the reader themselves |
| Tone range | Aspirational, humorous, seductive | Sombre, urgent, moral |
| Sponsor | Brand identity is central | Institutional; sometimes understated |
| Appeal mix | Pathos + logos + ethos | Pathos + ethos usually dominate |

## Section 1.5 — Political Cartoons

Compressed argument in a single frame — usually in the newspaper's editorial voice.

### The five tools of the political cartoonist

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Caricature** | Exaggerated physical features that make a public figure instantly recognisable and often ridiculous (large ears, distinctive hair, prominent nose). |
| **Symbolism** | An object stands for an idea — a dove for peace, an elephant for the BJP or US Republicans, a bear for Russia, scales for justice. |
| **Labels** | Direct text on figures or objects clarifying what they represent when caricature isn't enough ("Big Tech", "The Voter", "RBI"). |
| **Exaggeration** | Scale, proportion, or setting distorted for effect — a tiny politician facing a giant "Inflation" monster. |
| **Analogy** | A situation compared to another — an election reframed as a boxing match, a summit as a poker game. |

### Reading the argument — a repeatable method

1. **Inventory.** Identify every figure, object, and label. Cartoons reward pedantic inventory.
2. **Anchor.** Locate the historical/political moment — a cartoon is anchored in a specific week's news.
3. **Side.** Ask whose side the cartoonist is on. Whose absurdity is being exposed?
4. **Gap.** Find the irony or juxtaposition — political cartoons almost always run on the gap between what is claimed and what is depicted.

### Context matters

A cartoon in a US liberal paper reads a politician differently from one in a right-leaning one. Note the publication if given — it colours everything, including which caricature choices count as "sharp" versus "cheap".

## Section 1.6 — Comic Strips

Narrative in panels. Humour, character, and social observation — usually three or four panels, sometimes one.

### The grammar of the comic strip

- **Panels** — discrete moments. Size and shape encode duration and importance.
- **Gutters** — the white space *between* panels. The reader mentally fills in the elapsed action; much of the humour lives here.
- **Speech balloons vs thought bubbles vs captions** — external dialogue vs internal monologue vs narrator. Bubble shape (spiky = shout, wavy = radio/phone) carries tone.
- **Character design** — simplified, iconic. Scott McCloud: the more abstract the drawing, the more universal the reader-projection.
- **Pacing** — the setup usually sits in panel 1, complication in 2–3, punchline in 4 (or single panel = compressed setup+punch).
- **Recurring gags** — *Calvin and Hobbes*, *Peanuts*, *Dilbert* build meaning through readers' accumulated familiarity with characters.

### Analytical angle

Comic strips often smuggle social critique inside domestic comedy — office bureaucracy in *Dilbert*, existential anxiety in *Peanuts*, consumerism and childhood imagination in *Calvin & Hobbes*. Read the humour as commentary. That's the move that lifts a Criterion A response from surface to depth.

## Section 1.7 — Infographics

Data as design. Persuasion masquerading as neutrality. Never treat an infographic as "just facts".

### Structural features

- **Information hierarchy** — headline claim at top, supporting figures descending, source citation at bottom.
- **Iconography** — pictograms that translate abstract quantities into human units (one figure = 1000 people).
- **Chart choices** — pie for share of whole, bar for comparison, line for time trend. A *chosen* chart implies a preferred interpretation.
- **Colour coding** — semantic colour (red bad, green good) or thematic palette tied to the sponsor's brand.
- **Source citation** — grants credibility (or exposes bias). Check: WHO, government census, or advocacy group?
- **Framing devices** — axis truncation, cherry-picked time windows, misleading area scaling in bubble charts.

### Infographics are never neutral

The choice of which data to include, how to scale it, and what to leave out is authorial. A "just the facts" infographic from an advocacy NGO is still an argument. Treat it that way. Your analysis names both the data and the design decision behind its presentation.

### Writing about an infographic

Talk about the interaction between *data and design*. It's rarely enough to describe just the numbers or just the visuals. The mark-scoring move is showing how the visual choices shape how the data is received.

## Section 1.8 — Essay Structure and Time Plan

A thesis-driven, three-body, tightly evidenced piece of argument.

### The five-part structure

1. **Intro (5–6 lines)** — identify the text type + sponsor/publication, name the purpose & audience in one sentence, then state your thesis directly answering the guiding question. No throat-clearing, no "In this essay I will…".
2. **Body 1 — the big structural choice** — layout, image dominance, overall composition. Quote/refer to specific elements. Explain effect.
3. **Body 2 — language & tone** — diction, syntax, register, direct address. Micro-analysis of one or two loaded phrases.
4. **Body 3 — a third angle** — appeals, semiotics, cultural context, or a specific visual device. Pick the angle that gives you fresh material — do not repeat.
5. **Conclusion (3–4 lines)** — synthesise, do not summarise. Restate thesis in richer form; if space allows, gesture at what the text reveals about its cultural moment.

### Point → Evidence → Analysis → Link

Every body paragraph runs this four-move cycle. The `link` at the end brings the reader back to the guiding question — that's what earns Criterion C.

- **Point.** Topic sentence naming the authorial choice.
- **Evidence.** Direct reference: quote the copy, describe the visual detail specifically.
- **Analysis.** *How* the choice creates the effect — the mechanism.
- **Link.** Tie back to the thesis / guiding question in one sentence.

## Section 1.9 — Exam-Ready Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Guided textual analysis | The formal name for the SL Paper 1 task — analyse an unseen text with a guiding question. |
| Non-literary text | A text produced for a real-world purpose (ad, cartoon, PSA, infographic, speech) rather than as literary art. |
| Guiding question | The single question printed under the text; every paragraph must serve answering it. |
| Ethos, pathos, logos | Aristotle's three appeals: credibility, emotion, logic. |
| Kairos | The rhetorical use of timeliness — arguing at the right moment. |
| Register | Level of formality — colloquial, informal, neutral, formal, elevated. |
| Diction | The specific word choices an author makes. |
| Syntax | The arrangement of words into sentences. |
| Semiotics | Study of signs and symbols carrying cultural meaning. |
| Juxtaposition | Placing two elements side by side to reveal contrast or connection. |
| Connotation | The associations a word carries beyond its literal meaning. |
| Framing | How the author sets up what the reader notices and how they interpret it. |
| Foregrounding | Making one element stand out from the rest, drawing the reader's attention. |
| Visual hierarchy | The design order in which elements demand a viewer's attention. |
| Caricature | Exaggerated physical features that identify and often mock a public figure. |
| Call to action | An explicit instruction to the reader to do something. |

## Section 1.10 — Common Exam Traps

- **Feature-spotting.** Naming devices ("uses alliteration") without explaining effect. Zero marks under B.
- **Summarising the text.** Retelling what an ad says instead of analysing *how* it says it. Kills Criterion A.
- **Ignoring the guiding question.** Writing everything you notice, in no particular order. Wrecks Criterion C.
- **Treating infographics as neutral.** Not naming the persuasion. Ceiling on both A and B.
- **Modern-Indian bias.** Reading every text as if it were addressed to you. Consider the actual target audience — geographic, generational, class-coded.
- **Padding with theory names.** Dropping "Barthes says…" without a real point costs Criterion D through pretension.
- **Weak thesis.** "This ad is very persuasive" is not a thesis. A thesis argues a specific mechanism producing a specific effect on a specific audience.
- **Wrong text type identification.** Calling an infographic an "ad", or a PSA a "commercial ad", loses the whole framing.
- **Running out of time on paragraph 1.** A brilliant opening and no body is a 12/20.
