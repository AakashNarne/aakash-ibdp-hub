# English A SL — Assessed Work Mark Lessons

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - Build a thesis that answers the task and keep every paragraph anchored to it.
> - Use precise textual evidence, then interpret its implications; description and device-spotting are not analysis.
> - Explain how authorial choices create meaning and affect the audience/reader.
> - In Paper 2, compare the two works through specific choices and effects, not general statements about each work.
> - Keep the Paper 2 criterion-version caveat below in mind: the November 2025 sample pack includes revised commentaries for first assessment 2026.

## Paper 1: guided textual analysis

The additional Paper 1 examples in the Language A pack include a **SL Example B scored 17/20** (A 5/5, B 4/5, C 4/5, D 4/5). The examiner praises its nuanced interpretations of the text, well-chosen references, a clear focus on the guiding question, mostly sustained organization, and some insightful analysis of how features shape meaning. The analysis was not consistently insightful throughout, and register/style was not fully effective—strong but not perfect.

A lower-scoring SL Example C in the original sample set illustrates the traps: the student mostly understood literal meaning, ignored important visual/layout choices, shifted the guiding question to a less-supported purpose, and did not sustain focus. Some authorial choices were evaluated, but the focus was unclear, so the analysis was used inefficiently.

**Do:** state a defensible reading of the text; select precise verbal and visual evidence; explain the choice → meaning/effect → task connection; use a clear, purposeful structure.

**Do not:** retell the text, list devices, ignore layout or visual choices, make claims without references, change the guiding question to an interpretation the text does not support, or assume a structured introduction/body/conclusion alone makes the argument coherent.

## Paper 2: comparative essay

The revised additional samples A and B each scored **23/25** under the updated A/B1/B2/C/D criteria. Example A earned A 5/5, B1 5/5, B2 4/5, C 5/5, D 4/5; Example B earned A 4/5, B1 4/5, B2 5/5, C 5/5, D 5/5. The comments reward close knowledge of both works, interpretations tied to every part of the question, detailed analysis/evaluation of authorial choices, a clear and sustained line of reasoning, balanced treatment, and specific comparison of similarities/differences. The examples show that very high analysis does not require perfect language, and polished language cannot substitute for weak knowledge or comparison.

**Do:** define the key terms in the prompt, select two works that allow a relevant comparison, anchor points in specific scenes/choices, explain effects, and connect similarities/differences throughout the essay. Keep the balance and reasoning visible.

**Do not:** write two separate mini-essays with a token comparison at the end; generalize about an author or work without specifying where; use quotations haphazardly; mention a feature without contextualizing its effect; or let an interesting comparison drift away from the question.

> [!warning] Use the correct version of Paper 2 criteria
> The 2025 update says the Paper 2 examples A–F were revised so their commentaries reflect the criterion A point-value change and the division of criterion B into B1/B2 for first assessment 2026. Older A/B/C sample commentaries in the same booklet predate that revision. Treat their qualitative advice as transferable, but do not reuse their old point allocations as the current rubric.

## Common mistakes / examiner traps

- **Summary in place of interpretation:** move beyond what happens to implications and subtleties supported by the text.
- **Technique spotting:** identify a choice accurately and explain how it shapes meaning; an unexplained label is not analysis.
- **Unanchored claims:** integrate brief, precise references rather than making broad assertions or inserting quotations without explaining them.
- **Focus drift:** test every paragraph against the guiding question/essay prompt; a relevant fact can still be irrelevant to the task.
- **Imbalance and weak comparison:** give both works purposeful space and compare the authors’ choices and effects specifically.
- **Language as a substitute:** fluent, accurate writing helps, but high marks also require knowledge, analysis, focus and comparison.
