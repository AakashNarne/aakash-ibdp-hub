# English Paper 1 — News Article

## Purpose and conventions
A news article reports a recent event or development for a public readership. It commonly prioritizes key facts early, uses attribution, and provides context or detail later. Some articles depart from the inverted pyramid or include analysis, so describe the structure actually present. “News” does not mean entirely neutral: selection, order, wording, and sources frame events.

## Establish the report’s frame
Identify headline, subheading, byline, date, publication, opening lead, and any image/caption. Ask what the headline foregrounds, what the first paragraph treats as most important, and whose account is attributed. Distinguish reported fact, quotation, allegation, interpretation, and background information.

## Choices to analyse
- **Headline and lead:** selection of actor, action, consequence, and key facts establishes the angle. Compare the headline’s framing with the body’s fuller account.
- **Information order:** inverted pyramid, chronology, thematic grouping, or delayed revelation directs attention and affects urgency. Note which facts appear first and what is relegated.
- **Attribution and sourcing:** named officials, witnesses, experts, documents, anonymous sources, and institutions create different kinds of authority. Ask whose perspectives are included, quoted directly, paraphrased, or absent.
- **Language and tone:** verbs, labels, modality, passive voice, numbers, and quotation choices can create certainty, caution, urgency, or distance. Explain the effect without alleging bias unless the text supports it.
- **Quotations and voices:** who speaks first and last? Are voices balanced in length and prominence? Are statements challenged or contextualized?
- **Images and captions:** a photograph can humanize an event, dramatize it, or imply causality; captions anchor identification and context. Analyse the relationship with the headline and text.
- **Context and omissions:** background details help readers interpret an event. Notice missing context or affected groups only when the absence is relevant and demonstrable.

## Analytical approach
Build a claim about how the article frames the event. Support it with specific wording, order, sourcing, or image placement. Explain what readers are likely to know or consider as a result, and qualify claims about audience response. Evaluate clarity, credibility, and perspective separately: a clearly structured report can still provide limited viewpoints, while multiple sources do not automatically ensure fairness.

## Common traps
- Treating every article with a date and byline as wholly objective.
- Calling the report biased without evidence of framing or omission.
- Confusing an allegation in a quotation with an established fact.
- Summarizing the event rather than analysing how it is presented.
- Assuming the inverted pyramid is used when the structure shows otherwise.

## Exam checklist
What is the news angle? What is prioritized in headline and lead? How are claims attributed? Which voices appear or are missing? How do wording, order, and visuals shape the reader’s understanding? Have I stayed close to the text and prompt?
