# GP Rights & Justice 2 — Interactions — Notes

**Syllabus:** Thematic study *Rights and justice*, part 2, **Interactions of political stakeholders and actors**: the state and national governments; intergovernmental organizations; regional human rights tribunals; civil society organizations (including advocacy); marginalized, vulnerable or most affected groups; private companies and unions (Hodder p. 115). See [[_Syllabus Index]].
**Assessed in:** Paper 2 (15-mark essays) and Paper 1 (sources). The HL extension *Equality* and *Health* challenges draw on it too.

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - The **state is still the primary actor**: it makes the laws, runs the courts and decides whether outsiders get in. Every other actor works *through*, *around* or *against* it (Hodder p. 116).
> - **ICJ ≠ ICC.** The ICJ settles disputes *between states* (15 judges). The ICC tries *individuals* for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression (18 judges). Both run into **sovereignty**: no police force, so they depend on state compliance.
> - **Regional systems differ in teeth.** The European Court of Human Rights issues binding judgments that individuals can trigger. The African Court hears individuals only from states that opt in, and ASEAN and the OIC have no court at all (Hodder Table 2.14).
> - **NGOs have no vote but have information.** Amnesty and HRW research, publish and "name and shame". Their power is legitimacy and pressure, not enforcement.
> - **Companies and unions matter.** After **Rana Plaza (2013)**, brands and global unions signed a *binding* Accord, which is a case of non-state actors filling a gap the state left. The **UN Guiding Principles (2011)** set out who owes what: the state *protects*, business *respects*, victims get a *remedy*.

## 1. Why interactions matter

**Command terms:** *explain*, *examine*, *discuss*.

Hodder's opening point (p. 115): stakeholders "frequently interact with each other, as together they are more effective at bringing about change". The book's example chain runs like this:
1. A company mistreats workers and the local government does nothing.
2. The media and social media publicise the workers' complaints.
3. A workers'-rights NGO picks up the story.
4. The NGO pressures the government and political leaders to act.

**Stakeholders** here means any actor with an interest in the outcome: states, sub-national governments, IGOs, NGOs, companies, social and resistance movements, parties, pressure groups, leaders, the media and individuals (list at Hodder p. 115; definitions in [[Chapter 1: Foundations of Global Politics]]).

Link every interaction to the four key concepts:

| Key concept | The question to ask in any rights case |
|---|---|
| **Power** | Who can force compliance, and who can only persuade? |
| **Sovereignty** | Did the state consent to the court, treaty or monitor, or can it shut it out? |
| **Legitimacy** | Why do people accept a ruling or a report as authoritative (legal basis, neutrality, expertise)? |
| **Interdependence** | Which economic or diplomatic ties give outside actors leverage (supply chains, trade, aid)? |

> [!tip] Essay habit
> Never analyse one actor alone. The mark-scheme word is *interactions*. For every case, name **at least three actors** and say what each did to the others: pressured, referred, complied, ignored, funded.

## 2. The state and national governments

**Command terms:** *explain*, *evaluate*.

Hodder (p. 116) says the state "remains the primary actor in global politics" because it controls the laws, the judicial system and citizen welfare. Interdependence, though, "continues to challenge the supremacy of the state". According to the book, a state promotes rights when it:
- keeps the economic and political climate stable
- allows protest, free speech and full participation in government
- creates and enforces rights-promoting laws
- lets NGOs work and communicate openly with international human rights groups
- respects diversity (race, class, sexuality, gender identity)
- gives clear **access to justice** when rights are violated.

The state can also be the main **violator**. The same sovereignty that lets it protect rights lets it restrict them and keep scrutiny out. The two cases below show this.

> [!example] Case 1: Hong Kong National Security Law (2020): state vs civil society, media and outside criticism
> - The **NPC Standing Committee** in Beijing passed the law on **30 June 2020**, unanimously by **162** delegates. It was imposed through Annex III of the Basic Law and bypassed Hong Kong's own legislature ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Hong_Kong_national_security_law); [SCMP via HKSAR.org](https://hksar.org/hong-kong-adopts-national-security-law-with-life-in-jail-maximum-penalty)).
> - It created four offences, **secession, subversion, terrorism and collusion with foreign forces**, each carrying up to **life imprisonment** (same sources).
> - **Civil society:** the Hong Kong Alliance, which had run the Tiananmen vigil for 30 years, disbanded on **25 Sept 2021**. Its leaders were charged with "inciting subversion" ([Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2021/09/hong-kong-demise-of-vigil-group-shows-marking-tiananmen-crackdown-will-not-be-tolerated/)).
> - **Media:** Apple Daily founder **Jimmy Lai** was arrested for "collusion" on 10 Aug 2020 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Hong_Kong_national_security_law)). He was convicted on **15 Dec 2025** ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2025/dec/15/jimmy-lai-verdict-hong-kong-judges-national-security-charges)) and sentenced to **20 years** on **9 Feb 2026** ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/09/nx-s1-5706515/china-media-tycoon-jimmy-lai-sentenced-20-years-hong-kong)).
> - **Scale:** by 12 June 2025, 326 people had been arrested under the NSL and the 2024 Safeguarding National Security Ordinance, and 165 convicted (Security Bureau figures via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Hong_Kong_national_security_law)).
> - **External actors:** the UK, the EU and NATO issued statements, and the European Parliament passed resolutions demanding Lai's release (same source). They had little effect.
> - **Use it for:** sovereignty beating outside pressure. The UK and EU could *speak* but not *enforce*. A **realist** reads the outcome as power deciding it. A **liberal** sees the norm-shaming continuing and a record being built. Beijing frames it as security and non-interference, a relativist or sovereigntist argument (see [[GP Rights & Justice 1 — Contested Meanings — Notes]] §4).

> [!example] Case 2: Article 370 (India, 2019–24): state, courts, political parties and elections
> - On **5 Aug 2019** President Kovind issued **CO 272**, and on 6 Aug **CO 273** rendered Article 370 (Jammu and Kashmir's special status) inoperative. The **J&K Reorganisation Act 2019** split the state into two Union Territories, J&K and Ladakh ([SC Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/reports/abrogation-of-article-370-judgement-summary)), with effect from 31 Oct 2019 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Jammu_and_Kashmir_Legislative_Assembly_election)).
> - On **11 Dec 2023** a **five-judge Constitution Bench** led by CJI Chandrachud upheld the abrogation. It directed that statehood be restored "as soon as possible" and that assembly elections be held **before 30 Sept 2024**. Justice Kaul recommended a South Africa-style **truth and reconciliation commission** for human rights violations in J&K ([SC Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/reports/abrogation-of-article-370-judgement-summary)).
> - The elections ran in three phases (18 Sept, 25 Sept, 1 Oct 2024) and were counted on 8 Oct 2024. The National Conference–Congress alliance won and Omar Abdullah became Chief Minister ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Jammu_and_Kashmir_Legislative_Assembly_election); [NDTV](https://www.ndtv.com/india-news/jammu-and-kashmir-assembly-election-results-national-conference-congress-big-win-over-bjp-in-1st-j-k-poll-in-decade-6743990)).
> - **Use it for:** interactions *inside* the state. Executive, judiciary, parties and voters all shape the outcome.
>   - *Claim:* the court acted as a check by forcing a deadline for restoring **political rights** (elections).
>   - *Counterclaim:* the court declined to overturn a major executive action, and statehood was still not restored when it ordered elections. That is a legitimacy question.
>   - Link this back to *political justice* in Part 1.

## 3. IGOs: the UN courts and councils

**Command terms:** *distinguish*, *compare and contrast*, *evaluate*.

An **intergovernmental organization (IGO)** is a body of states that cooperate on shared interests. The UN is the most influential. Its rights machinery, per Hodder p. 116, is the **ICJ, ICC, UN Human Rights Council and UNHCR**.

> [!warning] Textbook wording
> Hodder calls the ICC a UN "organ". It isn't one. The ICC was set up by a separate treaty, the **Rome Statute**, and is independent of the UN, although the Security Council can refer cases to it (Hodder p. 118). The ICJ *is* a UN organ, "the principal judicial organ" ([ICJ](https://icj-web.leman.un-icc.cloud/sites/default/files/case-related/178/178-20200123-PRE-01-00-EN.pdf)). Hodder also writes "UN High *Commission* for Refugees". The correct name is **High Commissioner**.

### 3.1 ICJ vs ICC (Hodder pp. 117–118)

| | **International Court of Justice (ICJ)** | **International Criminal Court (ICC)** |
|---|---|---|
| Who is tried | **States only** | **Individuals**, including heads of state |
| Legal basis | UN Charter / ICJ Statute (principal judicial organ of the UN) | **Rome Statute 1998**, in force 2002 |
| Judges | 15, nine-year terms, elected by UNGA and UNSC | **18**, nine-year non-renewable terms, elected by the Assembly of States Parties ([ICC](https://www.icc-cpi.int/about/judicial-divisions)) |
| Jurisdiction | Disputes states consent to. Per Hodder, fewer than half of UN members accept compulsory jurisdiction. | Genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes (after 1 July 2002). Aggression was activated from **17 July 2018** ([ICC](https://www.icc-cpi.int/about/how-the-court-works)). |
| How cases start | State v state | Referral by a state party; UNSC referral; the prosecutor acting on outside information |
| Principle | Rulings are binding, but enforcement goes to the UNSC | **Complementarity**: the ICC acts only if national courts are unable or unwilling |
| Victims | No direct role | Can participate and claim **reparations** |

**Limits (Hodder pp. 117–118):** consent-based jurisdiction; cases take years; no guarantee of compliance; non-compliance goes to the UNSC, where a P5 **veto** can block enforcement.

> [!example] Case 3: ICJ, *DRC v Uganda*: justice, but slowly (Hodder Table 2.10)
> - The DRC filed in **June 1999** over Uganda's armed activities in 1998–2003. The Court found Uganda liable in **Dec 2005**, but the parties could not agree on reparations ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2022/02/1111612)).
> - On **9 Feb 2022** the ICJ fixed compensation at **USD 325 m**: USD 225 m for damage to persons (loss of life, rape, child soldiers, displacement), USD 40 m for property and USD 60 m for natural resources (gold, diamonds, timber). It is payable in **five annual instalments of USD 65 m** from Sept 2022 (same source; 225 + 40 + 60 = 325 and 5 × 65 = 325 were both checked).
> - **Use it for:** the ICJ *can* deliver concrete remedies. But **23 years** from filing to the compensation figure illustrates Hodder's "court cases can take a long time" point.

> [!example] Case 4: ICJ, *The Gambia v Myanmar*: a small state and an IGO act for the Rohingya
> - Hodder's Rohingya case (p. 122) describes the 2017 exodus to Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh.
> - The legal follow-up was **The Gambia**, backed by the **OIC**, filing a Genocide Convention case on **11 Nov 2019**. On **23 Jan 2020** the ICJ **unanimously** ordered provisional measures: Myanmar must prevent genocidal acts, preserve evidence and report to the Court every six months ([ICJ press release](https://icj-web.leman.un-icc.cloud/sites/default/files/case-related/178/178-20200123-PRE-01-00-EN.pdf)). The UN Fact-Finding Mission's findings fed into the case (same source).
> - **Use it for:** an interaction chain of *victim group → UN investigators → IGO (OIC) → small state → ICJ*. It shows the ICJ being used for human rights even though only states can bring cases.

> [!example] ICC in action: arrest warrant for Putin
> On **17 March 2023** ICC Pre-Trial Chamber II issued arrest warrants for **Vladimir Putin** and Maria Lvova-Belova over the unlawful deportation of Ukrainian children ([ICC](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/situation-ukraine-icc-judges-issue-arrest-warrants-against-vladimir-vladimirovich-putin-and)). It demonstrates Hodder's point that "heads of states" can face the court. It also demonstrates the limit: Russia is not a party, and the ICC has no police, so arrest depends on other states.

### 3.2 UN Human Rights Council (Hodder pp. 119–120)
- Passes **resolutions**, which can call for investigations and experts and bring in other UN bodies. They are **not legally binding**. NGOs are sometimes invited to give their view.
- Runs the **Universal Periodic Review (UPR)**, a review of every state's human rights record.
- Focus areas (Table 2.11): civil space and democracy; development and dignity (including MNC responsibilities); equality and non-discrimination; justice and rule of law; peace and security.
- **Limits:**
  - Rights-abusing members. Freedom House calls this "foxes in the henhouse".
  - Resolutions are non-binding, so states can ignore them.

### 3.3 UNHCR (Hodder pp. 120–123)
- Set up by the UNGA in **1950** to protect refugees, internally displaced people (IDPs) and stateless people. The 1951 Refugee Convention underpins its mandate.
- Table 2.12 definitions:
  - **Refugee**: fleeing conflict or persecution across a border.
  - **IDP**: seeking safety *inside* their own country.
  - **Asylum seeker**: seeking international protection whose refugee status has not yet been decided.
  - **Migrant**: moving for other reasons (work, education).
- **Statelessness** (p. 123). Causes: discriminatory nationality laws (e.g. women unable to pass on nationality), children of undocumented migrants, revocation, lost papers. Only states can grant nationality, which is sovereignty again.
- **Limits:** donor funding has not kept up with need, and the UNHCR can operate in a country only **with permission**. States may restrict its work or demand favours.

> [!warning] Textbook slip
> Hodder p. 123 heads the UNHCR limits section "Limitations of the UN Human Rights Council". The content is about the **UNHCR** (refugees), not the UNHRC. Don't mix them up in an exam.

## 4. Regional human rights tribunals

**Command terms:** *compare*, *to what extent*.

Regional systems try to fit human rights protection to local values. They are the textbook's test of whether regionalism answers the **cultural relativist** charge that rights are "too Western" (review question, p. 134).

| Organization | Instrument (year) | Binding? (Hodder Table 2.14) | Court? | Who can bring a case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Council of Europe** | European Convention on Human Rights (signed 4 Nov 1950, in force 3 Sept 1953) | Yes | **European Court of Human Rights**, Strasbourg | States **and individuals** |
| **OAS** | American Convention on Human Rights (1969) | No (per Hodder; see warning) | Inter-American Court | Only the Commission or states. Individuals and NGOs go to the Commission first. |
| **African Union** | African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (1981) | No (per Hodder: states can enter reservations) | African Court | Individuals and NGOs **only** against states that make an Art. 34(6) declaration |
| **OIC** | Cairo Declaration (1990): rights grounded in Sharia | No | None | — |
| **ASEAN** | ASEAN Human Rights Declaration (18 Nov 2012) | No | None (the intergovernmental commission meets twice a year) | — |

> [!warning] Three corrections to the textbook (verify before you quote Hodder)
> 1. **The Council of Europe is not the EU** and did not "evolve into" it (Hodder p. 124). The ECHR has **46 member states of the Council of Europe** ([CoE](https://www.coe.int/en/web/portal/46-members-states)), not "46 states within the EU". The EU has 27 members.
> 2. **The African Court:** Hodder's "eight of the 34 member states" is really **34 states that have ratified the Court Protocol**, of which only **8** have made the Art. 34(6) declaration letting individuals and NGOs sue. The AU itself has **55** members ([African Court](https://www.african-court.org/wpafc/basic-information/)).
> 3. **Inter-American Court:** Hodder calls its decisions "non-binding" and its column says "No". But Art. 68 of the American Convention says states parties "undertake to comply with the judgment of the Court in any case to which they are parties" ([OAS treaty text](https://www.oas.org/dil/treaties_b-32_american_convention_on_human_rights.htm)). Its *advisory opinions* are non-binding. Its contentious judgments bind states that have accepted its jurisdiction.

> [!example] Case 5: *Hirst v UK (No 2)*: a strong regional court meets a sovereign parliament
> - John Hirst was serving life for manslaughter and challenged the UK's **blanket ban** on convicted prisoners voting. UK courts said it was a matter for Parliament ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-7461/CBP-7461.pdf)).
> - On **6 Oct 2005** the ECtHR Grand Chamber found a violation of **Art. 3 of Protocol No. 1** (the right to free elections) by **12 votes to 5**. The ban affected about 48,000 prisoners, and Parliament had never weighed its proportionality ([HUDOC case summary](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=002-3706)). The majority called the ban "general, automatic and indiscriminate" (Commons Library, above).
> - The UK Parliament resisted for years, and the Committee of Ministers issued repeated calls, including an interim resolution in Dec 2015 (Commons Library). Minor administrative changes followed (prisoners on temporary licence or home-detention curfew). The **Committee of Ministers closed supervision on 6 Dec 2018** (CM/ResDH(2018)467) ([UK Constitutional Law Association](https://ukconstitutionallaw.org/2019/01/30/elizabeth-adams-prisoners-voting-rights-case-closed)).
> - **Use it for:** even the *strongest* regional system depends on political will. The judgment was binding and could be triggered by an individual, but compliance took **13 years** and was minimal. Good for "to what extent can regional tribunals protect rights?" Weigh it against the ECtHR's general high compliance.

## 5. Civil society, NGOs and advocacy

**Command terms:** *explain*, *evaluate the effectiveness*.

**Civil society organizations (CSOs)** are groups people form voluntarily around a common goal, including NGOs and community groups. Hodder (p. 126) says their **legitimacy** depends on being **non-profit, independent of government and without commercial affiliation**. **Advocacy** means publicising a problem as widely as possible (social media, letters, petitions, marches) to pressure those with power to change it.

| | **Amnesty International** | **Human Rights Watch** | **Child Rights International Network (CRIN)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founded | 1961 (political prisoners) | 1978 | 1990, responding to the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child |
| Nobel Peace Prize | 1977 | 1997 (landmines campaign) | — |
| Methods | Researchers, then evidence shared with governments and IGOs; letter-writing, petitions; annual *State of the World's Human Rights* report ("name and shame") | Undercover field research and interviews; reports, video, social media; concentrates on crises | Monitoring, public campaigns, challenging violators (e.g. its UK campaign against recruiting under-18s into the military) |
| UN link | Special consultative status since 1964: can advise, **cannot vote** | Works alongside Amnesty and accepts UDHR definitions | Campaigns grounded in UNCRC articles |
| Funding | Individual donations (claims over 10 m members) | Private donations | — |

Hodder's review question (p. 134) asks why Amnesty and HRW both insist rights are **universal**. The answer: their legitimacy rests on neutrality and a single UDHR standard applied to *every* state. Accepting relativism would let any state excuse abuses.

**Marginalized and vulnerable groups** (pp. 130–131). **Marginalization** is when people are treated as "different" because of their social identity (race, gender, sexuality, nationality, class, economic status) and denied rights and access to justice. **Vulnerability** can also come from age, addiction, pregnancy, statelessness or childhood. Hodder's example is **Maina Kiai**, a Kenyan lawyer, UNHRC adviser and HRW partnerships lead, who calls the LGBTQ+ movement one of the most successful recent social movements and urges others to learn from it.

## 6. Private companies and unions

**Command terms:** *explain*, *discuss*, *evaluate*.

Hodder (pp. 131–133):
- Workers are vulnerable because of the **unbalanced power** between employer and employee.
- **Marxists** argue profit-driven firms will never put workers first, and they distrust the UN as elitist.
- Figure 2.28 sets out a triangle of duties:
  - The **state** makes and enforces protective law.
  - The **employer** prevents abuse and acts quickly when it happens.
  - The **employee** must know that help can come from state agencies and NGOs.
- Zeid Ra'ad Al Hussein, a former UN High Commissioner for Human Rights: "Business cannot thrive in failing societies…"
- The **ILO** core principles are freedom of association and collective bargaining, and no forced labour, child labour or discrimination, plus a safe working environment. The **WTO** endorses a narrower set of "core" standards.
- A **union** is a democratic workers' collective that negotiates through **collective bargaining** (wages, safety, benefits). Some firms oppose unions.

> [!example] Case 6: UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs), 2011
> - The UN Human Rights Council **endorsed** them in **resolution 17/4 of 16 June 2011**. They were developed by the UN Secretary-General's Special Representative on human rights and transnational corporations ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/documents/publications/guidingprinciplesbusinesshr_en.pdf); [OHCHR press release](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2011/06/new-guiding-principles-business-and-human-rights-endorsed-un-human-rights)).
> - There are **three pillars**, the "Protect, Respect and Remedy" framework:
>   1. the **state duty to protect** against abuse by third parties, including business
>   2. the **corporate responsibility to respect** human rights through due diligence, meaning identifying, preventing and mitigating impacts, including through supply-chain "business relationships"
>   3. **access to remedy** for victims (judicial and non-judicial, including company grievance mechanisms) (same source).
> - **Use it for:** a textbook "interaction" document, where an IGO sets norms for states *and* firms. **Limitation:** it is *soft law*. Companies have a "responsibility", not a legal duty.

> [!example] Case 7: Rana Plaza (Bangladesh, 2013): companies, unions, IGO and state
> - On **24 April 2013** the Rana Plaza garment factory building near Dhaka collapsed, killing **more than 1,130** workers. The ILO gives **1,138**, with more than 2,500 injured ([ILO 2021](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/rana-plaza-more-needs-be-done-ensure-workplace-safety); [ILO 10th anniversary](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/occupational-safety-and-health-key-achieve-social-justice-says-ilo)).
> - **Companies + unions:** the **Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh** was signed on **15 May 2013** by more than 200 brands and retailers and two global unions (IndustriALL and UNI). It was a *legally binding* deal with independent inspections, worker training, a complaints mechanism and the right to refuse unsafe work. It was renewed in **Aug 2021** as the **International Accord**, extended to general health and safety and human rights due diligence, with around 150 brands signed up ([ILO Better Work/CBSD](https://cbsd.ilo.org/cbsd_initiatives/international-accord-for-health-and-safety-in-the-textile-and-garment-industry-formerly-known-as-the-accord-on-fire-and-building-safety-in-bangladesh)).
> - **IGO + state:** about **3,780** exporting factories were inspected through a government-led initiative (ILO-supported), the Accord and the Alliance. **39** were closed at once as imminently dangerous ([ILO InfoStory](https://webapps.ilo.org/infostories/en-GB/Stories/Country-Focus/rana-plaza.html)).
> - **Remedy:** the ILO-chaired Rana Plaza Coordination Committee met its **USD 30 m** compensation target in **June 2015** ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2015/06/501132)).
> - **Use it for:** the best single example of the UNGPs' three pillars working together. The **state** failed to protect. **Brands** accepted responsibility, under union and NGO pressure. **Victims** got a remedy through an IGO-run scheme. *Counterclaim:* action came only after a disaster, and compensation relied on voluntary donations. The **Marxist** reading is that capital moves only when its reputation is at stake.

> [!example] Case 8: ITUC Global Rights Index 2022 (Hodder p. 133 cites it)
> The International Trade Union Confederation surveyed **148 countries**. It found:
> - **77%** excluded workers from the right to form or join a union
> - **74%** (110 of 148) impeded union registration
> - **41%** restricted free speech and assembly
> - trade unionists were murdered in **13 countries**, including post-coup Myanmar ([ITUC executive summary](https://www.ituc-csi.org/IMG/pdf/2022_ituc_rights_index_exec_summ_en.pdf)).
>
> This is the evidence behind Hodder's claim that workers' rights are "increasingly threatened". Link it to Hodder's COVID-19 discussion point.

## 7. Paper 2 coaching: essay angles

Structure: define terms, give a thesis, then claims and counterclaims each with a named case (see [[Chapter 1: Foundations of Global Politics#Paper 2 — the essay structure]]).

> [!tip] Q1 — "To what extent is the state still the most important actor in protecting rights and justice?"
> - **Claim:** yes. The Hong Kong NSL shows a state overriding IGO, NGO and foreign pressure. Only states grant nationality (statelessness). The UNHCR needs permission to operate.
> - **Counterclaim:** non-state actors can fill gaps. Rana Plaza produced a binding brand–union Accord. The ECtHR can be triggered by individuals (*Hirst*).
> - **Evaluation:** even the counterclaims loop back to states. *Hirst* compliance was minimal, and the Accord needed Bangladesh's cooperation. Conclude with "primary but not sole". Realism and liberalism both fit here.

> [!tip] Q2 — "Evaluate the effectiveness of international courts in upholding rights."
> - **Claim:** *DRC v Uganda* (USD 325 m awarded); *Gambia v Myanmar* (unanimous provisional measures); the ICC Putin warrant.
> - **Counterclaim:** consent, delay (23 years), no enforcement, the UNSC veto, and a warrant that cannot be executed.
> - **Concepts:** legitimacy (courts' authority) against power (enforcement).

> [!tip] Q3 — "Regional human rights systems are more effective than global ones. Discuss."
> - **Claim:** the ECtHR is binding and open to individuals. Regional systems can reflect regional values (Hodder's relativism question).
> - **Counterclaim:** only 8 AU states allow individual petitions to the African Court. ASEAN and the OIC have no court. Even Europe had *Hirst*.

> [!tip] Q4 — "Private companies bear as much responsibility for rights as states. Discuss."
> - Use the UNGPs' pillars, Rana Plaza, the ILO and WTO core standards, and ITUC 2022.
> - Counter with the Marxist critique and soft-law limits.

## 8. Common mistakes / examiner traps

1. **ICJ vs ICC.** ICJ = states. ICC = individuals. Hodder flags this as the classic confusion (p. 118).
2. **Calling the ICC a UN organ.** It is a treaty-based, independent court with a link to the UNSC.
3. **Council of Europe ≠ EU.** The ECtHR belongs to the Council of Europe (46 states). Hodder's p. 124 wording is wrong.
4. **UNHRC ≠ UNHCR.** The Human Rights *Council* passes resolutions and runs the UPR. The High Commissioner for *Refugees* protects displaced people.
5. **"Binding" does not mean "enforced".** ICJ and ECtHR rulings are binding, yet *Hirst* took 13 years. Always separate legal force from actual compliance.
6. **Treating NGOs as powerless.** Their power is informational and normative: reports, shaming, consultative status. Evaluate it on those terms.
7. **Listing actors instead of showing interactions.** Show the chain: who pressured, referred or funded whom.
8. **Loose figures.** Rana Plaza: the ILO says 1,138 dead. *DRC v Uganda*: USD 325 m. *Hirst*: 12–5. The African Court: 8 of 34 accept individual petitions.

## 9. Self-test

1. Distinguish the ICJ from the ICC by who is tried, jurisdiction and one recent case each.
2. Explain the three pillars of the UN Guiding Principles and apply them to Rana Plaza.
3. Using Hodder's Table 2.14 and one verified correction, compare the European and African regional systems.
4. What does *Hirst v UK (No 2)* show about the limits of binding regional courts?
5. Explain how Amnesty International and HRW influence states despite having no vote, and why both defend universalism.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. **ICJ**: states only; disputes the parties consent to; 15 judges; example *DRC v Uganda*, USD 325 m in reparations (9 Feb 2022), or *Gambia v Myanmar*, unanimous provisional measures (23 Jan 2020). **ICC**: individuals; genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes (after 2002) and aggression (activated 17 July 2018); complementarity; 18 judges; example the arrest warrant for Putin (17 Mar 2023).
> 2. **Protect:** the state must prevent abuse by business. Bangladesh failed to enforce building safety. **Respect:** companies must do due diligence. Brands signed the binding Accord (15 May 2013) with unions. **Remedy:** victims need redress. The ILO-chaired committee raised USD 30 m in compensation (June 2015).
> 3. **Europe (Council of Europe):** binding ECHR; ECtHR open to individuals; 46 CoE states. It is *not* the EU, which is the textbook error. **Africa (AU):** the African Charter allows reservations; the African Court protocol has 34 ratifications but only **8** Art. 34(6) declarations, so most Africans cannot petition it directly. That gives it weaker reach and more sovereignty retained.
> 4. The Grand Chamber found the UK's blanket prisoner-voting ban violated A3P1 (12–5, 6 Oct 2005). Parliament resisted, and the Committee of Ministers closed the case only on 6 Dec 2018 after minimal administrative changes. Binding does not mean enforced: sovereignty and political will decide compliance.
> 5. They research violations, publish reports (Amnesty's annual report) and "name and shame". They mobilise members through petitions and protests. Amnesty uses its UN consultative status (since 1964) to advise, and HRW does undercover fieldwork. Their power rests on **legitimacy**: independence, non-profit status, and a single UDHR standard applied to all states. Relativism would let states excuse abuses.
