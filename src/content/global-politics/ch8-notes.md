# GP Rights & Justice 3 — Nature, Practice and Study — Notes

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - A promise on paper is not the same as a remedy: law, institutions, monitoring and state cooperation determine what people can actually claim.
> - A declaration states a shared standard; a ratified treaty creates obligations for its parties, while an international complaint route may require a separate state consent.
> - Shared standards can spread through treaties, institutions, public evidence and advocacy, but their meaning and implementation remain contested.
> - Match the response to the violation: domestic courts, treaty bodies, regional mechanisms, international courts and collective action have different powers and limits.

**Scope and order:** This note follows Hodder 2.3: codification; protection and monitoring; international and regional frameworks; world norms including R2P; international humanitarian law; responses to violations and perceived injustices. Material from 2.4 is excluded.

## 1. Codification: domestic and international law

**Command terms:** *define* and *explain* require a precise meaning and mechanism; *distinguish* separates legal form from implementation; *evaluate* weighs enforceability against sovereignty and compliance.

**Codification** is the process of putting rights into a formal legal framework so that they can be recognized and, where the instrument is binding, enforced through law. Hodder distinguishes domestic codification through constitutions and laws/judicial systems from international codification through treaties, conventions and covenants (ch. 2.3.1).

### 1.1 Constitutions and national law

A **constitution** is a state's fundamental set of rules: it allocates public power and may guarantee specific rights. **Constitutional interpretation** is how courts determine what those rules mean in a case; interpretations can shift, so codification is not a guarantee that a right will be understood or protected permanently. Ordinary statutes, police and courts also make rights enforceable in daily life. States remain central because they legislate, investigate, adjudicate and provide access to justice; **sovereignty** means they retain authority over their territory and population and may resist outside scrutiny.

> [!example] Case: *Roe v. Wade* and *Dobbs* — changing constitutional interpretation
> The US Supreme Court decided *Roe v. Wade* on 22 January 1973 and held that the Constitution protected a qualified right to terminate a pregnancy. On 24 June 2022, *Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization* held that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion and overruled *Roe* and *Casey*.[19][20] Use the sequence to show how courts can protect a claimed right through constitutional interpretation, yet later courts can reverse that interpretation.

### 1.2 The UN and international rights frameworks

The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)** is the UN's common standard of human rights, proclaimed by the General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948 as Resolution 217 A (III). It is a declaration, not a ratified treaty: in Hodder's account it is aspirational and not legally binding in itself. Its influence is that later treaties and national frameworks turn shared principles into more specific obligations.[1]

A **treaty** is a formal agreement between states governed by international law. **Signature** and **ratification** are not interchangeable: signature records a state's initial endorsement, while ratification is its formal consent to be bound under the treaty's terms. Always check the UN Treaty Collection for the particular state's status, any reservations and the date the treaty entered into force for that state. The following entry-into-force dates are for the instruments generally, not a claim that every state is a party.

| Instrument | Entry into force | Main focus in this syllabus |
|---|---:|---|
| International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) | 23 March 1976 | Civil and political rights, including legal protections and freedoms.[2] |
| International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) | 3 January 1976 | Economic, social and cultural rights, including work, health and education.[3] |
| Convention against Torture (CAT) | 26 June 1987 | Prohibiting torture and requiring prevention and accountability; Article 3 addresses transfer to a state where there are substantial grounds for believing that a person would be in danger of torture.[4][5] |
| Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) | 3 September 1981 | State obligations to eliminate discrimination against women.[21] |

Treaty obligations can make rights legally binding on participating states, but codification alone does not guarantee implementation. The treaty text, a state's party status, reservations, reporting duties and acceptance of any complaint procedure all matter. Hodder's evaluation is therefore balanced: treaties create standards and routes for scrutiny, but consequences may be limited and sovereignty can obstruct compliance.

## 2. Protection and monitoring

**Command terms:** *explain* how a mechanism works; *examine* what evidence it uses; *evaluate* its reach, independence and ability to secure compliance.

**Protection** means action to prevent violations, stop them or provide a remedy. **Monitoring** means gathering and reviewing information to assess whether rights are respected. Neither follows automatically from codification.

### 2.1 State and domestic mechanisms

States can protect rights by enforcing constitutional and statutory guarantees, maintaining impartial courts and police, creating or supporting a national human rights commission, and ensuring people can seek redress. Hodder gives two broad functions for national commissions: rights education and handling grievances or investigating alleged violations. Their effectiveness depends on independence, capacity, access for affected groups and whether officials act on findings.

### 2.2 UN and treaty-based monitoring

The UN Human Rights Council was established by General Assembly Resolution 60/251 on 15 March 2006. It promotes human rights and conducts the **Universal Periodic Review (UPR)**, a peer review of each UN Member State's human-rights record. Reviews began in 2008; OHCHR describes the process as reviewing all 193 Member States every 4.5 years. The UPR is cooperative and gives states and other participants a forum for recommendations, not a court judgment.[6][7]

UN treaty bodies monitor implementation of the treaties they oversee. Their work can include review of state reports, concluding observations and follow-up on selected recommendations. Some committees can also receive individual communications, state-to-state complaints or conduct inquiries, but these routes do not apply identically to every treaty. An individual complaint generally requires the state to have accepted the relevant procedure and the complainant to have exhausted domestic remedies, unless a recognized exception applies.[8][9]

Other monitoring actors include regional organizations, national institutions, civil society and NGOs. Hodder lists NGO strategies such as education, monitoring, investigation, documentation, advocacy and lobbying. These actors can gather evidence and pressure authorities, but they cannot substitute for state law or guarantee that recommendations will be implemented. Regional frameworks add another level of treaty protection and review; see [[GP Rights & Justice 2 — Interactions — Notes]] for the regional courts and stakeholder roles.

> [!example] Monitoring example: OHCHR assessment of Xinjiang, 31 August 2022
> OHCHR's assessment of the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region said that the extent of arbitrary and discriminatory detention of Uyghurs and other predominantly Muslim groups, within wider restrictions on rights, “may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity.” This is an official monitoring assessment, not a court verdict or a conviction; keep the report's qualified wording.[16]

## 3. Development of world norms and R2P

**Command terms:** *explain* how a norm develops; *discuss* competing actors and explanations; *to what extent* weighs norm change against sovereignty and actual implementation.

A **world norm** is a broadly shared understanding of how people should be treated. Hodder links norm development to interdependence and globalization: ideas travel through international and regional organizations, treaties, civil society and media, and standards can evolve. A treaty can reinforce a norm, but lasting change also depends on domestic law, monitoring and social conditions. The textbook's gender-equality example is CEDAW; it cautions that the relationship between ratification and improvements in women's lives is not automatic or linear.[21]

Social media can rapidly circulate evidence of alleged violations, make marginalized groups more visible and attract public or institutional attention. Treat circulation as a means of awareness and pressure, not as proof by itself; claims still need verification.

### 3.1 Responsibility to Protect

The **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)** is the 2005 World Summit political commitment that each state has the primary responsibility to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity. The international community should assist states and, if peaceful means are inadequate and national authorities manifestly fail, may take timely collective action through the Security Council in accordance with the UN Charter. R2P is not a free-standing legal permission to use force.[10]

Remember two linked teaching structures without conflating them:

| R2P pillars | What each means |
|---|---|
| Pillar 1 | The state protects its population from the four atrocity crimes. |
| Pillar 2 | The international community assists states to meet that responsibility. |
| Pillar 3 | If a state manifestly fails, collective action may be considered through the UN and under the Charter, case by case.[10] |

Hodder also describes responsibilities to **prevent**, **react** and **rebuild**. Prevention addresses causes and risks; reaction uses appropriate measures, with force only as an extreme last resort; rebuilding supports recovery and reconciliation after a crisis or intervention. **Humanitarian intervention** specifically means the use of military force for a humanitarian protective aim. It is narrower than R2P as a whole and can itself cause death and destruction.

> [!example] UN-authorized response: Libya, 17 March 2011
> Security Council Resolution 1973 (2011) authorized notified Member States to take “all necessary measures” to protect civilians and civilian-populated areas under threat of attack in Libya, while excluding a foreign occupation force.[11] Use this as an example of a Security Council-authorized response in the R2P era, not as evidence that R2P independently authorizes force.

## 4. International humanitarian law

**Command terms:** *define* the legal field, *explain* its purpose and *apply* the relevant protection to a conflict example.

**International humanitarian law (IHL)** is the body of rules for armed conflict that seeks to limit harm and protect people who are not fighting or can no longer fight. The four Geneva Conventions were adopted on 12 August 1949. Their common Article 3 provides minimum protections for victims of non-international armed conflicts; IHL is not confined to wars between states.[17]

> [!warning] Correct the textbook date and scope
> Hodder's extract says “Geneva Conventions of 1940”; the correct year is **1949**. Its suggestion that IHL is limited to interstate conflict is too broad: common Article 3 covers minimum protection in non-international armed conflict as well.[17]

Hodder's examples of later instruments include the 1972 Biological Weapons Convention, 1977 Additional Protocols, 1993 Chemical Weapons Convention, 1997 Ottawa Convention on anti-personnel mines and 2000 Optional Protocol on children in armed conflict. Use these as examples of rules being developed as weaponry and protection concerns change; do not claim that a treaty alone ensures compliance.

## 5. Responses to violations and perceived injustices

**Command terms:** *analyse* which actors and mechanisms respond; *evaluate* effectiveness and limitations; *discuss* alternative responses and their consequences.

Hodder's Table 2.17 spans several kinds of violation. Start by identifying the right at stake, the responsible actor and the route capable of addressing it. The mechanisms in this chapter are complementary, not interchangeable.

| Violation or perceived injustice | Rights or harm at stake in Hodder's account | Possible response route |
|---|---|---|
| Child soldiers | Children are recruited or used in armed groups and can lose safety, education and healthy development. | Domestic child-protection systems, IHL and relevant international monitoring; use rehabilitation and reintegration measures where applicable. |
| Human trafficking and forced labour | Coercion, exploitation, threats, debt bondage or confiscated identity papers undermine liberty and dignity. | Criminal and labour law, access to justice, independent investigation and victim support. |
| Forced relocation | People are compelled to leave homes to escape conflict, violence, rights violations or disasters. | Domestic remedies and protection, with international or humanitarian assistance where relevant. |
| Denial of prisoner-of-war rights | Captured combatants may face torture, deprivation or death despite humane-treatment rules. | IHL monitoring and accountability under the applicable conflict framework. |
| Violations of freedom of speech | Silencing journalists, activists or NGOs restricts expression and participation in civil society. | Courts and commissions, public documentation, advocacy and UN monitoring. |
| Rights restricted in the name of counter-terrorism | States may curtail expression, association, protection from torture and other rights. | Constitutional safeguards, independent review, treaty monitoring and remedies; CAT Article 3 is relevant where transfer could expose a person to torture.[5] |
| Gender discrimination | Unequal treatment can limit access to rights and justice. | Domestic equality law, CEDAW commitments, monitoring and advocacy.[21] |

> [!example] Worked case: *The Gambia v. Myanmar* at the ICJ
> On 23 January 2020, the International Court of Justice indicated provisional measures in the case brought by The Gambia under the Genocide Convention to protect rights claimed for the Rohingya. Myanmar was required to take measures to prevent acts within the Convention, preserve evidence and report to the Court within four months, then every six months pending a final decision.[12][13] This shows a state-to-state court route producing interim legal directions and monitoring deadlines; it does not make the ICJ a criminal court for individuals.

> [!example] ICC response: Sudan, 4 March 2009
> The International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Sudanese President Omar Al Bashir for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity. The warrant listed seven counts: five crimes against humanity and two war crimes. A warrant is an accusation and request for arrest, not a conviction.[14]

> [!example] ICC response: Ukraine, 17 March 2023
> The ICC issued arrest warrants for Vladimir Putin and Maria Lvova-Belova over alleged war crimes involving the unlawful deportation and transfer of children from occupied areas of Ukraine to Russia. The ICC said the alleged conduct dated from at least 24 February 2022. The status remains an allegation in a warrant, not a finding of guilt.[15]

For an essay, compare what each mechanism can do: a domestic court interprets national law; a treaty body monitors state obligations; the UPR reviews every state's record; the ICJ hears disputes between states within its jurisdiction; the ICC prosecutes individuals within its jurisdiction and complements, rather than replaces, national courts[18]; and R2P addresses only the four atrocity crimes through UN Charter processes. Ask whether a response is legally binding, whether the state accepted the mechanism, what remedy it can deliver and who can enforce it.

## 6. Common mistakes / examiner traps

1. **Calling the UDHR a binding treaty.** It is a declaration; treaty obligations arise from the treaty and a state's participation in it.
2. **Treating codification as proof of compliance.** A right in a constitution or treaty still needs domestic implementation, monitoring, remedy and political will.
3. **Confusing signature with ratification.** Check the treaty depositary record and the state's specific status; optional complaint routes can require additional consent.
4. **Calling UPR recommendations court judgments.** The UPR is a cooperative peer-review mechanism, not a court.
5. **Treating the ICJ and ICC as interchangeable.** The ICJ hears state disputes; the ICC prosecutes individuals. An arrest warrant is not a conviction.
6. **Treating R2P as a general right to intervene.** Its scope is the four atrocity crimes; collective action must operate through the UN Charter framework.
7. **Restricting IHL to interstate wars or dating the Geneva Conventions to 1940.** Use 1949 and remember common Article 3's minimum protection in non-international armed conflict.
8. **Treating an OHCHR assessment as a judicial finding.** Preserve the exact qualified language, such as “may constitute,” and distinguish monitoring from adjudication.
9. **Listing mechanisms without evaluating them.** State the authority, access rules, cooperation required, remedy and enforcement limit for each.

## 7. Self-test

1. Define codification and distinguish the legal status of the UDHR from a ratified treaty.
2. Explain how the UPR and treaty bodies monitor states, including one condition on an individual complaint.
3. Describe the three R2P pillars and use the 2011 Libya resolution to show how collective action is constrained.
4. Define IHL, state the correct year of the Geneva Conventions and explain why common Article 3 matters.
5. Compare the ICJ order in *The Gambia v. Myanmar* with one ICC response, giving the date and distinguishing an order or warrant from a conviction.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. **Codification** puts rights in a formal legal framework. The UDHR is a non-binding declaration proclaimed on 10 December 1948; a treaty binds participating states under its terms after it enters into force for them. The ICCPR entered into force on 23 March 1976 and the ICESCR on 3 January 1976.[1][2][3]
> 2. The UPR is a peer review of every UN Member State's record every 4.5 years. Treaty bodies review reports and issue observations; some have individual-complaint procedures only where the state accepted that procedure. Domestic remedies generally must be exhausted unless an exception applies.[7][8][9]
> 3. Pillar 1 is the state's duty to protect; Pillar 2 is international assistance; Pillar 3 is timely collective action through the UN, under the Charter and case by case, if peaceful means are inadequate and the state manifestly fails. Resolution 1973 on 17 March 2011 authorized notified Member States to take all necessary measures to protect Libyan civilians, while excluding a foreign occupation force.[10][11]
> 4. IHL regulates conduct in armed conflict and limits harm. The Geneva Conventions were adopted on 12 August 1949. Common Article 3 supplies minimum protection for victims of non-international armed conflict.[17]
> 5. On 23 January 2020 the ICJ ordered provisional measures in *The Gambia v. Myanmar*, including prevention of Convention acts, preservation of evidence and periodic reporting; it is a state-to-state case, not an individual criminal trial.[12][13] An ICC example is the 17 March 2023 Ukraine warrants for Putin and Lvova-Belova over alleged crimes. A warrant records an accusation, not guilt.[15]
