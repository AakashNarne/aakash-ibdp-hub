# Global Politics HL — Foundations and Exam Framework

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - The current course studies **power and global politics** through a shared Core, three compulsory thematic studies and, at HL, **global political challenges**.
> - Use the four key concepts—**power, sovereignty, legitimacy and interdependence**—as analytical tools, not labels to drop into an essay.
> - Explain who the actors are, what they want, how they exercise influence, whose perspective is represented and what evidence supports your claim.
> - For essays, answer the exact question, develop relevant evidence, compare perspectives and reach a qualified judgement; a rigid paragraph-count formula is not a substitute for analysis.
> - The new guide is first assessment 2026. Old course labels and old assessment structures in legacy notes may not apply.

## 2. The four key concepts

| Concept | Working definition | Ask in a case study |
|---|---|---|
| **Power** | The capacity to influence actors, decisions, outcomes or the conditions in which choices are made. | Who influences whom, through what mechanism, and with what result or resistance? |
| **Sovereignty** | A state’s authority to govern its territory and population and its independence in external affairs. | Which dimension—internal, external, de jure or de facto—is being exercised or challenged? |
| **Legitimacy** | The acceptance or justification of an actor’s authority or action by a relevant audience. | Who considers the authority or action acceptable, on what grounds, and who contests it? |
| **Interdependence** | Mutual reliance among actors, states, organizations or regions. | What is exchanged or relied on, how asymmetric is the relationship, and who can absorb the cost of disruption? |

These concepts overlap but are not interchangeable. For instance, an actor may have the **power** to make a decision, lack **legitimacy** in the eyes of an affected group, exercise authority under a claim of **sovereignty**, and depend on other actors through **interdependence**. Choose the concepts that explain the case instead of listing all four automatically.

## 3. Actors and perspectives

**Political actors** are individuals or groups that participate in, influence or are affected by political processes. Depending on the issue, relevant actors may include:

- states, national governments and political leaders;
- subnational and local governments;
- intergovernmental organizations (IGOs);
- organized civil society, NGOs and social or resistance movements;
- private companies, including multinational and transnational corporations;
- political parties, interest and pressure groups;
- formal and informal political forums, the media, and affected communities or individuals.

Do not force every actor type into every response. Identify the actors that matter to the question. Distinguish an actor’s **stated position** from its interests, capacity, actions and the effects on others; a state, NGO or community is not necessarily internally unified.

A **perspective** is a way of interpreting a political issue, shaped by an actor’s interests, values, experience, power and assumptions. Compare perspectives using evidence: what does each actor claim, what evidence or interests support that claim, and whose experience is missing? [[GP — Theoretical Perspectives — Notes]] gives the main theoretical lenses; [[GP Core — Framing and Stakeholders — Notes]] expands the actor taxonomy.

## 4. A practical analysis routine

For a source question, case study or essay paragraph:

1. **Answer the prompt.** Identify the key command term and define only the concepts that matter.
2. **Make a claim.** State a contestable point that directly addresses the question.
3. **Use specific evidence.** Give the actor, place, date and relevant event or figure; cite the source where appropriate.
4. **Explain the mechanism.** Show how the action or relationship produces the claimed political effect.
5. **Bring in another perspective.** Compare a counterclaim, affected group, alternative interpretation or theoretical lens where relevant.
6. **Evaluate and link back.** Weigh the evidence, limits and context, then state how far the paragraph supports your answer.

This is a flexible reasoning sequence, not a mandatory six-sentence paragraph template. The teacher’s GP BASICS handout suggests claims and counterclaims as a useful planning habit; it does not mean that every question requires a fixed number of each. Follow the particular question and assessment rubric.

### Example: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights

The UN General Assembly adopted the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights on 10 December 1948** by **48 votes in favour and eight abstentions**.[1] This gives a concrete starting point for evaluating universal standards: the vote shows broad support but not unanimity. A strong analysis would ask which states abstained, how different actors interpret universality, and how adoption differs from later implementation. The case can connect rights and justice with legitimacy, power and interdependence; it does not by itself prove that every state accepted or enforces every right. See [[GP Rights & Justice 1 — Contested Meanings — Notes]] for the detailed account and sources.

## 5. Assessment overview

The teacher’s course pack and current school syllabus describe the following HL assessment formats. Use the current teacher instructions for timing and component rules.

| Component | What it assesses | Practical preparation |
|---|---|---|
| **Paper 1** | Source-based questions: understanding a source, applying knowledge, comparing/contrasting sources, and evaluating/synthesizing sources with own knowledge. The pack gives 3-, 4-, 6- and 12-mark questions (25 marks total). | Practise concise source reading, direct comparison and evidence synthesis. Use sources as evidence, not decoration. |
| **Paper 2** | Two extended responses on the thematic studies: one from Section A and one integrating question from Section B (30 marks total; two 15-mark essays). | Build a direct thesis, developed evidence and balanced evaluation. Select cases for relevance rather than memorizing a single “perfect” case per theme. |
| **Paper 3 (HL)** | Stimulus-based questions connected to the HL global political challenges extension; the pack gives 3-, 4-, 6- and 15-mark questions (28 marks total). | Prepare connected case studies covering at least two challenge areas, and practise applying them to the stimulus and prompt. |
| **Engagement Project** | A locally or community grounded political issue studied through active engagement and complementary research. HL students add a separate recommendation of no more than 400 words to the report. | Follow the current guide and school deadlines; keep engagement authentic, ethical and documented. |

## 6. Building and maintaining case studies

A useful case-study record contains:

- **Issue and timeframe:** what happened, where and when?
- **Actors:** who made decisions, who influenced them, and who was affected?
- **Evidence:** one or more dated facts, figures, legal texts or documented statements from reliable sources.
- **Mechanism:** how did power, sovereignty, legitimacy or interdependence operate?
- **Perspectives:** what are the competing claims, interests and interpretations?
- **Evaluation:** what changed, what did not, who benefited or bore costs, and what remains uncertain?
- **Transfer:** which other course topics or challenge areas can the case illuminate?

A case is not evidence merely because it is familiar. Verify dates, figures and claims; distinguish a source’s report from your own inference; and update information that can change. The detailed GP notes linked above provide topic-specific cases and limits.

## 7. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Using old course labels or official-looking decimal codes | Check the first-assessment-2026 guide and the current [[_Syllabus Index]]. |
| Name-dropping concepts or theories without explaining them | Show the mechanism and connect it to the evidence and question. |
| Treating a government, NGO or population as one unified voice | Identify relevant internal differences and affected groups. |
| Using a case fact without a date, source or link to the argument | Verify the fact and explain what it demonstrates—and what it does not. |
| Writing a preset number of claims/counterclaims regardless of the prompt | Let the command term, question scope and evidence determine the structure. |
| Claiming that a policy, intervention or institution “worked” because it was announced | Define the intended objective and assess implementation and outcomes. |
| Treating a Paper 2 response as a list of facts or a one-sided opinion | Analyse competing perspectives and reach a justified, contextual judgement. |
| Applying a Paper 1 source summary where comparison or synthesis is required | Respond to the exact task and make explicit use of the required sources. |
| Leaving source-to-question links implied, even when the source and own example are relevant | State the analytical link directly; the Paper 1 soft-power sample lost a mark where the difficulty of measurement was only inferable. See [[GP Notes(School)#What the assessed-work samples show]]. |
| Comparing sources only in a generic final paragraph, or failing to name every required source | Build a running, point-by-point comparison with specific details and explicitly name/use the required number of sources. In the higher-scoring Paper 1 sample, explicit reference to only two sources cost credit where three were required. |
| Giving examples without developing how they prove the claim | Explain the example's mechanism and relevance; the examiner commentary criticized underdeveloped examples even when they were relevant. |
| Treating balanced description as evaluation | Explicitly assess evidence limits, bias and context, then make a supported judgement; the Brazil Engagement Project commentary identified these as areas for improvement. |

## 8. Self-test

1. Name the three parts of the current course and identify what is additional at HL.
2. Define the four key concepts and give one question each concept helps you ask about a case.
3. Name four relevant actor categories and explain why not every answer needs all of them.
4. What are the main skills assessed by Papers 1, 2 and 3?
5. Give two reasons a familiar case study is not automatically strong evidence in an essay.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. The shared Core, three compulsory thematic studies (Rights and justice; Development and sustainability; Peace and conflict), and—at HL—the global political challenges extension.
> 2. **Power:** capacity to influence actors, decisions or conditions. **Sovereignty:** state authority and external independence. **Legitimacy:** acceptance or justification of authority/action by an audience. **Interdependence:** mutual reliance. Apply each by asking who influences, who governs, who accepts, and who depends on whom.
> 3. Examples: states/governments, IGOs, NGOs/civil society, companies, movements, political leaders, local authorities, media or affected communities. Include the actors that are relevant to the question and evidence; forced lists can distract from analysis.
> 4. Paper 1 tests source understanding, application, comparison/contrast and synthesis/evaluation; Paper 2 tests thematic and integrative essays; Paper 3 (HL) tests application of the HL extension to stimulus material.
> 5. A case may be outdated or insufficiently sourced; it may not match the question; one fact may not show causation or policy success; or it may lack competing perspectives and acknowledged limits.
