## Section 3.1 — Defining Sovereignty

**Working definition** (IB-aligned). Sovereignty characterises a state's **independence**, its **control over territory**, and its **ability to govern itself** without external interference. It is the foundational principle of the modern international system.

### The Westphalian origin

Modern state sovereignty is traditionally traced to the **Peace of Westphalia (1648)**, which ended the Thirty Years' War. Two treaties (Osnabrück and Münster) established a European order based on:

1. **Territorial integrity** — each state has supreme authority within its borders.
2. **Non-interference** — no state should meddle in another's internal affairs (particularly on religious matters, in the original context).
3. **Legal equality of states** — regardless of size, all sovereign states are formally equal under international law.
4. **The state as the basic unit** of the international system.

**Historical nuance.** The claim that "Westphalia invented sovereignty" is contested. Scholars like Andreas Osiander argue the actual 1648 treaties did not enshrine sovereignty as we now understand it — that framing was projected back later. Nonetheless, "Westphalian sovereignty" is the standard IB shorthand for the traditional model, and you should use it.

### The state's defining criteria — Montevideo Convention (1933)

Under international law, a state is defined by four criteria:

1. A **permanent population**.
2. A **defined territory**.
3. A **government** capable of exercising authority.
4. The **capacity to enter into relations** with other states.

Not all sovereignty-claimants meet all four cleanly. Palestine has (1) and (2) contested; the Kurdish nation has (1) but no (2); "failed states" like Somalia have all four on paper but weak (3) in practice.

### Weber's monopoly of legitimate violence

Max Weber's classic definition of the state (1919): a state is a **"human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory."** Where that monopoly breaks down — active insurgencies, warlord control, contested authority — sovereignty exists only nominally.

## Section 3.2 — Sources of Sovereignty

An actor claiming sovereignty typically draws on some combination of five sources:

| Source | What it grounds sovereignty in | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Legitimacy** (internal) | Consent of the governed — democratic mandate, tradition, effective performance | Elected government of India; Bhutan's constitutional monarchy |
| **International recognition** | Acceptance by other states as an equal actor | UN membership; embassies exchanged; treaty participation |
| **Effective control** (Weberian) | Monopoly on legitimate violence within the territory | Contrast Somaliland (effective control but not recognised) with the Somali Federal Government (recognised but limited control) |
| **Constitutional / legal foundation** | Codified rules that define and constrain the state | Written constitutions; supreme court authority |
| **Popular sovereignty** | The idea that ultimate authority derives from the PEOPLE, not the state | Rousseau's social contract; Preamble to the Indian Constitution ("We, the people…") |

**Analytical move.** In a case study, name *which* sources of sovereignty are contested. Palestine has partial international recognition (~140+ UN members) and popular sovereignty (self-identification of Palestinians as a nation) but weak effective control in the West Bank and Gaza. That mix is the exam-ready insight.

## Section 3.3 — Internal and External Dimensions

Sovereignty has two dimensions that operate somewhat independently.

### Internal sovereignty

**Supreme authority within a territory** — the ability to make and enforce law over one's own population.

Requires:
- A functioning government with reach across the territory.
- Monopoly (or near-monopoly) on legitimate force.
- Capacity to collect taxes, deliver public services, enforce contracts.

**Where internal sovereignty is thin:** parts of Somalia (Al-Shabaab controls territory), Yemen (Houthi vs government split), pre-2021 Afghanistan (Kabul had legal sovereignty; Taliban had shadow control across large rural areas), Myanmar (ongoing civil war with ethnic armed organisations controlling border regions).

### External sovereignty

**Independence from other states** — the recognition by other sovereigns that this state's domestic decisions are its own; the right to conduct foreign relations as an equal.

Requires:
- Diplomatic recognition (usually via UN membership, or bilateral recognition).
- Freedom from foreign occupation or interference.
- Signatory status in international treaties.

**Where external sovereignty is contested:** Taiwan (de facto sovereign but recognised by only ~12 states); Palestine (recognised by ~140+ but blocked from full UN membership by US veto); Kosovo (recognised by ~100 states, not by Russia, China, Serbia); Somaliland (unrecognised despite three decades of stable governance); Western Sahara.

### The two can come apart

- **Strong internal, weak external**: North Korea has near-total internal control but is diplomatically isolated (though it holds UN membership).
- **Strong external, weak internal**: Somalia is recognised at the UN but the federal government controls only Mogadishu and shifting areas.
- **Weak on both**: Failed / fragile states — parts of Yemen, Libya, Central African Republic.
- **Strong on both**: The "normal" sovereign state — most UN member states most of the time.

## Section 3.4 — De Jure vs De Facto Sovereignty

- **De jure sovereignty** — sovereignty in law. Recognised by other states, member of international bodies, signatory to treaties.
- **De facto sovereignty** — sovereignty in practice. Actually controls the territory and exercises authority, regardless of legal recognition.

The clearest cases sit in one of two mismatches:

| Case | De jure | De facto |
|---|---|---|
| **Taiwan (ROC)** | Recognised by ~12 states; excluded from UN | Fully self-governing since 1949; own military, currency, democratic elections |
| **Palestine** | Recognised by ~140+ states; UN Observer State | Contested control — PA in parts of West Bank; Hamas in Gaza; both under Israeli constraint |
| **Somaliland** | Not recognised by any UN member state | Stable, elected government controlling defined territory since 1991 |
| **Kosovo** | Recognised by ~100 states; not by Russia, China, Serbia, ~90 others | Fully self-governing since 2008 declaration |
| **Northern Cyprus (TRNC)** | Recognised only by Turkey | Self-governing since 1974 |

Note the *asymmetry*: de facto sovereignty is usually built up over years of effective governance; de jure recognition depends on the strategic calculations of other states (especially P5 members whose veto can block UN entry).

## Section 3.5 — The Central Debate: Is Sovereignty Being Eroded?

The most-tested question about sovereignty in IB Global Politics. Every essay should be able to argue both sides.

### The erosion argument — sovereignty is weakening

- **Borderless trade and finance.** States cannot control capital flows the way they once did (2008 GFC exposed how synchronised financial systems override national fiscal choices; 2022 Russia sanctions demonstrated dollar structural power constraining sovereignty).
- **Digital communication.** Encrypted messaging, cross-border data flows, and cyber warfare bypass state control. The Snowden 2013 revelations showed US surveillance reached deep into allies' communications; Chinese platforms similarly extend PRC reach into diasporas.
- **Supranational institutions.** EU membership requires ceding legislative sovereignty in whole domains (single market, currency for eurozone, migration under Schengen). WTO rulings can override national trade policy. ICC jurisdiction (in principle) reaches heads of state.
- **Human rights regime.** ICC warrants against sitting leaders (Putin 2023, Netanyahu 2024) test whether international law bites through Westphalian shielding.
- **Climate accords.** Paris Agreement (2015) requires domestic policy commitments; failure has international consequences.
- **R2P (Responsibility to Protect).** 2005 UN doctrine: if a state cannot or will not protect its own population from mass atrocity, the international community may intervene. Direct erosion of non-interference.
- **MNC power.** Companies can arbitrage between jurisdictions (tax, labour, environmental regulation). Apple's tax structure, Google's data centres, Facebook's content moderation all reduce practical sovereignty even where legal sovereignty is intact.
- **Non-state actors and networks.** ISIS controlled territory; transnational advocacy networks shape policy; global finance moves faster than regulators.

### The persistence argument — sovereignty remains robust

- **States still control borders.** Trump's border wall; EU's post-2015 migration securitisation (Frontex, deals with Turkey and Libya); India's Citizenship Amendment Act; UK's post-Brexit points-based system.
- **Great powers act unilaterally when it serves them.** US withdrawal from Paris Accord (2017), UNHRC, WHO, INF Treaty. Russia's rejection of ICC jurisdiction. China's rejection of the 2016 South China Sea arbitration award.
- **BRICS+ and Global South pushback** against Western-designed institutions is itself a sovereignty reassertion — countries are pooling into alternative frameworks that reflect their own priorities.
- **COVID-19 revealed** that when it matters, states close borders, hoard vaccines, and prioritise national interest — even inside the EU where free movement is meant to be sacrosanct.
- **Digital sovereignty push.** EU's GDPR, DMA, DSA; India's IT Rules and Digital India Act; China's Great Firewall — states asserting authority over their digital environments.
- **Recent territorial reassertions.** India's Article 370 abrogation (2019) — reassertion of Indian sovereignty over Kashmir's autonomous status. Russia's Crimea annexation (2014) and Feb 2022 Ukraine invasion — brutal Westphalian reassertion.
- **Data localisation laws** (India, China, Russia, EU) — sovereignty being re-imposed on cross-border data flows that seemed to have escaped it.

### The correct exam framing

**Sovereignty isn't binary — it exists on a spectrum, and different dimensions erode at different rates.** North Korea has extreme sovereignty (isolated, unbound by external norms). EU members have pooled sovereignty. Failed or fragile states (Somalia, parts of Yemen) have degraded sovereignty despite legal recognition. Digital sovereignty is being *rebuilt* in the 2020s after two decades of erosion by global tech platforms.

The correct answer to "is sovereignty being eroded?" is: *it depends on which dimension, in which context, for which state.*

## Section 3.6 — Pooled Sovereignty: The EU Case

The European Union is the paradigmatic case of *voluntary* sovereignty pooling — a distinctive third way between full sovereignty and none.

### What pooled sovereignty means

Member states have transferred specific authority to supranational institutions:

- **Single market and competition policy** — European Commission enforces; EU law overrides national law in these domains.
- **Monetary policy (eurozone 20 states)** — ECB sets interest rates; individual states no longer print their own money.
- **Trade policy** — EU negotiates external trade agreements as a bloc; individual states cannot.
- **Free movement (Schengen area)** — border checks removed among 27 members.
- **Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP)** — coordinated (though not unified) foreign policy; unanimity often required.

### The trade-off

- **What states give up.** Some legislative sovereignty in specified domains. Ability to conduct trade independently. Ability to set monetary policy (in the euro area).
- **What states gain.** Access to a market of 450 million; collective bargaining power vs US, China; economies of scale in regulation and defence; peace among historical enemies (France-Germany).

### The 2026 test: is the EU pooling MORE or LESS sovereignty?

**MORE pooling in 2026:**
- **Joint defence borrowing** and EU-owned defence-industrial-base initiatives (post-Draghi report, Trump 2.0 retrenchment).
- **Digital sovereignty package** (DSA, DMA, AI Act) — EU acting as a bloc against US tech giants.
- **Migration compact** (New Pact on Migration and Asylum, 2024).

**Less pooling / renationalisation:**
- **Hungary and Slovakia** frequently block CFSP decisions on Ukraine.
- **Rule-of-law disputes** — EU-Hungary, EU-Poland (pre-2023) tension over judicial independence.
- **Border reintroductions** during COVID and migration crises.

The EU illustrates that pooling is a *choice*, and members can push back against it or accelerate it depending on strategic conditions.

### Brexit as the counter-example

The UK's 2016 referendum and 2020 departure was framed by advocates as **"taking back sovereignty"** — reversing the pooling trade-off. Constitutional consequences: UK regained trade-policy independence and freedom from ECJ jurisdiction, at the cost of single-market access, freedom of movement, and Northern Ireland arrangements that complicated the Good Friday Agreement.

Brexit shows sovereignty pooling is *reversible* — but at significant cost. The dynamic is asymmetric: pooling is slow and consensual; unpooling is disruptive and mostly one-directional (few states have followed).

## Section 3.7 — Contested Sovereignty: Case Bank

The IB rewards case studies with dates, actors, and concept-linkage. Master these six cases and you have coverage for almost any sovereignty question.

### 3.7.1 Taiwan / Republic of China

**Status.** De facto sovereign since 1949 (Nationalists retreated from mainland after CCP victory). De jure recognition: ~12 states (2026); excluded from UN since 1971 when PRC took the "China" seat.

**PRC position.** Taiwan is a renegade province of the People's Republic; reunification is the historic mission. Uses the "One China Principle" to force other states to choose between recognising Beijing OR Taipei — mostly Beijing wins.

**US position.** "One China Policy" — acknowledges the PRC position, does not endorse it. Taiwan Relations Act (1979) commits US to provide defensive arms. Strategic ambiguity: neither confirms nor denies whether it would defend Taiwan.

**2026 context.** Fourth Taiwan Strait Crisis dynamics; PLA drills, cyber operations, and economic pressure escalating short of invasion. Lai Ching-te (DPP) administration since 2024, taking a firmer pro-independence line while managing US alliance. TSMC dependency gives Taiwan structural leverage that no other unrecognised state has.

**Concept linkage.** Textbook case of de facto vs de jure divergence; recognition politics; structural power (TSMC); and hard/soft/smart power all in one place.

### 3.7.2 Palestine

**Status.** Recognised by ~140+ UN member states (majority of humanity by population). UN Observer State (2012). Full UN membership blocked repeatedly by US veto in the Security Council. De facto authority split: PA (Fatah) in parts of West Bank; Hamas in Gaza (weakened by post-Oct 2023 war); all under Israeli constraint.

**Israeli position.** Two-state solution is contested politically inside Israel; the current government's practical policy has moved toward consolidation of West Bank settlements.

**2024–26 developments.**
- Oct 7, 2023 Hamas attack; ensuing Gaza war.
- Nov 2024 ICC arrest warrants against Netanyahu and Gallant for war crimes (see 3.9.2).
- International Court of Justice (ICJ) case brought by South Africa alleging genocide; provisional measures issued.
- Sept 2025: several Western states (France, UK, Canada, Australia among them at various points) moved toward or completed formal recognition of Palestinian statehood.

**Concept linkage.** Recognition politics; internal vs external sovereignty; sovereignty vs occupation; power (US structural power via UNSC veto).

### 3.7.3 Kashmir

**Status.** Territorially disputed between India, Pakistan, and China since 1947 partition. India controls ~55% (J&K + Ladakh), Pakistan ~35% (Azad Kashmir + Gilgit-Baltistan), China ~15% (Aksai Chin).

**Key inflection: Article 370 abrogation (5 August 2019).** India revoked Jammu & Kashmir's special constitutional status; bifurcated the state into two Union Territories (J&K and Ladakh). Framed as internal sovereignty reassertion.

**Concept linkage.** Sovereignty reassertion at cost of local autonomy; internal vs external sovereignty (India argues internal, Pakistan externalises via UN); contested from below (Kashmiri self-determination claims) and from outside (Pakistan's UN campaigns).

### 3.7.4 Kurdish region

**Status.** ~30 million Kurds spread across Turkey, Iraq, Syria, Iran. No sovereign Kurdish state. Iraqi Kurdistan Regional Government (KRG) has substantial autonomy; the 2017 independence referendum was rejected by Baghdad. Rojava (northeast Syria) held by SDF/YPG until Turkish and post-Assad pressures diminished it in 2024–25.

**Concept linkage.** Statelessness despite national identity; autonomy vs sovereignty; the persistence of nations without states; regional power politics constraining self-determination.

### 3.7.5 Kosovo

**Status.** Declared independence from Serbia on 17 Feb 2008. Recognised by ~100 states (US, most EU, Japan). Not recognised by Serbia, Russia, China, and ~90 others. Excluded from UN by Russia/China veto threats. ICJ Advisory Opinion (2010) held that the declaration did not violate international law — but the political stalemate remained.

**Concept linkage.** Recognition politics; role of P5 vetoes in blocking UN admission; sovereignty as recognition-dependent; secession vs territorial integrity clash.

### 3.7.6 Ukraine — the current test

**Status.** Sovereign state since 1991. Territorial integrity violated by Russia in 2014 (Crimea annexation, Donbas war) and Feb 2022 (full-scale invasion). Under Russian occupation as of 2026: much of Luhansk and Donetsk, parts of Zaporizhzhia and Kherson, all of Crimea.

**2026 context.** Multiple 2026 truces (April, May); September 2026 US envoys shuttling Kyiv-Moscow negotiating a Trump peace proposal. Central sovereignty question in any peace deal: what happens to territory Russia now controls? What security guarantees for Ukraine's remaining sovereignty?

**Concept linkage.** The most brutal live case of Westphalian sovereignty violation; R2P debates (why didn't the international community stop it?); pooled sovereignty (EU's rapid embrace of Ukraine); ICC as international-law response.

## Section 3.8 — Modern Challenges to Sovereignty

Beyond the classical erosion factors, the 2020s have added new dimensions.

### Digital sovereignty

- **Data as territory.** Where data is stored, whose laws govern it, who can access it. Data localisation laws (India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, China's Cybersecurity Law, Russia's data-localisation regime) all try to re-territorialise data.
- **Platform sovereignty.** When a foreign platform (X, Meta, TikTok) shapes domestic political speech, who has sovereign authority over it? EU's DSA (2022) makes platforms legally accountable inside the EU. US TikTok ban debate is a mirror image.
- **AI sovereignty.** Which state controls AI models, training data, and compute matters for future sovereign power. EU AI Act (2024); US chip export controls; India's push for indigenous AI models.

### Health sovereignty

- **COVID-19 revealed** that pandemic response is both an intensely national (border closures, vaccine hoarding) and an intensely international problem.
- **WHO reform debate** — is a stronger international health authority a helpful pooling or an erosion of sovereignty? Pandemic Treaty negotiations (2022–24) illustrate the tension.

### Climate sovereignty

- **Paris Agreement (2015)** — NDCs (nationally determined contributions) are voluntary but internationally reviewed. Sovereignty formally preserved; social/reputational pressure real.
- **Carbon border adjustment mechanisms** (EU CBAM, 2023–) extend one state's climate policy across others' exports — a novel structural power move over sovereignty.
- **Loss and damage funds** (COP28, 2023) — a normative shift attributing responsibility across borders.

### Financial sovereignty

- **Sanctions weaponisation.** Post-2022, actors have realised how vulnerable holding dollar-denominated reserves makes them. Dedollarisation push (see Ch2 Power §2.3) is a sovereignty-reclamation project as much as an economic one.
- **CBDCs (Central Bank Digital Currencies)** — Chinese digital yuan (e-CNY) piloted extensively; over 130 central banks exploring CBDCs by 2026. A tool to bolster monetary sovereignty in a dollar-dominant world.

### Migration and border sovereignty

- **The border wall was 20th-century sovereignty. Fibre-optic cables, migrant boats, and drones are 21st-century sovereignty.**
- Post-2015 EU turn toward externalising borders (Turkey deal, Libya funding, Rwanda-UK deal proposals) is *how* sovereignty is reasserted in an interdependent world.

## Section 3.9 — Live 2026 Case Studies

Six cases to master by the SA1 date.

### 3.9.1 Russia-Ukraine: the sovereignty crisis of the era

**What happened.** Feb 2022 invasion; ongoing occupation of ~18% of Ukrainian territory. Multiple truces in 2026 (April, May) followed by attacks. September 2026: US envoys in Kyiv and Moscow negotiating a Trump peace proposal; fighting continues in parallel with talks.

**Sovereignty concepts engaged.**
- **Textbook Westphalian violation** — territorial integrity of a UN member state overrun by another UN member state, both P5-adjacent.
- **Recognition politics** — Russia's staged 2022 "referendums" in Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson to annex; near-total international non-recognition (only handful of states endorsed).
- **International-law response** — ICJ provisional measures (March 2022) ordering Russia to halt; ICC arrest warrant for Putin (March 2023).
- **EU response** — Ukraine granted EU candidate status (June 2022); accession process accelerated. A form of *sovereignty extension* via institutional integration.
- **The 2026 peace-deal sovereignty question** — any settlement negotiated over Ukraine's head raises sovereignty questions: can a great-power deal ratify occupation? Are US "security guarantees" a substitute for territorial recovery?

**Three-lens summary.**
- *Realist*: sovereignty is respected only when backed by power; Russia acted because Ukraine's sovereignty was not credibly guaranteed.
- *Liberal*: institutions responded (UNGA condemnation by 141 states in March 2022; ICC warrant; sanctions coordination); norms are being defended even where they fail to prevent.
- *Constructivist*: the war has reshaped Ukrainian sovereignty as a symbol (Zelensky's "we are here" refrain); German identity (Zeitenwende); Finnish-Swedish neutrality (ended).

### 3.9.2 ICC arrest warrants: the sovereignty–jurisdiction test

**The warrants.**
- **March 2023** — Vladimir Putin, for the alleged unlawful deportation of Ukrainian children.
- **November 2024** — Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant, for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity in Gaza. Also Hamas military leader Mohammed Deif (killed by IDF prior to warrant issue).

**Why they matter for sovereignty.** The Rome Statute (2002) established the ICC with jurisdiction over war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide, and (later) aggression — reaching sitting heads of state. **This is the sharpest live erosion of Westphalian sovereignty** in current politics: no head of state is immune from prosecution, at least in principle.

**State responses — the reality check.**
- **South Africa (BRICS summit, August 2023)** invited Putin, then arm-wrestled internally, ultimately invoked its own Diplomatic Immunities and Privileges Act to grant immunity — creating a legal fig-leaf for non-arrest despite ICC obligation.
- **Mongolia (September 2024)** hosted Putin without arresting; ICC issued a formal finding of non-compliance.
- **Hungary (April 2025)** hosted Netanyahu without arresting and announced withdrawal from the ICC.
- **US** — never a party to Rome Statute; has actively sanctioned ICC prosecutors under the Trump administrations.
- **Israel** — not a Rome Statute party; challenged the Court's jurisdiction; ICC Pre-Trial Chamber I rejected Israel's challenges and confirmed the warrants stand.

**Concept linkage.** Textbook power–sovereignty–legitimacy triangle. Structural power of a norm-setting IGO meets Westphalian sovereignty; states' responses illustrate the *conditional* strength of international law when great-power interests intrude. Also a live case for a Paper 2 question on "the limits of international law."

### 3.9.3 Taiwan: sovereignty in permanent liminality

**2026 dynamics.** Lai Ching-te's DPP administration continues a firmer pro-independence tilt while managing the US alliance under Trump 2.0's more transactional stance. PLA drills escalating short of invasion; grey-zone coercion (cyber, economic pressure on individual Taiwanese firms, disinformation) intensifying. TSMC's structural indispensability to the global chip supply gives Taiwan unusual leverage — a small state whose disappearance would trigger a global economic crisis.

**Concept linkage.** De facto vs de jure sovereignty in its clearest form. Recognition politics (US strategic ambiguity is itself a sovereignty position). Structural power (TSMC as sovereignty insurance).

### 3.9.4 Gaza and Palestine — sovereignty under war

**Sept 2025 wave of recognition.** Following the Oct 2023–2024 Gaza war, a wave of Western states moved to recognise Palestinian statehood — France, UK, Canada, Australia among them at various points. This is symbolic sovereignty extension in the absence of effective control; whether it changes anything materially depends on Israeli and US responses.

**Israeli sovereignty consolidation.** Continued West Bank settlement expansion under successive Netanyahu governments erodes any territorial basis for a future Palestinian state, even as international recognition rises.

**Concept linkage.** Recognition can outrun effective control; sovereignty and occupation coexist; international law bites unevenly.

### 3.9.5 EU strategic autonomy — sovereignty pooling accelerates

**Trigger.** Trump 2.0's retrenchment from NATO commitments and Ukraine support catalysed a decisive shift.

**2026 developments.**
- **Joint defence borrowing** and EU-owned defence-industrial-base initiatives.
- Draghi report (Sept 2024) provided the diagnostic; 2026 sees implementation.
- **European Sky Shield** expanded; Ukrainian defence innovation absorbed into EU industrial base.
- Ongoing tensions with Hungary and Slovakia over CFSP consensus, but bloc majority pushing ahead.

**Concept linkage.** *More* pooled sovereignty in a new domain (defence). A rational sovereignty *trade* — states surrender some autonomy to gain collective capacity in a domain where individual sovereignty would be inadequate.

### 3.9.6 Digital sovereignty — the state strikes back

**Multiple fronts in 2024–26.**
- **EU** — DSA and DMA enforcement generating first major fines and content-moderation obligations on US platforms. AI Act (2024) reaching extraterritorially.
- **India** — Digital India Act framework, IT Rules 2021 amendments, ongoing data-localisation push.
- **China** — Great Firewall persists; TikTok/Douyin split; PIPL (Personal Information Protection Law).
- **US** — TikTok divest-or-ban legislation (signed 2024, protracted implementation); ongoing chip export controls with clear sovereignty rationale.

**Concept linkage.** After two decades of digital sovereignty erosion, states are *reasserting* jurisdiction. Confirms the "spectrum, not binary" view: sovereignty is being rebuilt in one domain even as it erodes in others.

## Section 3.10 — Sovereignty Through Three Theoretical Lenses

### Case: The ICC warrants (Putin 2023, Netanyahu 2024)

| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Realism** | International law is what powerful states allow. Putin will never see The Hague because Russia is powerful; Netanyahu will never see The Hague because the US shields Israel. The ICC exists to prosecute the weak. |
| **Liberalism** | The warrants matter even if unenforced — they constrain travel, shape legitimacy, ratchet up normative pressure. The Rome Statute expanded international jurisdiction over sitting heads of state, a genuine sovereignty transformation. Over decades norms deepen. |
| **Constructivism** | The warrants change how we *think* about heads of state — from immune sovereigns to accountable individuals. That norm shift matters over generations even without individual prosecutions. |

### Case: The EU's 2026 defence pooling

| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Realism** | Rational balancing against US unreliability. Sovereignty pooling is a *self-help* move when unilateral capability is insufficient. |
| **Liberalism** | Deepening institutions in a new domain. Pooled sovereignty as the paradigm liberal success extends further. |
| **Constructivism** | The idea of "European sovereignty" (a phrase Macron has pushed for years) is being institutionalised. Identity is running ahead of hardware. |

## Section 3.11 — The Power–Sovereignty Interface

Sovereignty is the concept **most tightly linked with power** in exam questions. Anticipate the linkage:

- Every act of **hard power** by one state against another (invasion, sanctions, cyber) is a **violation or contest of sovereignty**.
- Every exercise of **structural power** by an IGO (ICC, WTO, IMF) is at some level a **constraint on sovereignty**.
- Every act of **soft power** shapes the *legitimacy* of sovereign choices (Chapter 4 territory).
- **Pooled sovereignty** (EU) is a *voluntary trade of sovereignty for pooled power*.
- **Recognition** — the granting or withholding of de jure sovereignty — is itself an exercise of relational and structural power (the P5 veto is a sovereignty gatekeeper).
- **Sanctions** are the sharpest peacetime power tool that reveals sovereignty limits (frozen Russian reserves impossible without dollar structural power).

**Exam move.** In any Paper 2 essay that names one concept, at least one paragraph should invoke the other. They are the analytical twins of the course.

## Section 3.12 — Exam-Ready Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sovereignty** | A state's independence, territorial control, and self-government free from external interference; internal (supreme authority within) and external (recognition by other states). |
| **Westphalian sovereignty** | The traditional model traced to the 1648 Peace of Westphalia: territorial integrity, non-interference, legal equality of states, state as basic unit. |
| **Internal sovereignty** | Supreme authority within a territory — the ability to make and enforce law over one's own population. |
| **External sovereignty** | Independence from other states; recognition as an equal actor in international affairs. |
| **De jure sovereignty** | Sovereignty in law — recognised by other states, member of international bodies, signatory to treaties. |
| **De facto sovereignty** | Sovereignty in practice — actually controlling the territory and exercising authority, regardless of legal recognition. |
| **Recognition** | The formal act by which one state acknowledges another as a sovereign entity. |
| **Popular sovereignty** | The idea that ultimate authority derives from the people, not the state. |
| **Pooled sovereignty** | Voluntary transfer of specific sovereign authority to supranational institutions (EU as the paradigm case). |
| **Sovereign equality** | The principle that all sovereign states are formally equal under international law regardless of size or power. |
| **Non-interference** | The Westphalian norm that states should not intervene in each other's internal affairs. |
| **R2P (Responsibility to Protect)** | The 2005 UN doctrine that the international community may intervene when a state fails to protect its own population from mass atrocity crimes; a formal qualification of non-interference. |
| **Failed / fragile state** | A state whose government has lost effective control over significant portions of its territory or cannot deliver core public services. |
| **Autonomy** | Devolved self-government within a larger sovereign state (Iraqi Kurdistan, Catalonia, Scotland, pre-2019 Jammu & Kashmir under Article 370). |
| **Self-determination** | The principle that peoples have the right to freely determine their political status; can conflict with territorial integrity. |
| **Territorial integrity** | The principle that a state's borders should not be forcibly altered — a bedrock of the UN Charter (Article 2(4)). |
| **Digital sovereignty** | A state's ability to control data, platforms, infrastructure, and AI within its jurisdiction — a modern extension of traditional sovereignty into the digital domain. |
| **Rome Statute** | The 1998 treaty establishing the ICC; parties commit to cooperate with the Court's jurisdiction over serious international crimes. |
| **Montevideo criteria** | The four legal criteria for statehood: permanent population, defined territory, government, capacity to enter relations with other states. |

## Section 3.13 — Common Exam Traps

1. **Treating sovereignty as binary.** It's a spectrum. Different dimensions erode at different rates. North Korea's sovereignty is not the same as Somalia's is not the same as an EU member's.
2. **Confusing sovereignty with independence, autonomy, or self-determination.** They are related but distinct. Iraqi Kurdistan has autonomy; it does not have sovereignty. Catalonia and Scotland claim self-determination; they don't currently have sovereign statehood.
3. **"Westphalia invented sovereignty" as if uncontested.** Historians (Osiander) argue the 1648 treaties did not enshrine modern sovereignty. Use "Westphalian" as a *model* not a historical claim.
4. **Ignoring de jure / de facto distinction.** Taiwan, Palestine, Somaliland, Kosovo all require this analytical tool.
5. **Assuming R2P is a settled norm.** It is *contested*. Libya 2011 remains its most controversial case; Russia's 2022 invasion rhetorically invoked R2P-like framing (protecting Russian-speakers) which most states rejected.
6. **Only citing the EU as pooled sovereignty.** ASEAN, African Union, MERCOSUR, WTO membership all involve degrees of pooling. Range widens your answer.
7. **Missing the digital sovereignty angle.** In 2026 this is where the most active sovereignty debates are; a Paper 2 essay that ignores it looks dated.
8. **Case studies without dates or actors.** "Palestine is contested" earns nothing. "~140 UN member states recognise Palestine; full UN membership blocked by US veto in the Security Council" is usable evidence.
9. **Ignoring internal contestation of sovereignty.** Sovereignty is challenged from below (secessionist movements — Catalonia, Scotland, Kashmir) as well as from above (IGOs, MNCs, ICC).
10. **Not linking sovereignty to power.** The two concepts are twins. Every essay on sovereignty benefits from at least one paragraph invoking power (usually structural or hard).
