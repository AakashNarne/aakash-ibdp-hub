# GP Rights & Justice 1 — Contested Meanings

**Syllabus:** Thematic study *Rights and justice*, part 1, **Contested meanings**: concepts, definitions and views on rights; human rights; theoretical views on rights and justice; justice (meanings, theories, global perspectives). See [[_Syllabus Index]].
**Assessed in:** Paper 2, Section A (15-mark essay, one question per theme) and Section B (integrating questions). Also useful for the HL extension challenges *Equality* and *Identity*.

> [!abstract] If you remember nothing else
> - **Rights** are claims or entitlements a person holds *because they are human*. **Justice** is about people being treated fairly and getting what they deserve. Both are **contested**: people agree on the words and disagree about what they mean and who has to deliver them.
> - The **UDHR (10 Dec 1948)** is the reference point: 30 rights, claimed to be *universal, inalienable, indivisible*. Critics call it Western. It passed 48–0 with **8 abstentions**, and Saudi Arabia and apartheid South Africa were among the abstainers.
> - Three ways to classify rights: **negative vs positive** (the state must hold back vs the state must act); **three generations** (liberty / equality / fraternity, from Vasak); **individual vs collective**. In real cases they overlap. Say so in essays.
> - **Universalism vs cultural relativism** is the central tension. The death penalty is the textbook's example. India's **Uniform Civil Code** debate is your home example.
> - The six kinds of justice to know: **social, political, egalitarian (Rawls), cosmopolitan, ecological, Ubuntu**. Ubuntu stresses community and repair over punishment. In practice it looks like South Africa's **TRC**: 7,112 amnesty applications, **849 granted**.

---

## 1. Why these meanings are "contested" and what that has to do with power

**Command terms:** *define*, *explain*, *discuss*.

Hodder's point (pp. 95–97): ideas of right and wrong come from family, religion, school, the media and the state. They are **social before they are legal**, so different communities end up with different versions. The book ties rights and justice straight back to [[Chapter 1: Foundations of Global Politics|the four key concepts]]:

| Key concept | How it shows up in rights and justice |
|---|---|
| **Power** | Who defines a right, and who has the capacity to deliver or deny it |
| **Sovereignty** | Each state chooses how, or whether, to apply and enforce its citizens' rights |
| **Legitimacy** | States, courts, IGOs and NGOs have their authority to judge rights questioned |
| **Interdependence** | Values travel along economic ties, and so do rights norms |

Four further concepts sit inside the theme (Hodder p. 97):
- **Equality**: all people have the same intrinsic value.
- **Liberty**. **Negative liberty** is freedom *from* coercion. **Positive liberty** is being *able to do* what you want. The distinction is classically Isaiah Berlin's, from his Oxford lecture "Two Concepts of Liberty", 31 Oct 1958 ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Concepts_of_Liberty)).
- **Justice**: being treated fairly and getting what one deserves.
- **Rights**: what a person needs to live a life of dignity and purpose.

> [!tip] "Contested" does not mean "opposite"
> Hodder's review question (p. 114) makes this point, and examiners reward it. Contested meanings often overlap. A cultural relativist and a postcolonialist can *agree* that the UDHR carries Western bias while disagreeing about what to put in its place. In an essay, show both the overlap and the difference.

## 2. Rights: definitions and the UDHR

**Command terms:** *define*, *explain*, *to what extent*.

**Rights**: basic claims and entitlements that human beings have simply because they are human, which allow people to live safe, meaningful and dignified lives (Hodder p. 97).
**Human rights**: rights held by *all* humans regardless of nationality, culture, religion, sex or status. The standard modern definition comes from the UDHR.

### 2.1 Origins: from Yalta to the UDHR (Hodder pp. 98–100)
- The UN came out of Allied planning in 1944–45 (Yalta, Feb 1945) and came into existence on **24 Oct 1945**. Article 1 of the UN Charter already mentions "equal rights", "self-determination", "human rights and fundamental freedoms".
- According to Hodder, the **Commission on Human Rights** had 19 members from different political, cultural and religious backgrounds and drafted the UDHR in under two years. Three drafters worth naming:
  - **Eleanor Roosevelt** (USA), the chair.
  - **Peng-chun Chang** (China), who insisted on universalism and balanced individual rights against duties to the community.
  - **Charles Malik** (Lebanon), who worked to make it apply across religions.
- Adopted by the UN General Assembly on **10 December 1948** as Resolution 217(III), by **48 votes in favour, 0 against, 8 abstentions** ([UN Dag Hammarskjöld Library](https://research.un.org/en/undhr/ga/plenary)). The abstainers were the USSR, Byelorussian SSR, Ukrainian SSR, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, **Saudi Arabia** and **South Africa**. Saudi Arabia objected mainly to Art. 18 (freedom to *change* religion) and Art. 16 (equal marriage rights) ([Wikipedia, UDHR, citing UN Yearbook 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights)).
- **10 December** is now Human Rights Day. The UDHR lists **30 rights**, which Hodder groups as civil, legal, social, political, economic, and cultural/solidarity rights (p. 101).

> [!example] Use the 1948 vote as evidence
> The founding document of "universal" rights was **not unanimous**. The Soviet bloc abstained because it prioritised economic and social rights over individual freedoms. Saudi Arabia abstained on religious-cultural grounds. South Africa abstained to protect apartheid. That gives you one fact covering three different grounds for contesting rights: ideology, culture, and simple self-interest.

### 2.2 The UDHR's key principles (Hodder Table 2.2, p. 102)

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Universal and inalienable** | Everyone has them because they are human. They cannot be taken away or given away. |
| **Indivisible** | All rights matter equally. You cannot pick and choose. |
| **Interdependent and interrelated** | Rights combine to make a fulfilled life possible |
| **Participation and inclusion** | People can take part in, and get information about, decisions affecting them |
| **Accountability and rule of law** | States must answer for violations, and individuals need a route to justice |

**Natural rights** are the philosophical root. They are rights that exist before, and independently of, any state's laws or culture. **John Locke** (1632–1704) argued for natural equality, freedom, and the right to preserve life and property. These ideas fed the American and French Revolutions and abolitionism (Hodder p. 102). This is exactly why critics call the UDHR Western: its foundations come from the European Enlightenment.

## 3. Three ways to classify rights

### 3.1 Negative vs positive rights (Hodder pp. 102–103)
**Command terms:** *compare and contrast*, *distinguish*, *evaluate*.

| | **Negative rights** | **Positive rights** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Rights that require those with power to **refrain** from acting. They are respected "by doing nothing at all". | Rights that require authorities to **take action**, which places responsibility on those with power |
| Origin (per Hodder) | Associated with **Kant** and the individual-centred Western tradition | Emerged in the **20th century** |
| UDHR examples | Art. 3 (life, liberty, security of person); Art. 5 (no torture) | Art. 26 (right to education, free at elementary level); Hodder also lists Art. 11 (fair trial) |
| Everyday examples | Privacy; walking home safely; keeping your property | Clean environment; help in danger; going to school |

**The overlap:** Hodder's prison example. Guards must not abuse prisoners, which is a negative duty. They must also *actively* stop prisoners abusing each other, which is a positive duty. So one right, freedom from cruel treatment, is both. Conclusion: in real situations positive and negative rights are **interdependent**, and "many disagree with the distinction" (p. 103).

> [!example] Indian case: education as a positive right
> - The **86th Constitutional Amendment (2002)** inserted **Article 21A**: "The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years…" ([Legislative Dept, GoI](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/16970eb1a68d4fe46ac40acd783a6c34.pdf), verified from the search-result text of the Act).
> - The enabling **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act 2009** (assent 26 Aug 2009) came into force on **1 April 2010** ([Wikipedia, RTE Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_of_Children_to_Free_and_Compulsory_Education_Act,_2009)).
> - The Act requires schools (government and private, except private unaided *minority* schools) to reserve **25% of seats** for children from weaker and disadvantaged groups. The Supreme Court upheld this on **12 April 2012** ([PRS India](https://prsindia.org/theprsblog/supreme-court-upholds-25-reservation-in-private-schools?page=229&per-page=1)).
> - **Why it's a good example:** a textbook positive right. The state must *spend and act*, and it even imposes duties on private actors. It also shows the tension. Private schools argued the 25% quota infringed *their* freedom to run their institutions, which is a negative-rights argument. The minority exemption is a collective-rights concession.

### 3.2 The three generations of rights (Hodder pp. 103–104)
The classification is **Karel Vasak**'s (1970s, International Institute of Human Rights, Strasbourg), modelled on the French Revolution's *liberté, égalité, fraternité*.

| | **1st generation: Liberty** | **2nd generation: Equality** | **3rd generation: Fraternity** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Civil and political | Economic, social, cultural | Collective / solidarity |
| Examples | Freedom from slavery, privacy, fair trial, no arbitrary arrest | Safe working conditions, housing, food, health care, social security | Clean environment, development, benefiting from world trade |
| Maps onto | Mostly **negative** rights | Mostly **positive** rights | Rights of **communities**, a response to globalisation |

> [!warning] Trap
> The generations are **not a ranking** and not a strict chronology. The UDHR already contained 2nd-generation rights in 1948 (e.g. Art. 26, education: Hodder Table 2.3). The Vienna Declaration (1993) states that "all human rights are universal, **indivisible and interdependent and interrelated**… on the same footing, and with the same emphasis" ([OHCHR, VDPA para. 5](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/vienna-declaration-and-programme-action)).

### 3.3 Individual vs collective rights (Hodder p. 104)
**Collective rights** belong to groups that share a cultural or ethnic identity. The classic example is **self-determination**, the right of a people to choose its own government and, if it wants, form a state (Hodder cites Palestinians and Catalans). Some rights can be read either way: freedom of religion, a clean environment.
→ *Recognition of individual and group rights* is a full **Debate** in part 4. See [[GP Rights & Justice 4 — Debates — Notes]] (to be created).

## 4. Universalism vs cultural relativism

**Command terms:** *discuss*, *evaluate*, *to what extent*, *examine*.

- **Universalism**: human rights belong to all people regardless of culture, religion or nationality (Hodder p. 105).
- **Cultural relativism**: every culture has its own moral code, so "right" and "wrong" should be set by local communities, not by a universal authority like the UN. On this view the UDHR's claim to universality imposes **Western values** (Hodder p. 104).

**Hodder's example: the death penalty (p. 105).** UDHR Art. 3 (the right to life) is often read as requiring abolition. Relativists answer that this reading is Western. Hodder notes that the Quran permits capital punishment in restricted circumstances, and that states such as Saudi Arabia, Iran and Egypt refuse abolition on relativist grounds.
- *Current scale (verified):* Amnesty International recorded **2,707 executions in 2025**, up 78% from **1,518 in 2024**. The figures exclude China's thousands. Iran carried out **2,159** and Saudi Arabia at least **356**. Only **17 countries** carried out executions ([Amnesty USA summary of the 2025 global report](https://www.amnestyusa.org/reports/amnesty-international-global-report-death-sentences-and-executions-2025/)).
- *Evaluation:* only 17 executing states, and Gambia abolishing the death penalty for murder in 2025 (same source), suggest a universalist norm that is still spreading. The concentration of executions in a few states shows **sovereignty** limiting it.

**The diplomatic version: "Asian values" (1993).**
- The **Bangkok Declaration** (Asian states, 29 Mar–2 Apr 1993), para. 8: human rights "are universal in nature" but must be considered "bearing in mind the significance of national and regional particularities and various historical, cultural and religious backgrounds". It also reaffirms "non-interference in the internal affairs of States" ([HURIGHTS Osaka text](https://www.hurights.or.jp/archives/other_documents/section1/1993/04/final-declaration-of-the-regional-meeting-for-asia-of-the-world-conference-on-human-rights.html)).
- The **Vienna Declaration** (25 June 1993) accepted that particularities "must be borne in mind", then insisted it is "the duty of States, regardless of their political, economic and cultural systems, to promote and protect all human rights" ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/vienna-declaration-and-programme-action)).
- This is the compromise to quote. Jack Donnelly (quoted in Hodder p. 106) calls it **"relative universality"**: universal core, local implementation.

> [!example] Indian case: the Uniform Civil Code (UCC)
> - **Article 44** (a Directive Principle): "The State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India." In the Constituent Assembly (23 Nov 1948) some Muslim members argued it would violate freedom of religion and interfere with personal law ([constitutionofindia.net](https://www.constitutionofindia.net/articles/article-44-uniform-civil-code-for-the-citizens/)).
> - The **22nd Law Commission** issued a public notice on **14 June 2023** asking the public and recognised religious organisations for views on a UCC. The consultation window closed on **28 July 2023** ([Law Commission notice](https://lawcommissionofindia.nic.in/notice/uniform-civil-code-public-notice/); [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseIframePage.aspx?PRID=1932397)).
> - **Uttarakhand** became the first state to bring a UCC into force, on **27 January 2025** ([The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/uttarakhand/uniform-civil-code-to-come-into-effect-in-uttarakhand-from-january-27/article69140802.ece)).
> - Earlier, in *Shayara Bano v Union of India* (22 Aug 2017), the Supreme Court held instant triple talaq unconstitutional by a **3:2** majority ([SC Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/cases/shayara-bano-union-india-triple-talaq-case-background/)).
> - **How to use it:** the debate runs on several lines at once.
>   - *Universalist* claim: equal rights for women across communities (UDHR Art. 16, equal marriage rights).
>   - *Relativist / collective-rights* counterclaim: religious minorities' right to their own personal law.
>   - *Power* point: critics read a majority-imposed code as a threat to minority identity.
>   - *Complication:* here the "universalist" position is pushed by a **nationalist** government, and some rights groups oppose it. The same rights language serves different political agendas.

> [!example] Indian case: *Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India* (6 Sept 2018)
> - A five-judge Constitution Bench **unanimously** struck down Section 377 IPC, a colonial-era law, as far as it criminalised consensual sex between adults. The court found violations of **Arts 14, 15, 19 and 21**, overturning *Suresh Kumar Koushal v Naz Foundation* (2013) ([Wikipedia, citing the judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navtej_Singh_Johar_v._Union_of_India)).
> - **Why it's useful:** it turns the usual relativist argument upside down. The *restriction* was a British colonial import. Striking it down could be framed as **decolonising** Indian law as much as "Westernising" it. That lets you challenge the assumption that universal rights = Western imposition. The Upendra Baxi quote in Hodder p. 106 fits here.

## 5. Theoretical views on rights and justice

**Command terms:** *evaluate*, *discuss*, *compare*.
The full theories are in [[GP — Theoretical Perspectives — Notes]]. Don't re-learn them here. Learn what each says **about rights**. Hodder's Table 2.5 (p. 106) is compressed below.

| Theory | View of rights and justice | Hodder's key quote |
|---|---|---|
| **Liberalism** | Rights are natural, held because we are human, and exist whether or not society accepts them. Individual-centred. **Codification** (writing rights into law) and courts are the way to secure them. The UDHR is the model. | Jack Donnelly: international legal universality is "one of the greatest achievements" of the human rights movement |
| **Marxism** | Rejects natural rights. Rights are social agreements. Capitalism maximises profit at workers' expense, and human rights instruments leave the *structural* causes of exploitation untouched. | Noam Chomsky: rights have "purely instrumental value… a useful tool for propaganda" |
| **Postcolonialism** | Rights must address real injustice now. All of us owe justice to the marginalised. Distrusts human rights law as elite-designed and ineffective. | Upendra Baxi (Indian jurist): look at rights "from the standpoint of the historically oppressed" |
| **Feminism** | Believes in rights but sees any fixed definition as **imperialistic**. Questions *individual* rights in favour of wider goals such as female emancipation. Rejects universalism and judges rights law case by case. | Wendy Brown on #MeToo and feminist mobilisation from Afghanistan to Türkiye |
| **Realism** | *Missing from Hodder's table on purpose.* Rights and justice are largely irrelevant to state behaviour. States act on national interest, and ethics plays no role. | — |

> [!tip] Using theories in essays, and linking to the ideology map
> Use the left/centre/right framework in [[GP — Theoretical Perspectives — Notes|the theory note, Section 2]]:
> - The **liberal** view is the "official" UN view, centrist and institutionalist.
> - The **Marxist and postcolonial** critiques come from the left and attack whose interests the rights regime serves.
> - The **realist** silence matters in its own right. It explains why states sign treaties and then ignore them.
> - **Constructivism** isn't in Hodder's table, but it is the best lens for *how* rights norms spread, for example the diffusion from the UDHR to the Vienna Declaration to national courts (Makwanyane, Navtej).

## 6. Justice: meanings and types

**Command terms:** *define*, *explain*, *compare and contrast*, *evaluate*.

**Justice** is an ethical concept about how people should and should not be treated, usually understood as being treated **fairly** and getting what one **deserves** (Hodder p. 107). It is contested because people disagree about what anyone "deserves". Is clean water owed to everyone, or does it depend on the society? That second answer is the relativist one.

### 6.1 Social vs political justice (Hodder Table 2.6, pp. 107–108)

| | **Social justice** | **Political justice** |
|---|---|---|
| Concerns | Comparing access across a society or the world: food, water, education, health, housing, safety, work and a living wage | People's power to shape decisions in their society, and fair treatment by police and courts |
| Aim | Fewer differences between groups | Participation, fair trials, no persecution for political beliefs |
| Hodder's examples | Unequal access to necessities, from local communities to the global level | **North Korea**: low political justice, because citizens can't share in decision-making. Full adult suffrage means higher political justice, **but democracy alone is not enough.** |

Social and political injustice are often **interdependent**. Hodder's case is the **US criminal justice system** (Table 2.7, p. 108, compiled from Census.gov, Prison Policy Initiative and others): Black Americans are **13%** of the US population but **38%** of people in prison or jail.
→ For Aakash's course case study, see [[Chapter 1: Foundations of Global Politics#Section 1.3 — The Three Thematic Studies|North Korea]]: a nearly total absence of political justice.

### 6.2 Egalitarian justice: John Rawls (Hodder pp. 109–110)
**Egalitarian justice** focuses on social justice. Everyone is owed equal respect, dignity, freedoms and opportunities.
- **John Rawls**, *A Theory of Justice* (1971), called his theory "justice as fairness".
- **Difference principle:** social and economic inequalities are just only if they are to "the greatest benefit of the least-advantaged members of society" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Rawls](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rawls/)).
- He derived his principles from behind a **veil of ignorance** in the *original position*: choose the rules of society without knowing where you will end up in it ([SEP, Original Position](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/original-position/)).
- **Against utilitarianism** (Hodder p. 110): "Each person possesses an inviolability founded on justice that even the welfare of society as a whole cannot override." So Rawls would reject spending public money only on the most talented students. That fits the RTE Act's 25% quota neatly.
- Rawls also claims "A society regulated by a public sense of justice is inherently stable." This connects justice to peace and stability.

### 6.3 Cosmopolitan justice (Hodder pp. 110–112)
**Cosmopolitan justice** takes egalitarian ideas **global**. Everyone has obligations to everyone, borders are not moral walls, and IGOs such as the UN and AU must act when needed.
- It can justify **humanitarian intervention** when three conditions hold:
  1. The state is structurally unjust (abusive or failed).
  2. The intervention will succeed.
  3. It is a last resort.
- **Migration test.** Per UNHCR figures in Hodder p. 111, **76%** of refugees are hosted by low- and middle-income countries and 70% live in states neighbouring their own. Rich states tend to **pay rather than host**: per Hodder's UNHCR tables (pp. 111–112), the USA gave about $2.2bn to UNHCR in 2022 but hosted far fewer refugees than Türkiye (3.67m, mid-2022).
- **Indian angle:** India hosted about 212,000 refugees and asylum seekers in 2022 (Hodder Fig. 2.11, reading of a garbled figure label, so treat the number as approximate). India is **not a party** to the 1951 Refugee Convention ([FMR / Janmyr](https://www.fmreview.org/issue67/janmyr/)). It shows **sovereignty** limiting cosmopolitan obligations.
- The full treatment of humanitarian intervention is in the **Debates** part: [[GP Rights & Justice 4 — Debates — Notes]] (to be created). Libya 2011 is in [[Chapter 1: Foundations of Global Politics]].

### 6.4 Ecological justice (Hodder p. 112)
**Ecological justice** extends justice to **all living beings**, not only humans. Non-human beings have **"moral considerability"**, and we owe them justice *not only* because it benefits us. Its roots are in habitat loss and mass extinction.
- *Verified example:* **Ecuador's 2008 Constitution** was the first in the world to give Nature ("Pachamama") rights. Art. 71 says Nature "has the right to exist, persist, maintain and regenerate its vital cycles", drawing on the Andean Indigenous idea of *sumak kawsay* ([Ecojurisprudence Monitor](https://ecojurisprudence.org/initiatives/ecuador-constitution/); [GARN text of Art. 71](https://www.garn.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Rights-for-Nature-Articles-in-Ecuador-s-Constitution.pdf)).
- *Verified example of injustice:* Brazil's space agency INPE reported **13,235 km²** of Amazon deforestation in 2020–21. That was up 22% and the highest since 2006 ([BBC, 19 Nov 2021](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-59341770)).
- Link: 3rd-generation right to a clean environment → [[_Syllabus Index|HL extension: Environment]].

### 6.5 Zulu Ubuntu (Hodder p. 113; more on pp. 287–288)
**Ubuntu** is a Zulu/Southern African philosophy in which **community** is the basic building block of society. Desmond Tutu, in *No Future Without Forgiveness*, put it as: "My humanity is inextricably bound up in yours. We belong in a bundle of life." Justice under Ubuntu is **restorative** (repair relationships, restore dignity, reconcile). Liberal legal justice is typically **retributive** (punish the individual offender).

> [!example] African case: the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission
> - Set up under the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act 1995 and chaired by **Archbishop Desmond Tutu** ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Truth-and-Reconciliation-Commission-South-Africa)).
> - Mandate: gross human rights violations from **March 1960 to May 1994**. It held over 2,500 hearings ([DOJ&CD TRC site](https://www.justice.gov.za/TRC/index.html)).
> - **Amnesty for full disclosure.** The Amnesty Committee's official summary (1 Nov 2000) records **7,112 applications**: **849 granted**, **5,392 refused**, the rest withdrawn or duplicates ([DOJ&CD, Summary of Amnesty Decisions](https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/amntrans/index.htm); same figures at [SAHO](https://sahistory.org.za/article/truth-and-reconciliation-commission-trc-0) and [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/south_african_truth_commission)).
> - **Ubuntu in the courts:** in ***S v Makwanyane*** (**6 June 1995**), the new Constitutional Court **unanimously** held the death penalty unconstitutional and relied on *ubuntu*'s emphasis on reconciliation over retribution ([Himonga et al., PER 2013](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1727-37812013000500009); [SAFLII judgment](https://www.saflii.org/za/cases/ZACC/1995/3.html)). This is a direct contrast with the relativist defence of capital punishment in §4.
> - **Evaluation angles:**
>   - *For:* Ubuntu offered a non-Western model of justice that prioritised peace and truth over punishment. It helped a transition without civil war.
>   - *Against:* most of the 5,392 refused applicants were never prosecuted, and reparations were largely not implemented (Cornell LII, above). A victims'-rights perspective asks whether *justice* was traded for *peace*.

> [!info] Liberty ↔ equality: the thread through every perspective
> Hodder closes (pp. 113–114) with liberty (personal freedom and choice) and equality (equal intrinsic worth) held in "delicate balance". Every type of justice above weighs the two differently:
> - The **negative-rights/liberal** view leans towards liberty.
> - **Egalitarian and social** justice lean towards equality.
> - **Ubuntu** reframes both through community.

## 7. Paper 2 coaching: essay angles (not essays)

Structure reminder: [[Chapter 1: Foundations of Global Politics#Paper 2 — the essay structure]]. Define terms, state your thesis, then two claims and two counterclaims, each with a *named* case.

> [!tip] Q1 — "To what extent can universal human rights be reconciled with culturally relative understandings of justice?"
> - **Define:** universalism, cultural relativism, justice.
> - **Claim:** they can be reconciled through "relative universality" (Donnelly), meaning a universal core with local implementation. Evidence: the Vienna Declaration 1993 "particularities" clause; *Makwanyane* grounding a universal norm (no death penalty) in a local one (ubuntu).
> - **Counterclaim:** reconciliation breaks down on specific practices. Evidence: the death penalty (Iran 2,159 executions in 2025); Saudi Arabia's 1948 abstention over Art. 18.
> - **Complication:** "culture" is often invoked by elites to protect power. Bangkok para. 8 sits next to its non-interference clause. Use a **postcolonial** reading and a **realist** one.
> - **India:** the UCC, with gender equality against religious personal law.
> - **Concepts:** sovereignty, legitimacy.

> [!tip] Q2 — "Compare and contrast positive and negative rights in promoting justice."
> - **Claim:** negative rights are cheaper and easier to enforce in court (Navtej, where the state had to *stop* criminalising).
> - **Counterclaim:** negative rights without positive provision are empty. A child "free" to learn needs a school (Art. 21A/RTE).
> - **Evaluation:** Hodder's prison example and Vienna's "indivisible" show the categories blur.
> - **Theories:** classical liberalism (negative rights); social liberalism and Marxism (positive rights).

> [!tip] Q3 — "Evaluate the effectiveness of an alternative (non-Western) conception of justice."
> - **Claim:** Ubuntu and the TRC produced truth and a peaceful transition. It was a restorative model, not a retributive one.
> - **Counterclaim:** 849 amnesties against few prosecutions of those refused, and unpaid reparations, add up to an *impunity* critique.
> - **Theories:** postcolonialism (valuing non-Western justice); liberalism (accountability and rule of law); feminism (whose voices testified?).
> - **Concepts:** legitimacy (of the new state), power.

> [!tip] Q4 (Section B, integrating with Development and sustainability) — "Justice requires more than the protection of individual rights. Discuss."
> - Use **3rd-generation rights**, **cosmopolitan justice** (refugee hosting burden: 76% hosted by low- and middle-income states) and **ecological justice** (Ecuador Art. 71 vs Amazon deforestation).
> - Counter with the liberal/Rawlsian defence of the individual's "inviolability".

## 8. Common mistakes / examiner traps

1. **Treating the UDHR as law.** It is a *declaration* (a UNGA resolution) and not binding in itself. Binding force comes through later treaties and codification, covered in part 3: [[GP Rights & Justice 3 — Nature, Practice and Study — Notes]] (to be created).
2. **"Universal" = "unanimous".** The 1948 vote had 8 abstentions. Use that fact; don't assume consensus.
3. **Presenting cultural relativism as a single "non-Western" bloc.** India, South Africa and Ecuador all show non-Western states *expanding* rights. Relativist arguments are also made *within* Western states (e.g. US retention of the death penalty).
4. **Presenting negative and positive rights, or the three generations, as watertight.** Hodder explicitly says they overlap. Say so.
5. **Using "justice" without saying which kind.** Social, political, egalitarian, cosmopolitan, ecological and Ubuntu are different claims. Name the one you mean.
6. **Leaving realism out of a rights essay.** Its view that rights are irrelevant to state interest is a strong counterclaim, not an omission.
7. **Loose figures.** The TRC granted **849** amnesties from **7,112** applications. Figures like "~1,200 granted" circulate and are wrong.
8. **Mixing up egalitarian and cosmopolitan justice.** Egalitarian justice applies *within* a society (Rawls). Cosmopolitan justice is *global*, with duties across borders.
9. **Confusing negative *liberty* with negative *rights*.** They are related but not the same. Liberty is a freedom. A right is an entitlement that places a duty on someone else.

## 9. Self-test

1. Define *positive* and *negative* rights, and give one UDHR article and one Indian example of each.
2. Name the three generations of rights, who devised the classification, and the slogan it borrows from.
3. What was the UN General Assembly vote on the UDHR, and why does it matter for the universalism–relativism debate?
4. Distinguish egalitarian from cosmopolitan justice, naming the key thinker or principle for each.
5. Explain how Ubuntu shaped justice in post-apartheid South Africa, with two verified figures or facts, and give one criticism.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. **Negative rights** require those with power to *refrain*. Examples: UDHR Art. 5 (no torture); in India, *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018), where the state had to stop criminalising consensual same-sex relations. **Positive rights** require the state to *act or provide*. Examples: UDHR Art. 26 (education); in India, Art. 21A and the RTE Act 2009 (free, compulsory education for ages 6–14, in force 1 Apr 2010, with a 25% private-school quota). Credit the point that the two overlap (Hodder's prison example).
> 2. **1st generation, liberty:** civil and political rights. **2nd, equality:** economic, social and cultural rights. **3rd, fraternity:** collective/solidarity rights such as development and a clean environment. The classification is **Karel Vasak**'s (1970s), modelled on the French Revolution's *liberté, égalité, fraternité*.
> 3. **48 in favour, 0 against, 8 abstentions** (10 Dec 1948, Res. 217(III)). The abstainers included the Soviet bloc (it prioritised socio-economic rights), Saudi Arabia (Arts 16 and 18 on marriage and changing religion) and South Africa (apartheid). "Universal" rights were contested from day one on ideological, cultural and self-interested grounds.
> 4. **Egalitarian:** equal respect, dignity and opportunity *within* a society. Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971): the difference principle (inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged), the veil of ignorance, and the individual's "inviolability" against utilitarian trade-offs. **Cosmopolitan:** extends these duties *globally* across borders. IGOs must act, and humanitarian intervention can be justified if the state is structurally unjust, success is likely, and it is a last resort. Migration and refugee-hosting is the test case.
> 5. Ubuntu ("my humanity is bound up in yours") means restorative, community-centred justice. The **TRC** (chaired by Tutu) offered amnesty for full disclosure: **7,112 applications, 849 granted, 5,392 refused**. In ***S v Makwanyane* (6 June 1995)** the Constitutional Court unanimously abolished the death penalty, citing ubuntu. **Criticism:** few of those refused amnesty were later prosecuted and reparations were largely unimplemented, so victims may have given up justice for peace.
