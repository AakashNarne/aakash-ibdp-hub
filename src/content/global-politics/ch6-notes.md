# GP Rights & Justice 1 — Contested Meanings — Notes

**Syllabus:** Thematic study *Rights and justice*, part 1: **contested meanings**. It covers definitions of and perspectives on rights (positive and negative rights, cultural relativism) and on justice (social, political, egalitarian, cosmopolitan and ecological justice, and Zulu Ubuntu), plus theoretical views. See [[_Syllabus Index]].
**Assessed in:** Paper 2 (essays), Paper 1 (source analysis) and the HL extension challenges *Equality* and *Identity*.

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **Rights** are claims or entitlements people hold *because they are human*. **Justice** means being treated fairly and getting what you deserve. Both are **contested**: people agree on the words but disagree about what they mean and who has to deliver them.
> - The **UDHR** was adopted on **10 Dec 1948** by **48 votes to 0, with 8 abstentions**. It lists 30 rights and claims to be universal, inalienable and indivisible. The first "universal" standard was therefore not unanimous.
> - There are three ways to classify rights: **negative vs positive**, the **three generations** (Vasak: liberty, equality, fraternity) and **individual vs collective**. Hodder stresses that in real cases they overlap.
> - The central tension is **universalism vs cultural relativism**. Hodder's example is the death penalty: Amnesty recorded **2,707 executions in 2025**, and India's **Uniform Civil Code** debate is the Indian example.
> - Know six kinds of justice: **social, political, egalitarian (Rawls), cosmopolitan, ecological and Ubuntu**. In practice Ubuntu meant South Africa's **TRC**, which received **7,112** amnesty applications and granted **849**.

## 1. Why meanings are contested: the key concepts

**Command terms:** *define*, *explain*, *discuss*.

Ideas of right and wrong come from family, school, religion, friends, the media and the wider community. State legal systems usually rest on these shared moral ideas. At the global level the UN, other IGOs and NGOs hold "definitive ideas" about which rights everyone deserves (Hodder p. 95). Because rights and justice are **social before they are legal**, different communities build different versions. Hodder links rights and justice directly to **power**, the central concept of the course (p. 95).

| Key concept | Hodder's definition (pp. 96–97) | Why it matters for rights and justice |
|---|---|---|
| **Power** | The ability to affect change; an aspect of relations among people | Who defines a right, and who can deliver it or deny it |
| **Sovereignty** | A state's right to make decisions independently | Each state chooses how, or whether, to apply and enforce its citizens' rights |
| **Legitimacy** | Being commonly accepted as having authority to exercise power over others | The authority of states, courts, IGOs and NGOs to rule on rights is often questioned |
| **Interdependence** | Mutual reliance among groups, organisations and regions | Values, rights norms among them, travel along economic ties |

Four concepts embedded in this theme (Hodder p. 97):
- **Equality**: all people have the same intrinsic value.
- **Liberty**: **negative liberty** is freedom *from* coercion. **Positive liberty** is being *able to do* what you want. Isaiah Berlin developed the distinction in the 1950s and 60s. The idea goes back at least to Kant ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liberty-positive-negative/)).
- **Justice**: being treated fairly and getting what you deserve.
- **Rights**: what you need to live a life of dignity and purpose.

> [!note] "Contested" does not mean "opposite"
> Hodder's final review question (p. 114) makes this point: contested meanings often **overlap**. A cultural relativist and a postcolonialist can both agree that the UDHR carries Western bias. Examiners reward essays that show where views agree as well as where they differ.

## 2. Rights and the UDHR

**Command terms:** *define*, *explain*, *to what extent*.

**Rights**: basic claims and entitlements that human beings have simply because they are human. They allow people to live safe, meaningful and dignified lives (Hodder p. 97).
**Human rights**: rights held by *all* humans regardless of nationality, culture, religion, sex or status. The UDHR is the reference definition for most actors (Hodder p. 98).

### 2.1 From the UN to the UDHR (Hodder pp. 98–100)
- Allied planning began on 21 Aug 1944, followed by the Yalta Conference on 4–11 Feb 1945. The UN came into existence on **24 Oct 1945**. Article 1 of the UN Charter already refers to "equal rights", "self-determination" and "human rights and fundamental freedoms".
- The **Commission on Human Rights** drafted the UDHR in under two years. Three drafters to know:
  - **Eleanor Roosevelt** (USA), Chair.
  - **Peng-chun Chang** (China), Vice-Chair. He balanced individual rights with duties to the community and insisted on universalism.
  - **Charles Malik** (Lebanon), Rapporteur. He worked to make the text apply across religions.
  - The UN records these three posts from the Commission's first session in Jan–Feb 1947 ([UN Library](https://research.un.org/en/undhr/chr/1)).
- The UDHR was adopted by the General Assembly in Paris on **10 December 1948** as **Resolution 217 A (III)**. The vote was **48 in favour, 0 against, 8 abstentions** ([UN Library](https://research.un.org/en/undhr/ga/plenary); [UN Yearbook 1948–49](https://cdn.un.org/unyearbook/yun/pdf/1948-49/1948-49_545.pdf)). The abstainers were the six Soviet-bloc states, **Saudi Arabia** and **South Africa** ([European Parliament EPRS](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/ATAG/2018/628295/EPRS_ATA(2018)628295_EN.pdf)).
- 10 December is now **Human Rights Day**. The UDHR's 30 rights fall into civil, legal, social, political, economic, and cultural/solidarity rights (Hodder p. 101).

> [!warning] Textbook check
> Hodder (p. 99) says the Commission had **19** members. The UN's own history page says **18** ([UN](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/udhr/history-of-the-declaration)). Use 18.

### 2.2 The UDHR's key principles (Hodder Table 2.2, p. 102)

| Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Universal and inalienable** | Held by everyone because they are human. They cannot be taken away or given away |
| **Indivisible** | All rights are equally important, so you cannot pick and choose |
| **Interdependent and interrelated** | The rights combine to make a fulfilled life possible |
| **Participation and inclusion** | Everyone may take part in, and get information about, decisions that affect them |
| **Accountability and rule of law** | States must answer for violations, and individuals need a route to justice |

**Natural rights** exist independently of any state's laws or culture. **John Locke** (1632–1704) argued for perfect equality and freedom, and for the right to preserve life and property. These ideas inspired the American and French Revolutions and the abolition of the slave trade (Hodder p. 102). Because these foundations come from the European Enlightenment, critics call the UDHR **Western**. Hodder adds that its drafters were mostly Western or Western-educated men (p. 100).

## 3. Classifying rights

### 3.1 Negative vs positive rights (Hodder pp. 102–103)
**Command terms:** *distinguish*, *compare and contrast*, *evaluate*.

| | **Negative rights** | **Positive rights** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Require those with power to **refrain**, so the right is respected "simply by doing nothing at all" | Require authorities to **take action**, which places responsibility on those with power |
| Origin (per Hodder) | Associated with **Kant** (1724–1804) and individual-centred Western thought | Emerged in the **20th century** |
| UDHR examples | Art. 3 (life, liberty and security of person); Art. 5 (no torture) | Art. 26 (education, free at elementary level); Hodder also lists Art. 11 (fair trial) |
| Everyday examples | Privacy; walking home safely; keeping your property | A clean environment; help when in danger; going to school |

**The overlap.** Hodder's prison example (p. 103): guards must not abuse prisoners, which is a negative duty. They must also actively stop prisoners abusing each other, which is a positive duty. So the same right has both sides, and in practice the two kinds are **interdependent**.

### 3.2 The three generations of rights (Hodder pp. 103–104)
**Karel Vasak** of the International Institute of Human Rights in Strasbourg introduced this classification in the 1970s. He set it out in the *UNESCO Courier* in November 1977 ([Cambridge Core](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/netherlands-international-law-review/article/abs/human-rights-universal-panacea-some-reflections-on-the-socalled-human-rights-of-the-third-generation/AA621FCE558794D936439147055C8854)). It is modelled on the French Revolution's *liberté, égalité, fraternité*.

| | **1st generation: liberty** | **2nd generation: equality** | **3rd generation: fraternity** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Civil and political | Economic, social and cultural | Collective or solidarity rights |
| Examples | Freedom from slavery, privacy, a fair trial, no arbitrary arrest | Safe working conditions, housing, food, health care, social security | A clean environment, development, benefiting from world trade |
| Closest to | **Negative** rights | **Positive** rights | Rights of **communities**, a response to globalisation |

> [!warning] Trap
> The generations are **not a ranking** and not a strict timeline. The UDHR already contained 2nd-generation rights in 1948 (Art. 26). The **Vienna Declaration** (25 June 1993, para. 5) says that "All human rights are universal, indivisible and interdependent and interrelated" ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/vienna-declaration-and-programme-action)).

### 3.3 Individual vs collective (group) rights (Hodder p. 104)
**Collective rights** belong to groups or communities, often those that share a cultural or ethnic identity. The classic example is **self-determination**: a people's right to choose its government and, if it wishes, form its own state. Hodder's examples are Palestinians and Catalans. Some rights can be read either way, such as freedom of religion or the right to a clean environment.

> [!example] Case study 1: India's Right to Education (a positive right that collides with group rights)
> - The **86th Constitutional Amendment (2002)** inserted **Article 21A**: "The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years". The **RTE Act 2009** was enforced from **1 April 2010** ([PIB](https://pib.gov.in/newsite/erelcontent.aspx?relid=68827)).
> - The RTE Act requires all schools except private unaided *minority* schools to reserve **25% of seats** for disadvantaged children. The Supreme Court upheld this on **12 April 2012** in *Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan* ([PRS India](https://prsindia.org/theprsblog/supreme-court-upholds-25-reservation-in-private-schools?page=167&per-page=1)).
> - In ***Pramati Educational & Cultural Trust*** (**6 May 2014**), a five-judge Constitution Bench held that the Act does not apply to minority schools, whether aided or unaided, protecting their Art. 30 rights ([Casemine](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/in/5609af45e4b0149711416015)). On **1 Sept 2025** a two-judge bench doubted *Pramati* and asked the Chief Justice to consider a larger bench ([Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/explained/explained-law/explained-rte-and-minority-schools-10226838); [The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/can-minority-run-schools-be-exempted-from-rte-law-sc-refers-question-to-larger-bench/article70001115.ece)).
> - **Use it to show:**
>   - A **positive** right: the state must act and spend.
>   - Private schools making a **negative-rights** objection: "leave us free to run our schools".
>   - A **collective** right (minority institutions) outweighing an **individual** child's right. This is a live and contested balance.

## 4. Universalism vs cultural relativism

**Command terms:** *discuss*, *evaluate*, *to what extent*, *examine*.

- **Universalism**: human rights belong to all people regardless of culture, religion or nationality (Hodder p. 105).
- **Cultural relativism**: every culture has its own moral code, so "right" and "wrong" should be set by local communities, not by a universal authority such as the UN. On this view the UDHR's claim to universality imposes **Western values** (Hodder p. 104).

**Hodder's example (p. 105).** UDHR Art. 3, the right to life, is often read as requiring the death penalty to be abolished. Relativists answer that this reading is Western. The Quran permits capital punishment in restricted circumstances, and states such as **Saudi Arabia, Iran and Egypt** refuse to abolish it on relativist grounds.

> [!example] Case study 2: the death penalty in 2025
> - Amnesty International recorded **at least 2,707 executions in 2025** in **17 countries**. That is up **78%** from **1,518 in 2024** and the highest figure since 1981. China's executions, estimated in the thousands, are excluded ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/05/executions-surge-highest-recorded-figure-44-years/)).
> - The top executioners were China (thousands), **Iran (2,159+)**, **Saudi Arabia (356+)**, Yemen (51+) and the **USA (47)** ([Amnesty facts and figures](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/05/death-penalty-in-2025-facts-and-figures/)). Iran alone accounts for about 80% of the recorded total.
> - **Evaluation:**
>   - Only 17 of 193 states executed anyone, which suggests the universalist norm is winning.
>   - But executions are concentrated in a few sovereign states, and one of them is the **USA**. Relativist-style resistance is therefore not only "non-Western".

**The diplomatic version: "Asian values" (1993).**
- The **Bangkok Declaration** (29 Mar–2 Apr 1993, para. 8) said rights are universal but must be seen in the context of "national and regional particularities and various historical, cultural and religious backgrounds" ([UN Digital Library](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/167021?ln=en); [Oxford Public International Law](https://opil.ouplaw.com/display/10.1093/law:epil/9780199231690/law-9780199231690-e1744?print=pdf)).
- The **Vienna Declaration** (1993, para. 5) accepted that these particularities "must be borne in mind". It then added that it is still "the duty of States, regardless of their political, economic and cultural systems, to promote and protect all human rights" ([OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/vienna-declaration-and-programme-action)).
- Jack Donnelly's **"relative universality"** (quoted in Hodder p. 106) describes this compromise: a universal core, implemented in locally appropriate ways.

> [!example] Case study 3: India's Uniform Civil Code (UCC)
> - **Article 44**, a Directive Principle, says the State "shall endeavour to secure" a UCC. In the Constituent Assembly some Muslim members argued it would violate freedom of religion ([constitutionofindia.net](https://www.constitutionofindia.net/articles/article-44-uniform-civil-code-for-the-citizens/)).
> - In *Shayara Bano v Union of India* (**22 Aug 2017**), the Supreme Court held instant triple talaq unconstitutional by a **3:2** majority ([SC Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/reports/shayara-bano-union-india-triple-talaq-plain-english-summary-of-the-judgment/)).
> - In Aug 2018 the 21st Law Commission said a UCC was "neither necessary nor desirable at this stage". The **22nd Law Commission** reopened consultation on **14 June 2023**, and the deadline was extended to **28 July 2023** ([The Wire](http://cms.thewire.in/government/law-commission-uniform-civil-code-notice); [The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/law-commission-extends-deadline-for-submitting-views-on-ucc-till-july-28/article67080851.ece)).
> - **Uttarakhand** became the first state to implement a UCC, on **27 Jan 2025** ([The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/uttarakhand/uttarakhand-rolls-out-uniform-civil-code-cm-dhami-unveils-portal/article69145972.ece); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/india-news/u-khand-announces-implementation-of-ucc-today-becomes-first-state-to-do-so-125012700127_1.html)).
> - **How to use it:**
>   - *Universalist and individual* claim: equal marriage, divorce and inheritance rights for women (UDHR Art. 16).
>   - *Relativist and collective* counterclaim: religious communities' right to their own personal law.
>   - *Power* point: a majority-imposed code can threaten minority identity.

> [!example] Case study 4: Section 377, *Navtej Singh Johar v Union of India* (6 Sept 2018)
> - A **five-judge Constitution Bench unanimously** struck down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC) as far as it criminalised consensual sex between adults ([SC Observer](https://www.scobserver.in/cases/navtej-singh-johar-v-union-of-india-constitutionality-of-section-377-ipc-background)). This overruled *Suresh Kumar Koushal v Naz Foundation* (2013) ([Judgment, SCI](https://main.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2016/14961/14961_2016_Judgement_06-Sep-2018.pdf)).
> - **Why it's powerful:** it reverses the usual relativist argument. Section 377 was a **British colonial** law, so striking it down can be framed as *decolonising* Indian law rather than "Westernising" it. That challenges the claim that universal rights = Western imposition. Pair it with Upendra Baxi's postcolonial quote in §5.

## 5. Theoretical views on rights and justice

**Command terms:** *evaluate*, *discuss*, *compare*. The full theory is in [[GP — Theoretical Perspectives — Notes]]. Learn here only what each theory says **about rights** (Hodder Table 2.5, p. 106).

| Theory | View of rights and justice | Key quote in Hodder |
|---|---|---|
| **Liberalism** | Rights are held because we are human and exist whether or not society accepts them. The view is individual-centred, and **codification** (writing rights into law and treaties) is the way to secure them. The UDHR exemplifies it | Jack Donnelly: international legal universality is "one of the greatest achievements" of the human rights movement |
| **Marxism** | Rejects natural rights; rights come from social agreements. Capitalism maximises profit at workers' expense, and human rights instruments ignore the structural causes of exploitation | Noam Chomsky: rights have "purely instrumental value… a useful tool for propaganda" |
| **Postcolonialism** | Rights must address real injustice now, and everyone owes justice to the oppressed. It distrusts human rights law as ineffective and designed by elites | Upendra Baxi: see rights "from the standpoint of the historically oppressed groups" |
| **Feminism** | Believes in rights, but sees any set definition as **imperialistic**. It questions individual rights in favour of wider goals such as female emancipation, and judges rights law case by case | Wendy Brown on #MeToo and "the biggest feminist mobilization" in history |
| **Realism** | Left out of the table on purpose. Rights and justice are largely irrelevant because states act on national interest and ethics plays no role | — |

> [!tip] Essay use
> - **Liberalism** is the "official" UN view.
> - **Marxism** and **postcolonialism** ask *whose interests* the rights regime serves.
> - **Realism** explains why states sign rights treaties and then ignore them.

## 6. Justice: meanings and types

**Command terms:** *define*, *explain*, *compare and contrast*, *evaluate*.

**Justice**: an ethical concept about how people, individually or as a society, should and should not be treated. It is usually understood as being treated **fairly** and getting what one **deserves** (Hodder p. 107). It is contested because people disagree about what is deserved. Is clean water owed to everyone, or does it depend on the society? Relativists say it depends.

### 6.1 Social vs political justice (Hodder Table 2.6, pp. 107–108)

| | **Social justice** | **Political justice** |
|---|---|---|
| Concerns | Comparing access across a society or the world: food, water, education, health, housing, safety, work and a living wage | People's power to shape decisions in their society, and fair treatment by police and courts |
| Ideal | Fewer gaps between groups | Participation, fair trials, no persecution for political beliefs |
| Hodder's example | Inequality from local communities up to the global level | **North Korea** has little political justice. Full adult suffrage raises it, but "democracy alone is not sufficient evidence" |

Social and political injustice are often **interdependent**. Hodder uses the **US criminal justice system** (Table 2.7, p. 108), which draws on Census.gov, the Prison Policy Initiative and others:
- Black Americans are **13%** of the population but **38%** of people in prison or jail, about 2.9 times their population share.
- The arrest rate is **6,109 vs 2,795 per 100,000** for Black and white Americans.
- The prison incarceration rate for Indigenous people is **763 per 100,000**, against **350** for the nation as a whole.

### 6.2 Egalitarian justice: John Rawls (Hodder pp. 109–110)
**Egalitarian justice** focuses on social justice. Everyone is owed equal respect and dignity, with no exceptions, and equal freedoms and opportunities.
- Rawls set out **justice as fairness** in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). He chose principles from behind a **veil of ignorance**, so that race, class and gender cannot bias the choice. His aim was to benefit even the **least advantaged** ([SEP, Rawls](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rawls/)).
- **Against utilitarian trade-offs** (Hodder p. 110): "Each person possesses an inviolability founded on justice that even the welfare of society as a whole cannot override." Rawls would therefore reject spending public money only on the most talented students.
- Rawls also held that "A society regulated by a public sense of justice is inherently stable" (Hodder p. 109). This ties justice to peace.

### 6.3 Cosmopolitan justice (Hodder pp. 110–112)
**Cosmopolitan justice** extends egalitarian duties **globally**. Everyone must work towards justice for all individuals, and IGOs such as the UN or the AU must act when needed.
- **Humanitarian intervention** is justified if three conditions hold:
  1. The state is structurally unjust (abusive or failed).
  2. The intervention will succeed.
  3. It is a last resort.

  The full debate is in [[GP Rights & Justice 4 — Debates — Notes]].
- Its test case is **migration**. Borders are central to sovereignty, so states often resist hosting refugees.

> [!example] Case study 5: who carries the refugee burden?
> - **Hodder, citing UNHCR (pp. 111–112):**
>   - 70% of refugees live in countries neighbouring their own, and 76% are hosted by low- and middle-income countries.
>   - In 2022 the USA gave UNHCR **USD 2,195,608,503**, far more than any other donor. Germany was second with USD 536 m.
>   - **Türkiye** hosted **3,673,808** refugees at mid-2022, the most of any country.
>   - India hosted roughly 212,000 refugees and asylum seekers in 2022 (Fig. 2.11). The label in our extract is garbled, so treat this figure as approximate.
> - **Latest UNHCR figures (end-2025):**
>   - **65%** of refugees are hosted in neighbouring countries.
>   - **68%** are hosted by low- and middle-income countries ([UNHCR Global Trends](https://www.unhcr.org/global-trends)).
> - **India** is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention ([FMR](https://www.fmreview.org/issue67/janmyr/)).
> - **Evaluation:** rich states **pay rather than host**. Cosmopolitans call this falling short of the ideal. Realists call it rational sovereignty.

### 6.4 Ecological justice (Hodder p. 112)
**Ecological justice** extends justice to **all living beings**, not only humans. Non-human beings have **"moral considerability"**: we owe them justice, and not only because it benefits us. Its roots are in habitat loss and mass extinction.

> [!example] Case study 6: nature's rights vs Amazon deforestation
> - **Ecuador's 2008 Constitution**, Art. 71: "Nature, or Pacha Mama… has the right to integral respect for its existence and for the maintenance and regeneration of its life cycles" ([Georgetown PDBA text](https://pdba.georgetown.edu/Constitutions/Ecuador/english08.html)). It was the first national constitution to give nature rights ([Ecojurisprudence Monitor](https://ecojurisprudence.org/initiatives/ecuador-constitution/)).
> - **Brazil:** the space agency INPE estimated **13,235 km²** of clear-cut deforestation in the Legal Amazon between Aug 2020 and Jul 2021. That was up about **22%** and the worst figure in over 15 years ([INPE](http://www.obt.inpe.br/OBT/noticias-obt-inpe/estimativa-de-desmatamento-por-corte-raso-na-amazonia-legal-para-2021-e-de-13-235-km2); [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-59341770)).
> - **Use it to show:** ecological justice as an **Indigenous, non-Western** concept written into law, set against sovereign development choices.

### 6.5 Zulu Ubuntu (Hodder p. 113)
**Ubuntu** is a Zulu African philosophy in which **community** is the essential building block of society. **Desmond Tutu** wrote in *No Future Without Forgiveness*: "My humanity is inextricably bound up in yours… We belong in a bundle of life." **Nelson Mandela** said it means enriching the lives of your community, not just your own. Justice under Ubuntu is **restorative** (repair, reconcile, restore dignity). The usual liberal model is **retributive** (punish the individual offender).

> [!example] Case study 7: the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)
> - The TRC was set up by the **Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act 34 of 1995** and chaired by Tutu. It covered gross human rights violations from **1 March 1960 to 10 May 1994** ([Act, DOJ&CD](https://www.justice.gov.za/legislation/acts/1995-034.pdf); [O'Malley Archive](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv02167/04lv02264/05lv02335/06lv02357/07lv02359/08lv02363.htm)).
> - **Amnesty in exchange for full disclosure.** The official summary (1 Nov 2000) records **7,112 applications**, of which **849 were granted (≈11.9%)** and **5,392 refused (≈75.8%)** ([DOJ&CD](https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/amntrans/index.htm); [SAHO](https://sahistory.org.za/article/truth-and-reconciliation-commission-trc-0)).
> - **Ubuntu in court:** in ***S v Makwanyane*** (**6 June 1995**) the Constitutional Court unanimously held the death penalty unconstitutional. The judgment introduced ubuntu into South African law, with its emphasis on reconciliation rather than retribution ([SAFLII](https://www.saflii.org/za/cases/ZACC/1995/3.html); [Himonga et al., PER 2013](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S1727-37812013000500009&script=sci_arttext)).
> - **Evaluation:**
>   - *For:* a non-Western model that put truth and peace first.
>   - *Against:* few of those refused amnesty were later prosecuted ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/south_african_truth_commission)). Victims may have traded *justice* for *peace*.

> [!info] Liberty and equality: the thread through every view
> Hodder ends (pp. 113–114) with liberty and equality in a "delicate balance". Liberty means personal freedom and making your own decisions. Equality means that all people have the same intrinsic value. Each view weights them differently:
> - **Negative rights and liberalism** lean towards liberty.
> - **Social and egalitarian** justice lean towards equality.
> - **Ubuntu** reframes both through community.

## 7. Essay angles for Paper 2

Structure: define the terms, state a thesis, give claims and counterclaims each backed by a *named* case, then reach a judgement. See [[Chapter 1: Foundations of Global Politics]].

> [!example] "To what extent can universal human rights be reconciled with cultural relativism?"
> - **Claim:** "relative universality" is possible (Vienna 1993; *Makwanyane*, which grounded a universal norm in ubuntu; *Navtej*, where decolonising law and universal rights point the same way).
> - **Counterclaim:** the death penalty (Iran 2,159+ executions in 2025) and the Saudi abstention in 1948.
> - **India:** the UCC.
> - **Concepts:** sovereignty, legitimacy.

> [!example] "Compare and contrast positive and negative rights in promoting justice."
> - Negative rights can be enforced by the state *stopping* an action (*Navtej*).
> - Positive rights need the state to spend and act (Art. 21A and the RTE Act).
> - Hodder's prison example and the Vienna Declaration's word "indivisible" show the categories blur.

> [!example] "Evaluate a non-Western conception of justice."
> - **Claim:** Ubuntu and the TRC delivered truth and a peaceful transition. Ecuador shows Indigenous ideas of nature's rights written into law.
> - **Counterclaim:** only 849 of 7,112 amnesty applications were granted and there were few prosecutions, which supports an impunity critique.
> - **Lenses:** postcolonial and feminist.

## 8. Common mistakes / examiner traps

1. **Calling the UDHR binding law.** It is a General Assembly *declaration*. Binding force comes later through treaties and codification: see [[GP Rights & Justice 3 — Nature, Practice and Study — Notes]].
2. **Treating "universal" as "unanimous".** The 1948 vote had **8 abstentions**. Name them.
3. **Treating relativism as a "non-Western bloc".** The USA executed 47 people in 2025. India, South Africa and Ecuador have *expanded* rights.
4. **Presenting negative/positive rights or the three generations as watertight or ranked.** Hodder says they overlap. Vienna 1993 says all rights are "indivisible".
5. **Saying "justice" without saying which kind.** Name it: social, political, egalitarian, cosmopolitan, ecological or Ubuntu.
6. **Mixing up egalitarian and cosmopolitan justice.** Egalitarian justice (Rawls) applies *within* a society. Cosmopolitan justice applies *across borders*.
7. **Leaving realism out.** Its dismissal of rights is a strong counterclaim, not a gap in your essay.
8. **Loose figures.** The TRC granted **849** amnesties, not "about 1,200". The UN says the Commission had **18** members, not 19.
9. **Confusing negative *liberty* with negative *rights*.** Liberty is a freedom. A right is an entitlement that places a duty on someone else.

## 9. Self-test

1. Distinguish positive from negative rights, giving one UDHR article and one Indian example of each.
2. Name Vasak's three generations of rights and the slogan behind them. Why is it wrong to call them a ranking?
3. What was the UNGA vote on the UDHR, and how can it be used in a universalism vs relativism essay?
4. Distinguish egalitarian from cosmopolitan justice, using Rawls and refugee-hosting data.
5. Explain how Ubuntu shaped justice in post-apartheid South Africa, with two verified facts and one criticism.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. **Negative rights** require those with power to *refrain*. Examples: UDHR Art. 5 (no torture); in India, *Navtej Singh Johar* (6 Sept 2018), where the state had to stop criminalising consensual same-sex relations. **Positive rights** require the state to *act or provide*. Examples: UDHR Art. 26 (education); in India, Art. 21A (86th Amendment, 2002) and the RTE Act, in force from 1 Apr 2010 with a 25% private-school quota. Credit the overlap shown by Hodder's prison example.
> 2. **1st generation, liberty:** civil and political rights. **2nd, equality:** economic, social and cultural rights. **3rd, fraternity:** collective or solidarity rights such as development and a clean environment. The slogan is the French Revolution's *liberté, égalité, fraternité*, and Vasak set it out in the *UNESCO Courier* in 1977. It is not a ranking: the UDHR already held 2nd-generation rights in 1948, and Vienna (1993) calls all rights "indivisible and interdependent".
> 3. **48 in favour, 0 against, 8 abstentions** (10 Dec 1948, Res. 217 A (III)). The abstainers were six Soviet-bloc states, Saudi Arabia and South Africa. The "universal" standard was contested from the start on ideological grounds (Soviet bloc), cultural and religious grounds (Saudi Arabia) and self-interested grounds (apartheid South Africa).
> 4. **Egalitarian justice** means equal respect and opportunity *within* a society. Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) uses the veil of ignorance and holds that individual "inviolability" cannot be overridden by the general welfare. **Cosmopolitan justice** extends those duties *globally*: IGOs must act, and intervention is permitted if the state is structurally unjust, success is likely and it is a last resort. Test case: 65% of refugees are hosted by neighbouring states and 68% by low- and middle-income states (UNHCR, end-2025). Rich donors such as the USA (about USD 2.2 bn to UNHCR in 2022) pay more than they host.
> 5. Ubuntu ("my humanity is inextricably bound up in yours") stands for **restorative**, community-centred justice. The **TRC** (Act 34 of 1995, chaired by Tutu, covering 1960–94) received **7,112** amnesty applications and granted **849**. ***S v Makwanyane*** (6 June 1995) unanimously struck down the death penalty and introduced ubuntu into law. **Criticism:** few of those refused amnesty were prosecuted, so victims may have given up justice for peace.
