# Chapter 5: Interdependence

> **Global Politics HL — post-SA1 chapter.** Interdependence is the fourth core key concept. Like Legitimacy, it is not the primary focus of your 29 September 2026 SA1, but you should understand it well enough to invoke in a Paper 2 essay and use it as an integrating concept across the thematic studies.
>
> **What this chapter does.** Fixes the definition (Keohane & Nye), catalogues the dimensions of interdependence, distinguishes sensitivity from vulnerability, and shows how interdependence interlocks with power (weaponised interdependence) and sovereignty (interdependence erodes and reshapes both). Cross-references [[chapter 2 — Power]] and [[chapter 3 — Sovereignty]].

---

## Section 5.1 — Defining Interdependence

**Working definition** (IB-aligned). Interdependence refers to the **mutual reliance** between and among actors on access to resources, systems, or arrangements. Globalisation has *increased* interdependence and, in doing so, has changed the power relationships between actors.

The concept was developed by **Robert Keohane** and **Joseph Nye** in *Power and Interdependence* (1977) as the liberal counter to realist state-centrism. Their key claim: in a highly interconnected world, force loses relevance and institutions gain it — because the costs of using force become prohibitive when both sides depend on the other.

---

## Section 5.2 — Dimensions of Interdependence

Interdependence isn't one thing. It runs across at least six dimensions:

| Dimension | What flows | Illustrative case |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Trade, investment, supply chains, capital | India imports ~85% of crude oil — a Gulf crisis is an Indian domestic crisis. Apple's supply chain sourced from ~43 countries pre-2024 with China dominating final assembly. |
| **Security** | Alliance commitments, intelligence, non-proliferation | NATO Article 5 mutual defence. Five Eyes intelligence sharing. Russia-Ukraine → European gas crisis → global food price spike via Ukrainian grain exports. |
| **Environmental** | Emissions, ecosystems, weather | India's emissions affect Bangladesh's flood risk. Amazon deforestation affects global rainfall patterns. Climate change is the paradigmatic case: no state can solve it alone. |
| **Health** | Pathogens, medical supplies, workforce | COVID-19 — a market in Wuhan shut down the global economy in weeks. Vaccine supply chains and mRNA IP concentrated in a few states. |
| **Informational** | Data, platforms, media | A Facebook algorithm change reshapes political discourse across 190 countries simultaneously. TikTok content moderation in Beijing affects US election dynamics. |
| **Technological** | Standards, IP, compute, AI models | The chip supply chain (US-Netherlands-Taiwan-China) is the sharpest 2020s interdependence — and the sharpest current vulnerability. |

## Section 5.3 — Sensitivity vs Vulnerability (Keohane & Nye)

**The classic distinction** — sharpen it and you have a Criterion-A move.

- **Sensitivity** — how *quickly* changes in one country produce costly effects in another.
- **Vulnerability** — how *costly it is to adjust* to those changes.

**Example.** Both India and China are highly *sensitive* to Middle East oil supply disruptions — prices spike within days. But India is more *vulnerable* than China because India lacks the sovereign wealth reserves and long-term contracts with Russia/Saudi that China has secured; India's adjustment options are narrower.

**Asymmetric interdependence** is common. Small states are typically more *vulnerable* to great powers than vice versa. The US-China relationship is deeply interdependent but the vulnerabilities are asymmetric — decoupling hurts both, but not equally.

## Section 5.4 — Weaponised Interdependence

A 2020s update to Keohane and Nye. Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman (2019, "Weaponized Interdependence") showed that interdependence is not just a *pacifier* (the liberal view) but also a *weapon*. Actors that sit at network chokepoints can coerce others through those very connections.

**Two mechanisms:**

- **Panopticon effect** — network positions grant surveillance access. US-based SWIFT, cloud platforms, and undersea cable landings give the US visibility into global flows.
- **Chokepoint effect** — network positions grant the ability to deny access. Cutting Russia from SWIFT (March 2022); US export controls on advanced chips to China (2022–); China's rare-earth export controls in response.

**Implication for realists.** Interdependence is not the peace-producer liberalism suggested. It creates new coercive tools. The lesson for smaller states: dependence is exploitable, so *strategic autonomy* becomes rational.

**Implication for liberals.** Institutions must be robust and jointly governed to prevent the network from becoming one actor's weapon.

## Section 5.5 — Interdependence, Power, and Sovereignty

Interdependence interlocks with the other three core concepts.

- **Interdependence ↔ Power.** Structural power (Ch 2, §2.3) works *through* interdependence — the US dollar is powerful because global finance depends on it. Take away the interdependence, take away the structural power.
- **Interdependence ↔ Sovereignty.** Interdependence *erodes* sovereignty by making states less able to control what enters and leaves their territory (Ch 3, §3.5). But it also creates *new* sovereignty projects — digital sovereignty, food sovereignty, energy sovereignty — that try to selectively reduce vulnerability.
- **Interdependence ↔ Legitimacy.** A government that fails to manage interdependence (COVID missteps, oil price shocks, food inflation) can lose domestic legitimacy quickly.

**Exam move.** In any question naming interdependence, at least one paragraph should invoke either power or sovereignty (and ideally both).

## Section 5.6 — Live 2026 Cases

### 5.6.1 The chip supply chain

The sharpest live case of interdependence *and* asymmetric vulnerability *and* structural power. See Ch 2, §2.9 Case 1 for the power framing. In interdependence terms: everyone depends on Taiwan for advanced chips; TSMC's ~90% market share in leading-edge nodes means one island's fate constrains the entire global tech economy. This is why Taiwan matters far beyond its 24-million population.

### 5.6.2 Russia's 2022 gas / grain weaponisation

Russia sought to leverage European gas dependence into political concession. It largely failed (Europe accelerated LNG imports, gas storage, renewables) but at high cost. Ukraine's disrupted grain exports caused a global food-price spike that hit low-income food-importing states hardest — asymmetric vulnerability at the interdependence-security intersection.

### 5.6.3 BRICS+ de-dollarisation as vulnerability reduction

The 2026 BRICS de-dollarisation push (Ch 2, §2.9 Case 3) is a coordinated attempt to reduce vulnerability to US financial structural power. Whether it succeeds is uncertain; that it is attempted at all reflects post-2022 learning about vulnerability.

### 5.6.4 EU's 2026 strategic autonomy

The Draghi report (2024) and EU 2026 defence-industrial-base initiatives (Ch 3, §3.9.5) are explicit vulnerability-reduction projects — reducing dependence on US security guarantees that Trump 2.0 made unreliable.

### 5.6.5 India's 85% oil import dependence

Structurally exposed. India has diversified sources (Russia post-2022 at discounted prices; Gulf; US; Africa) but the underlying vulnerability remains. Explains why India's Middle East diplomacy is so active — the interdependence is the constraint.

## Section 5.7 — Exam-Ready Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Interdependence** | The mutual reliance between and among actors on access to resources, systems, or arrangements. |
| **Sensitivity** (Keohane & Nye) | How quickly changes in one country produce costly effects in another. |
| **Vulnerability** (Keohane & Nye) | How costly it is for a country to adjust to changes elsewhere. |
| **Asymmetric interdependence** | Interdependence in which one side is more sensitive or vulnerable than the other. |
| **Complex interdependence** | A world with multiple channels connecting societies, no fixed hierarchy of issues, and reduced role for military force (Keohane & Nye). |
| **Weaponised interdependence** (Farrell & Newman) | Using network chokepoints to coerce others through the very connections that bind actors together. |
| **Chokepoint effect** | Actors at network centre can deny others access (SWIFT ejection; chip export controls; rare-earth controls). |
| **Panopticon effect** | Actors at network centre gain surveillance visibility across the network. |
| **Strategic autonomy** | A state's or bloc's attempt to selectively reduce vulnerability by rebuilding domestic capacity or diversifying dependencies. |
| **Decoupling** | Deliberate reduction of economic interdependence between two actors (US-China tech decoupling since ~2018). |
| **De-risking** | A softer version of decoupling — reducing dependence on a single supplier in specific sensitive areas, not full decoupling. Von der Leyen's term. |

## Section 5.8 — Common Exam Traps

1. **Treating interdependence as symmetric.** It rarely is. Always ask *who* is more sensitive and *who* is more vulnerable.
2. **Conflating sensitivity with vulnerability.** Sensitivity = speed of impact; vulnerability = cost of adjustment. Master this distinction.
3. **Treating interdependence as inherently pacifying.** Weaponised interdependence shows the same networks can be coercive.
4. **Missing the interdependence-sovereignty link.** Interdependence erodes traditional sovereignty but also generates new sovereignty projects (digital, data, food, energy).
5. **Case studies without dates.** "Global supply chains are interdependent" earns nothing. "TSMC produces ~90% of leading-edge chips; US Aug 2022 CHIPS Act; sequential export controls 2022–24, partial recalibration 2026" is usable evidence.
6. **Only economic interdependence.** Health, information, environment, security dimensions matter equally in the 2020s.
