## Section 4.1 — Defining Legitimacy

**Working definition** (IB-aligned). Legitimacy refers to an actor or an action that is **commonly considered acceptable** by a relevant population. It provides the *rationale* for governance and for exercising power.

Three properties to hold in mind:

1. **Legitimacy is granted, not claimed.** An actor can *assert* legitimacy, but it only exists when others *accept* it. Legitimacy is inter-subjective.
2. **Legitimacy is contested.** What counts as legitimate to one audience may be illegitimate to another. Every ruler is legitimate to their supporters and illegitimate to their opponents; the analytical question is *how much* of the relevant population accepts them.
3. **Legitimacy is dynamic.** It can be gained, extended, eroded, or lost — sometimes within weeks (Arab Spring collapses; Putin's post-invasion legitimacy at home; Ashraf Ghani's 24-hour collapse in Kabul, August 2021).

## Section 4.2 — Sources of Legitimacy

An actor can draw on more than one source. Max Weber's 1919 classification remains the standard starting point, updated with modern additions.

| Source | Basis | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Democratic / constitutional (rational-legal)** | Free and fair elections, rule of law, constitutional constraints | Elected governments of India, UK, Germany, Japan |
| **Traditional / hereditary** | History, continuity, custom | UK monarchy, Saudi Arabia, Bhutan, Thailand |
| **Charismatic** | Personal quality and moral authority | Nelson Mandela; Gandhi; MLK; Zelensky in wartime |
| **Performance-based / output legitimacy** | Delivering economic growth, security, stability | Chinese Communist Party ("performance legitimacy"); Singapore under PAP |
| **International recognition** | UN membership, diplomatic recognition, treaty participation | Newly-independent states in the post-1945 wave; Palestine as UN Observer State |
| **Ideological / revolutionary** | Alignment with a foundational ideology or revolutionary project | Iran's Islamic Republic; Cuba; historically the USSR |

**Modern additions to Weber:**
- **Procedural legitimacy** — decisions are seen as legitimate because they followed the correct process, even if the outcome is unpopular. Courts, UN Security Council votes rely on this.
- **Deliberative legitimacy** — decisions gain legitimacy through inclusive deliberation, not just voting. Habermas's contribution.

## Section 4.3 — Legitimacy vs Legality vs Morality — Don't Conflate Them

An easy exam trap.

- **Legality** — what is permitted by law. Formal, codified, testable in courts.
- **Legitimacy** — what a relevant population accepts as appropriate. Political, contested, evolves.
- **Morality** — what is ethically right by some standard (rights-based, consequentialist, virtue-based, religious). Philosophical.

**These three often diverge:**

| Case | Legal? | Legitimate? | Moral? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P5 UN Security Council veto** | Yes (UN Charter) | Increasingly contested (privileges 1945 victors) | Debated |
| **Gandhi's Salt March (1930)** | No (broke British salt tax law) | Yes (to Indian public, growing global audience) | Yes (per most ethical frameworks) |
| **US-led 2003 Iraq invasion** | Contested (no explicit UNSC authorisation) | Contested (fractured domestic and global publics) | Widely regarded as unjust |
| **Russia's 2022 Ukraine invasion** | Illegal (UN Charter Article 2(4)) | Illegitimate to 141 UNGA-condemning states; legitimate to Kremlin base | Regarded as unjust by most frameworks |
| **Civil disobedience — MLK's civil rights actions** | Illegal at the time | Legitimate to a majority in retrospect | Yes |

**Exam move.** Any question involving controversial state action can be sharpened by mapping it across all three dimensions.

## Section 4.4 — Legitimacy in Action — Cases

### Case 4.4.1 — The Taliban and international recognition (2021–2026)

The Taliban regained control of Afghanistan in August 2021 after the US withdrawal. They have effective control (de facto sovereignty) but no widespread diplomatic recognition. Multiple states (China, Russia, UAE, Pakistan, Iran) have engaged pragmatically without formal recognition; Western states have withheld recognition on human rights grounds (particularly the treatment of women and girls). This is **power without international legitimacy** — showing that de facto control and international legitimacy can diverge.

### Case 4.4.2 — Russia's 2024 presidential election

March 2024 election: Putin won 87% of the vote in an election in which the main opposition candidate (Boris Nadezhdin) was disqualified and Alexei Navalny was dead in an Arctic prison. The Kremlin claims **democratic legitimacy** through the vote. Most Western states, election observers, and NGOs dispute the claim — opposition was excluded, imprisoned, or dead. Domestic legitimacy is strong in a Kremlin-dominated media environment; international legitimacy is thin. Illustrates the *audience-specific* nature of legitimacy claims.

### Case 4.4.3 — Chinese Communist Party performance legitimacy

The CCP does not hold competitive multi-party elections and does not claim democratic legitimacy. Instead, it claims **performance legitimacy**: three decades of unprecedented economic growth (~10% GDP growth for 30 years), 800 million lifted out of poverty, industrial and infrastructure transformation. As growth slows in the 2020s (property overhang, demographic ageing), performance legitimacy becomes more precarious — a live 2020s question.

### Case 4.4.4 — The Arab Spring (2011) — rapid legitimacy collapse

Ben Ali (Tunisia), Mubarak (Egypt), Gaddafi (Libya), Saleh (Yemen) were all overthrown or forced out within months. Regimes that appeared stable for decades revealed that their legitimacy was thinner than it looked — held together by fear and patronage more than genuine acceptance. Illustrates how quickly legitimacy can collapse once fear breaks.

### Case 4.4.5 — Ashraf Ghani's 24-hour collapse (August 2021)

The Afghan government, backed by the US and with a legally recognised president, collapsed in about 24 hours as the Taliban advanced. Ghani fled. Illustrates that **de jure legitimacy without domestic legitimacy is brittle** — no state was more internationally recognised in 2021 than the Afghan government; almost no Afghans defended it.

### Case 4.4.6 — Contested elections and democratic backsliding (2020s)

Trump's 2020 election-denial and January 6, 2021 Capitol events showed even the paradigm liberal democracy is not immune to legitimacy crises. Hungary, Turkey, and India (Freedom House 2023 downgrade to "partly free") illustrate that democratic legitimacy is not a one-way ratchet.

## Section 4.5 — The Power–Sovereignty–Legitimacy Triangle

The three concepts form a triangle, and the interactions are exam gold.

- **Power without legitimacy** is fragile. The Taliban has power without international legitimacy; sanctions and isolation constrain it.
- **Legitimacy without power** is symbolic. A recognised government in exile (Rojava intermittently; Tibetan government-in-exile) has legitimacy claims but limited effective power.
- **Sovereignty without legitimacy** is contested. Russia's staged 2022 annexations produced formal sovereignty claims without international legitimacy.
- **Legitimacy grants sovereignty durability.** A widely-recognised, internally-popular state (Norway, Costa Rica, New Zealand) can sustain sovereignty at very low cost — no army, no fortress, no coercion needed.

**Exam move.** Any P2 essay engaging one of these concepts benefits from mapping the relationship to the other two.

## Section 4.6 — Exam-Ready Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Legitimacy** | The acceptability of an actor or action to a relevant population; provides rationale for governance and for exercising power. |
| **Democratic legitimacy** | Legitimacy grounded in free and fair elections, rule of law, and constitutional constraints. |
| **Charismatic legitimacy** (Weber) | Legitimacy grounded in personal quality and moral authority. |
| **Traditional legitimacy** (Weber) | Legitimacy grounded in history, continuity, and custom. |
| **Rational-legal legitimacy** (Weber) | Legitimacy grounded in impersonal, codified rules and procedures. |
| **Performance / output legitimacy** | Legitimacy grounded in delivering results (growth, stability, security) rather than procedural authority. |
| **Procedural legitimacy** | Legitimacy grounded in decisions having followed correct process, regardless of outcome. |
| **Deliberative legitimacy** (Habermas) | Legitimacy grounded in inclusive deliberation, not just voting. |
| **Legality** | What is permitted by law — formal, codified, testable. |
| **Morality** | What is ethically right by some standard — philosophical. |

## Section 4.7 — Common Exam Traps

1. **Conflating legitimacy with legality.** They diverge — Gandhi's Salt March was illegal but legitimate; the P5 veto is legal but increasingly illegitimate.
2. **Treating legitimacy as objective.** It is *audience-specific*. Putin has legitimacy in Kremlin domestic media environments and none in Ukrainian ones.
3. **Assuming democratic legitimacy is the only legitimate form.** Traditional (monarchies), performance (CCP), charismatic (Mandela) all produce genuine legitimacy claims that populations accept.
4. **Missing performance legitimacy for authoritarian regimes.** Absent this, essays on China, Singapore, or the UAE lose analytical purchase.
5. **Failing to distinguish domestic from international legitimacy.** They diverge (Taliban strong domestically in parts, weak internationally; ICC-warranted leaders vice versa).
6. **Static analysis.** Legitimacy evolves. Regimes that looked stable (pre-Arab Spring) can collapse; new claims (Palestine, Kosovo) can consolidate.
