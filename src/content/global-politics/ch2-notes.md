## Section 2.1 — Defining Power

**Working definition** (IB-aligned). Power is the **ability to affect change** — to make actors do (or not do) something they otherwise would not, or to shape the environment in which choices are made.

Three properties of power to hold in mind at all times:

1. **Contested.** No universally agreed definition. Every essay should state which conception you're using.
2. **Relational.** Power is not a *thing* an actor "has." It's a relationship *between* actors, and it varies with context. The US has enormous military power globally but limited power to influence what OPEC+ does with oil quotas. India has significant regional influence but constrained global institutional power at the IMF.
3. **Dynamic.** Power distributions *shift.* Every essay should acknowledge that today's power balance is not permanent — the current transition from US-led unipolarity to contested multipolarity is a live example.

### Six canonical definitions worth naming

| Thinker | Core definition |
|---|---|
| **Max Weber** | The ability to impose one's will over others despite resistance. |
| **Robert Dahl** (1957) | A has power over B to the extent A can get B to do something B would not otherwise do. |
| **Steven Lukes** (1974) | Power operates on three faces: overt decision-making, agenda-setting, and preference-shaping. |
| **Joseph Nye** (1990, 2004) | The ability to affect others to get the outcomes you want — through coercion, payment, or attraction. |
| **Susan Strange** (1988) | Power is not just relational (A over B) but *structural* — control over the frameworks in which everyone operates. |
| **Michel Foucault** | Power is diffuse, capillary, everywhere — circulated through discourse and institutions, not "held" by actors. |

**Exam move.** In any Paper 2 essay on power, quote at least one of these definitions and explain which aspect it captures that others miss.

## Section 2.2 — Classification 1: Hard, Soft, and Smart Power (Joseph Nye)

Joseph Nye Jr. (Harvard) is the most-cited contemporary theorist of power. He first named **soft power** in *Bound to Lead* (1990) and elaborated it in *Soft Power* (2004). The framework is IB gold — you will use it in almost every essay on power.

### Nye's three ways to affect others

1. **Threats** (sticks) — hard power.
2. **Payments** (carrots) — also hard power, using economic inducements.
3. **Attraction** (co-option) — soft power. Getting others to *want* what you want.

**The key insight.** **Command power** (making others do things through coercion or payment) is not the only kind of power. **Co-optive power** — shaping preferences so others align with you voluntarily — is often more efficient, more durable, and cheaper. If you can attract, you don't have to coerce.

### Hard power

Coercion via military force or economic sanctions.

**Cases:**
- US freezing ~$300B in Russian foreign reserves (post-Feb 2022 invasion).
- India's cross-border strikes after Uri (2016) and Balakot (2019).
- US "maximum pressure" sanctions on Iran (2018–), Venezuela, North Korea.
- The 2022–24 Western sanctions regime on Russia (SWIFT ejection, oil price cap, tech export controls).
- **2026 example — US semiconductor export controls on China.** Sequential tightening from 2022 to 2024; partially cooled in early 2026 under a Trump-era recalibration but the architecture remains. Denies advanced chips and manufacturing equipment to Chinese firms as an economic-coercion tool aimed at retarding China's AI capability.

### Soft power

Attraction and persuasion via culture, ideology, and legitimate foreign policy.

**Nye's three primary sources of a country's soft power:**

1. **Culture** — where it's attractive to others. Hollywood, K-pop, Bollywood, the English language, universities (Harvard, Oxford, IITs) that pull global talent, cuisine (Italian, Japanese, Indian), fashion (Paris, Milan). Culture as soft power works only when others find it appealing — it isn't automatic.
2. **Political values** — when the country lives up to them at home and abroad. US free-speech norms are soft power when the US practises them consistently. The Nordic countries' commitment to gender equality is soft power because they demonstrably live it. Hypocrisy destroys this source instantly — Guantánamo and Abu Ghraib gutted US soft power for a generation.
3. **Foreign policies** — when they're seen as legitimate and moral. Coalition-building through the UN before the 1991 Gulf War preserved US soft power. Unilateral action without UN sanction (2003 Iraq invasion) destroyed it.

**Cases:**
- **K-pop and Korean cinema** → South Korea's cultural soft power (BTS, *Squid Game*, *Parasite*).
- **India's soft-power toolkit** — yoga diplomacy (International Yoga Day passed at UN, 2014), Bollywood exports, the ~32-million-strong global diaspora, IITs, Ayurveda branding.
- **Ukraine's 2022 soft power surge** — Zelensky-led narrative winning global sympathy and material support despite Ukraine's smaller absolute power.
- **China's Confucius Institutes and CGTN** — heavy investment; results contested. Blocked in liberal democracies (many US universities closed Confucius Institutes 2019–22) while gaining ground in the Global South.

### Smart power

The deliberate combination of hard and soft power — not just mixing them, but **strategic intelligence** about which type to deploy for which situation. When to use force. When to persuade. When to build institutions. When to walk away.

- Hillary Clinton adopted "smart power" as a Secretary of State framework in 2009.
- **The Marshall Plan (1948)** — economic aid + strategic alignment. Textbook smart power.
- **The Belt and Road Initiative (2013–)** — Chinese loans + infrastructure + political leverage. A smart-power project even critics acknowledge.
- **US backing of Ukraine (2022–26)** — arms + intelligence + Zelensky-narrative amplification + economic sanctions coordination. All three at once.

### Nye's power conversion

Countries have **power resources** (population, GDP, military spending, cultural exports) that must be *converted* into **power outcomes** (achieving what they want). The conversion is not automatic. A country with vast resources can fail (Vietnam-era US, USSR, contemporary Russia in Ukraine); a country with modest resources can punch above its weight (Norway on climate and peace diplomacy; Singapore on trade governance). **Strategy** and **legitimacy** are what convert resources into outcomes.

### The limits of soft power

- **Slow.** Cultural attraction builds over decades, not years. You can't manufacture it in a crisis.
- **Uncontrollable.** Governments can't dictate what makes their culture attractive. Hollywood isn't a State Department instrument even if it *produces* soft power.
- **Contextual.** What's attractive in one country is repellent in another. Iran finds Western liberalism threatening, not attractive.
- **Vulnerable to backlash.** Perceived cultural imperialism (McDonald's-isation, English hegemony) generates counter-movements.

### Hard vs Soft — the trade-off table

|  | Hard power | Soft power |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Coercion, payment | Attraction, persuasion |
| **Speed** | Fast (sanctions bite in weeks; wars in days) | Slow (decades to build reputation) |
| **Cost per outcome** | High (military, economic disruption) | Low once built, but building it is expensive |
| **Reversibility** | High (sanctions can be lifted; wars can end) | Low (destroyed reputation takes decades to rebuild) |
| **Precision** | Targetable (specific sanctions, specific strikes) | Diffuse (attracts many, targets few) |
| **Legitimacy costs** | Often high (backlash, resentment) | Low if authentic; catastrophic if faked |

**Nye's rule of thumb.** The more you rely on hard power, the more soft power you *lose* over time. Bullies aren't loved. And countries that are only loved aren't respected. The strategic art is knowing when each is needed — the smart-power move.

## Section 2.3 — Classification 2: Structural Power (Susan Strange)

British political economist **Susan Strange** (*States and Markets*, 1988) argued that Nye's framework misses the deepest form of power: control over **the rules of the system**, not just outcomes within it. Anyone who sets the rules by which everyone else plays doesn't need to coerce them individually — they've *pre-arranged* the outcomes.

### Strange's four structural power domains

| Structure | Who controls it (broadly) | Example of power exerted |
|---|---|---|
| **Security** | The state that provides / withholds military protection | US alliance system (NATO, US-Japan, AUKUS); China's expanding regional deterrence |
| **Production** | Whoever sets terms of manufacturing and trade | WTO rules; MNC supply-chain choke-points; China's dominance of rare-earth processing |
| **Finance** | Whoever controls credit creation and reserves | US dollar as reserve currency; SWIFT; IMF conditionality; BRICS attempted alternatives |
| **Knowledge** | Whoever controls information, technology, and belief systems | Global academic English; Silicon Valley platform standards; US chip design (ASML, TSMC, Nvidia) |

### Live 2026 examples of structural power

- **The US dollar system.** Roughly 58% of global reserves and ~88% of forex-trade turnover are dollar-denominated. This is structural power in the finance domain — the US can sanction any actor with a bank that touches the dollar system. Freezing Russia's reserves in 2022 was possible *only* because of dollar structural power.
- **SWIFT.** The messaging backbone of international bank transfers. Cutting Russian banks off SWIFT (March 2022) was structural coercion — nothing about the technical infrastructure had to physically change; the *rule* did.
- **Semiconductor structural power.** US, Netherlands (ASML), and Taiwan (TSMC) together control the advanced-chip production stack. This is the highest-leverage structural chokepoint of the 2020s — the US extends export controls into Chinese production because ASML lithography machines and TSMC fabrication capacity are hard structural chokes.
- **ICANN and internet governance.** Root domain servers, IP allocation, and standards bodies are still disproportionately US-influenced.
- **BRICS+ dedollarisation push (2026).** The 2026 BRICS summit prep foregrounded de-dollarisation strategies — bilateral currency swaps, cross-border payment systems, discussions of a BRICS unit of account. This is an explicit *contest* over structural power in finance. Realists say it will fail; liberals say it will incrementally erode dollar hegemony; constructivists say the *idea* of dedollarisation matters even if the mechanics lag.

### Structural vs relational — the sharp contrast

- **Relational power.** A gets B to do X in a specific transaction (trade negotiation, UN veto, arms deal).
- **Structural power.** A shapes the environment so that B's choices are already constrained toward what A wants — without any specific bargaining.

Relational power is easier to see and count. Structural power is more consequential.

## Section 2.4 — Classification 3: Power OVER / TO / WITH / WITHIN (Rowlands / Follett)

A framework distinct from Nye and Strange, from **Mary Parker Follett** (1920s) and developed by **Jo Rowlands** (*Questioning Empowerment*, 1997). Now standard in development studies, feminist theory, and IB Global Politics materials (Oxfam's global-programme framework uses it directly). It reframes power away from "who dominates whom" toward "what can be done, and by whom."

### The four types

**1. Power OVER** — the traditional, coercive form. One actor exercises power *over* another, extracting compliance through threat, force, or authority. Fundamentally *zero-sum*: my gain is your loss. Aligns roughly with Nye's hard power and Lukes' first face.
- Examples: an authoritarian government suppressing dissent; an occupying army over a population; a colonial administration; an abusive corporation over gig workers.

**2. Power TO** — the *capacity* or ability of an actor to act, to create, to achieve. Generative rather than coercive. About *agency* and *possibility*. Power *to* is what you gain when you acquire a skill, a resource, a legal right, an education. It does not require dominating anyone.
- Examples: literacy programmes give women the power *to* read contracts; land rights give indigenous communities the power *to* refuse extraction; voter registration gives citizens the power *to* participate; access to microfinance gives entrepreneurs the power *to* start a business.

**3. Power WITH** — *collective* power built through solidarity, cooperation, coalition-building. Not zero-sum: two actors combining their power *with* each other produce more than either had alone. The foundation of social movements, unions, alliances.
- Examples: the global climate movement pooling advocacy across NGOs, cities, and youth activists; India's farmers' protest movement (2020–21) that united across state and caste lines; the Fridays for Future coalition; South Africa's anti-apartheid Congress Alliance; NATO as a state-level "power with" alliance.

**4. Power WITHIN** — the internal dimension: *self-worth, self-knowledge, sense of one's own agency and dignity*. Rooted in identity and consciousness. A person or community that lacks power *within* cannot exercise power *to*, cannot form power *with*, and remains vulnerable to power *over*. Feminist and community-empowerment traditions treat this as the foundation for all other kinds.
- Examples: consciousness-raising in the women's movement of the 1970s; Ambedkar's insistence that Dalit self-respect had to precede political emancipation; Black consciousness and Steve Biko's writings; trauma-informed community work in post-conflict zones (Rwanda, Bosnia); indigenous language revitalisation as a power-within intervention.

### Why this framework matters for GP

- **It expands who counts as a political actor.** Realism and Nye focus on states and (in Nye's case) national attractiveness. The Follett/Rowlands framework brings *communities, movements, and individuals* into the analytical frame — essential for the IA (Engagement Project) which is almost always about people trying to build power *to*, *with*, or *within*.
- **It reframes empowerment.** Development projects that only give people resources (power *to*) without building solidarity (*with*) or self-worth (*within*) tend to fail. The three positive forms reinforce each other.
- **It reveals that "power over" isn't the whole story.** A government can hold immense power *over* its population and still lose to a mobilised civil society that builds power *with* and power *within* — as in Sudan 2019 (Bashir overthrown), or the Arab Spring 2011.

### Contrast with Nye

Nye's typology is fundamentally about *states* and how they get outcomes in the international system. The Follett/Rowlands typology works across *scales* — individuals, communities, movements, states — and is neutral about whether power operates through attraction or coercion. In a Paper 2 essay, invoking both frameworks together is a strong analytical move: use Nye for the state-level analysis (US soft power in Vietnam vs Iraq), Follett/Rowlands for the sub-state and non-state dimensions (Vietnamese civil society's power *within*; the anti-war movement's power *with*).

## Section 2.5 — Classification 4: How Power Operates — Lukes' Three Faces

Steven Lukes' *Power: A Radical View* (1974) is the standard reference for *how* power is exercised. Every IB essay on power benefits from naming at least the third face.

- **First face — decision-making power.** Direct coercion or influence over decisions. A state vetoes a UN resolution. A CEO fires an employee. **Visible, measurable.** Robert Dahl's original conception of power (1957) sits here.
- **Second face — agenda-setting power.** Deciding what gets discussed at all. The US kept the Vietnam War off the UN agenda for years. Fossil-fuel lobbies delayed climate action for decades by shaping what counted as "reasonable" policy debate. **Non-decisions can be exercises of power** — Bachrach and Baratz (1962) added this face to Dahl.
- **Third face — ideological / preference-shaping power.** Convincing others their interests align with yours, so no coercion is needed. Advertising, socialisation, "manufactured consent" (Herman & Chomsky). Post-1945 US influence over global norms of democracy and free markets is a classic example. So is Chinese state framing of "harmonious society" domestically. Foucault's diffuse-power view sits here.

The third face is the hardest to detect and the most consequential. **If you can shape what people *want*, you don't need to force them to comply.** This is the deepest form of soft power and structural power.

### Applying Lukes to a single case — the US dollar system

- **First face.** US Treasury freezes Russian reserves. Iran cut off from banking. Direct, visible.
- **Second face.** Dollar dominance keeps alternative currencies off the agenda in most G7 discussions — a de-dollarisation policy debate barely existed pre-2022 despite being technically obvious for decades.
- **Third face.** Global oil is priced in dollars, global reserves are dollar-denominated, and this is treated as *natural* rather than a choice. The dollar system is naturalised — that's third-face power.

## Section 2.6 — Distribution of Power: Polarity

**Polarity** describes the number of major power centres in the international system:

| Type | Definition | Historical / current examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Unipolar** | One dominant power | Post-1991 to roughly the 2008 GFC — US as sole superpower (Krauthammer's "unipolar moment") |
| **Bipolar** | Two roughly balanced great powers | Cold War (1945–1991): US vs USSR |
| **Multipolar** | Three or more comparable power centres | Pre-WWI Europe; arguably today with US, China, EU, India, Russia + regional powers (Brazil, Turkey, Indonesia) |
| **Non-polar** (Richard Haass) | Power diffused across many state and non-state actors, no clear hierarchy | Argued for 2020s — MNCs, cities, NGOs, and networks wield significant power alongside states |

### The current shift — contested multipolarity

Most scholars agree we're transitioning from a US-led unipolar order to a *contested multipolarity*. Evidence:

- China's GDP now ~75% of US GDP nominal; already larger at purchasing-power parity.
- **BRICS+ expansion (Jan 2024)** — Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE joined the original five (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa). The 2026 summit pushed further with de-dollarisation on the agenda.
- India as the world's fifth-largest economy, expected third by 2027–2030.
- The erosion of dollar hegemony at the margins — yuan-settled Russian oil, BRICS payment systems.
- **US retrenchment signals** — Trump 2.0 (2025–) reduced Ukraine commitments, questioned NATO obligations, catalysed the EU's "2026 independence moment" and Draghi-report defence build-up.

### Realist vs liberal readings of polarity

- **Realists** (Waltz, Mearsheimer) — bipolarity is most stable (clear balance); multipolarity is most conflict-prone (many uncertain alignments); unipolarity is transitional (challengers rise).
- **Liberals** — polarity matters less than institutions and interdependence. A multipolar world dense with cooperation can be peaceful; a bipolar world with no rules can be catastrophic.

## Section 2.7 — Indicators of Power

Power isn't one thing — you'd use different indicators depending on the *type* of power you're describing:

| Dimension | Indicators |
|---|---|
| **Military** | Defence spending (SIPRI data); nuclear arsenal (SIPRI/FAS); force projection capability (aircraft carriers, foreign bases); alliance network |
| **Economic** | GDP (nominal and PPP); share of global trade; reserve currency status; FDI flows; supply-chain centrality |
| **Technological** | R&D spending; patents; AI/semiconductor leadership; space capabilities |
| **Diplomatic** | UN Security Council seat; number of embassies; treaty ratifications; membership in major IGOs |
| **Soft / cultural** | Cultural exports (film, music, cuisine); educational attractiveness (foreign students); global media reach; ideological appeal |
| **Structural** | Ability to set global rules (WTO, IMF, ICANN); dominance of global infrastructure (SWIFT, dollar system, undersea cables) |

**A powerful analytical move for essays:** pick a case and show that a country ranks *high* on one dimension and *low* on another. India is a rising economic and demographic power, but its military is regionally strong yet globally limited, and its structural power in institutions like the IMF remains modest.

## Section 2.8 — Rising and Declining Powers — Case Snapshots

### China's rise

- From ~4% of global GDP in 2000 to ~19% (both nominal and PPP) in 2024. Blue-water navy expanding rapidly (largest by hull count).
- **Belt and Road Initiative** (2013–) involves 150+ countries.
- **Realist reading**: classic rising challenger to the US — Thucydides Trap dynamics.
- **Liberal reading**: China is *inside* many institutions and benefits from the current order.
- **Constructivist reading**: China's identity as a "civilisational state" shapes its exceptionalist claims.
- **2026 pressure points**: US chip export controls, TSMC-Taiwan dependency, ageing population, property-sector debt overhang, rare-earth chokehold used counter-strategically.

### US relative decline (but persistent hegemony)

- Still dominates in most dimensions — reserve currency, blue-water navy, tech ecosystem, soft power.
- Share of global GDP has fallen from ~40% (1960) to ~25% today.
- Domestic polarisation and repeated withdrawals from institutions (Paris Accord 2017, UNHRC, WHO) eroded soft power.
- **2026 context — Trump 2.0 retrenchment**: reduced Ukraine commitments, negotiating direct with Putin (bypassing Zelensky and NATO allies), pushed Europe toward "strategic autonomy."
- **Debate**: is the US in *decline*, or in *relative* decline while remaining dominant? Mainstream answer: relative decline, with structural power (dollar, tech) still intact.

### India's ascent

- 5th-largest economy (2024), expected to overtake Japan and Germany by 2027–2030.
- Democratic legitimacy claim distinguishes it from China.
- **QUAD** (with US, Japan, Australia) + **SCO** (with China, Russia, Central Asia) + **BRICS founder** — a "strategic autonomy" posture pursued through multiple institutional homes at once.
- **2026 signal**: PM Modi publicly told Putin to end the Ukraine war (Sept 2026) while staying silent on Trump's Ukraine deal and Netanyahu — a calibrated multi-alignment.
- Weak points: per-capita GDP still low; military-industrial capability still catching up; PLA modernisation outpaces India-China border reality.

### Russia's contradictory position

- Enormous nuclear arsenal + P5 UN veto = major hard power.
- Weak economy (~10% of the US, similar to Italy).
- The Ukraine war (Feb 2022–) has revealed the *limits* of Russian conventional military power while confirming its willingness to use force.
- **A "great power in one dimension only."** Nuclear + veto + territorial ambition without matching economic base.
- **2026 status**: locked in truce-and-attack cycle with Ukraine; multiple 2026 truces (April, May) followed by attacks; US envoys shuttling Kyiv–Moscow in September 2026 under a Trump peace proposal.

### Non-state and network power

- **Apple's market cap** exceeds the GDP of all but ~10 states.
- **Meta, Google, X** shape the information environments of 5+ billion people.
- **ISIS** at its 2014–2017 peak controlled territory the size of the UK — without statehood.
- **Amnesty International** and **Human Rights Watch** shape human-rights discourse globally with budgets a fraction of a mid-sized state.
- **Nvidia's AI-chip near-monopoly** — as of 2026, a company with structural power over the AI race for state and non-state actors alike.

## Section 2.9 — Live 2026 Case Studies

These are the cases you should be able to deploy with dates, actors, and concept-linkage on the day of SA1.

### Case 1: The US–China chip war and rare-earth counter-leverage

- **Timeline.** US chip export controls sequential tightening 2022 → 2023 → 2024; partial recalibration ("cooled down") early 2026.
- **Actors.** US Commerce Department, Nvidia, ASML (Netherlands), TSMC (Taiwan), SMIC (China), Huawei.
- **Concepts.** Hard power (export controls as economic coercion); structural power (chokehold on advanced chip production stack); Lukes' second face (US shaping what "national-security tech" even means); asymmetric interdependence (both sides hurt by decoupling, unequally).
- **Chinese counter-move.** Rare-earth export controls, IP retaliation, indigenous chip push. Rare earths are China's own structural chokehold.
- **Verdict.** Live case of hard power meeting structural power meeting relational power. Exam-ready across power classifications.

### Case 2: Russia-Ukraine — the truces and Trump's proposal (2026)

- **Timeline.** Feb 2022 invasion; multiple 2026 truces (April 2026, May 2026); September 2026 US envoys in Kyiv and Moscow negotiating a Trump peace proposal.
- **Actors.** Russia, Ukraine, US (Trump administration), NATO (fractured), EU (pushed toward autonomy), Zelensky, Putin.
- **Concepts.** Hard power exposed (Russian military underperformance); soft power (Zelensky-led narrative); structural power (Western dollar/SWIFT sanctions); sovereignty violated (Chapter 3); interdependence (European gas crisis, global grain prices).
- **Realist reading.** Mearsheimer thesis — NATO expansion caused the war; power politics as usual.
- **Liberal reading.** Institutions coordinated the response (UN condemnation, ICC warrant, sanctions coordination). Norms are being defended.
- **Constructivist reading.** The war reshaped Ukrainian identity (Europeanisation), German identity (Zeitenwende — end of pacifist posture), and Finnish/Swedish identity (NATO accession, ending decades of neutrality).

### Case 3: BRICS+ de-dollarisation push (2026)

- **Timeline.** Jan 2024 BRICS expansion (Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE added). 2026 summit prep foregrounds de-dollarisation strategies. Global launch of de-dollarisation agenda.
- **Actors.** Russia, China, India, Brazil, South Africa (originals); Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE (2024 joiners); observer states.
- **Concepts.** Structural power contest (finance domain); soft balancing against the US; relational vs structural power (BRICS contests the rules, not just individual outcomes).
- **Skeptical view.** Dollar's network effects and depth make displacement extremely slow. Even 15% dollar share shrinkage takes decades.
- **Sympathetic view.** The *idea* of an alternative matters; bilateral currency swaps (China-Russia, China-Saudi in yuan) chip at the edges; sanctions vulnerability has made dedollarisation a survival issue for non-aligned states.

### Case 4: ICC warrants — the sovereignty–power interface

- **March 2023**: ICC issues arrest warrant for Vladimir Putin (deportation of Ukrainian children).
- **November 2024**: ICC issues arrest warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu, Yoav Gallant, and Hamas leaders (Gaza war crimes).
- **State reactions**: Split. Some ICC states parties committed to arrest if Putin/Netanyahu visited (South Africa arm-wrestled over Putin at BRICS 2023, invoked domestic law to grant immunity; Hungary hosted Netanyahu 2025 without arrest, then withdrew from ICC). Others reaffirmed obligation.
- **Concepts.** Structural power (ICC as norm-setting institution); sovereignty erosion vs sovereignty reassertion; legitimacy (of the court, of the accused, of state non-compliance).
- **Exam-ready take.** The ICC test of 2024–26 is the sharpest live illustration of power–sovereignty tension: institutional structural power meets Westphalian state sovereignty, and state responses reveal the real limits of international law.

### Case 5: EU strategic autonomy and the "2026 independence moment"

- **Trigger.** Trump 2.0 retrenchment from NATO and Ukraine; Draghi report (Sept 2024) diagnosed European strategic dependency.
- **2026 developments**: EU defence-spending surge; joint borrowing for defence; Ukrainian defence innovation absorbed into EU industrial base; European Sky Shield initiative expanded.
- **Concepts.** Power *to* (building EU capacity independent of US); power *with* (member-state coordination); structural power (attempting to build EU-owned defence-industrial base rather than depending on US primes).
- **Realist reading.** EU is finally balancing — a rational response to US unreliability.
- **Liberal reading.** EU is deepening integration in a new domain (defence); pooled sovereignty extending beyond trade and monetary policy.
- **Sceptical reading.** France-Germany-Poland divisions still block genuine unified defence; the "moment" may pass without structural change.

### Case 6: Taiwan — the sharpest hard/soft/structural test

- **Ongoing 2026 tension**: Fourth Taiwan Strait Crisis dynamics continue; Beijing tests grey-zone coercion (naval exercises, cyber operations, economic pressure) short of invasion.
- **Actors.** PRC (Xi), ROC/Taiwan (Lai Ching-te), US (Trump 2.0 — ambiguous), Japan (increasingly hawkish), TSMC.
- **Concepts.** Hard power (PLA naval expansion, drills); soft power (Taiwan democracy narrative, TSMC as global structural indispensability); structural power (Taiwan's chip fabrication is a global economy chokepoint); sovereignty (de facto vs de jure — see Chapter 3).
- **Exam-ready angle.** Taiwan illustrates *all* four power classifications and directly links to sovereignty — one of the highest-yield case studies for a P1/P2 question spanning both concepts.

## Section 2.10 — Power Through Three Theoretical Lenses

Every case can be read through realism, liberalism, and constructivism. Deploying all three on the *same* case is a strong Paper 2 move.

### Case: Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine

| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Realism** | Great-power politics as usual. Russia acted to preserve its sphere and prevent NATO encroachment. Mearsheimer's controversial 2014 thesis vindicated. Power balancing produces war. |
| **Liberalism** | Institutions and norms matter. UN General Assembly condemnation (141 states), ICC warrant, coordinated sanctions, Ukrainian NATO/EU orientation reinforced. The response *validates* the liberal system even as the war shows its limits. |
| **Constructivism** | Identity is central. Putin's imagined Russian-world (Russkiy Mir) narrative constructed Ukraine as illegitimate. In response, Ukrainian identity Europeanised at speed; Finnish/Swedish neutrality identity collapsed; German pacifist identity broke ("Zeitenwende"). |

### Case: BRICS+ dedollarisation

| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Realism** | Rising powers balancing against US financial hegemony — soft balancing. Structural power contest. |
| **Liberalism** | New institutions being built (New Development Bank, Contingent Reserve Arrangement, BRICS Pay). Interdependence increasing among BRICS+ states. |
| **Constructivism** | The *idea* of a post-Western financial order shifts what actors think is possible — even without full mechanical dedollarisation. |

## Section 2.11 — Power and Sovereignty — The Live Interface

Power and Sovereignty are the two SA1-priority concepts. They interact constantly in exam questions. Anticipate the linkage:

- Every exercise of **structural power** by an IGO (ICC, WTO, IMF) is at some level a **constraint on sovereignty**.
- Every act of **hard power** by one state against another (invasion, sanctions, cyber) is at some level a **violation or contest of sovereignty**.
- Every act of **soft power** shapes the *legitimacy* of sovereign choices (Chapter 4 territory).
- **Pooled sovereignty** (EU) is a *voluntary trade of sovereignty for pooled power*.

**Exam move.** In any Paper 2 question that names one concept, at least one paragraph should invoke the other. They are the analytical Siamese twins of the course.

## Section 2.12 — Exam-Ready Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Power** | The ability to affect change; contested, relational, and dynamic. Takes hard, soft, smart, and structural forms. |
| **Hard power** | Coercion via military force or economic sanctions. |
| **Soft power** (Nye) | Attraction and persuasion via culture, political values, and legitimate foreign policy. |
| **Smart power** | The deliberate combination of hard and soft power, strategically chosen. |
| **Structural power** (Strange) | Control over the rules and frameworks of the international system, not just outcomes within it. |
| **Power over** | Coercive, zero-sum power one actor exercises over another. |
| **Power to** | Generative capacity or ability to act. |
| **Power with** | Collective power built through solidarity and coalition. |
| **Power within** | The internal dimension — self-worth, agency, dignity — that enables all other forms. |
| **Command power** (Nye) | Getting others to do what they otherwise would not — through threats or payments. |
| **Co-optive power** (Nye) | Shaping others' preferences so they voluntarily want what you want. |
| **Power conversion** | Translating power resources (GDP, military, culture) into power outcomes; requires strategy and legitimacy. |
| **First face of power** (Lukes) | Direct decision-making — visible coercion or influence. |
| **Second face of power** | Agenda-setting — control over what is discussed or ignored. |
| **Third face of power** | Preference-shaping — convincing others their interests align with yours. |
| **Security dilemma** | A state's efforts to increase its security appear threatening to others, producing arms races. |
| **Relative gains** | The realist idea that states care about how much they gain *compared to competitors*, not just absolute wealth. |
| **Balance of power** | Realist theory that peace is preserved when power is balanced between roughly equal blocs. |
| **Thucydides Trap** (Allison) | The tendency for a rising power's challenge to an established one to end in war. |
| **Polarity** | The number of major power centres — unipolar, bipolar, multipolar, non-polar. |
| **Soft balancing** | Non-military ways states resist a dominant power — coalition-building, institutional workarounds, symbolic dissent. |

## Section 2.13 — Common Exam Traps

1. **Naming a type of power without applying it.** "Russia used hard power" earns nothing. "Russia used hard power — the Feb 2022 invasion of Ukraine — but its conventional military underperformance revealed the *limits* of its power resources" earns credit.
2. **Treating power as a thing rather than a relationship.** Power is relational; the same actor is powerful in one dimension and constrained in another.
3. **Ignoring structural power.** Weaker essays mention hard and soft only. Strong essays name Strange's structural power and give at least one example (dollar system, ICC, TSMC).
4. **Confusing "smart power" with "hard + soft mixed."** Smart power is a *strategic* choice about which to deploy when, not a portfolio.
5. **Assuming polarity is fixed.** The system is transitioning; name the transition ("contested multipolarity") rather than picking one label.
6. **Skipping the third face of power.** The most consequential form is the hardest to see; a Paper 2 essay that only cites the first face is missing the analytical ceiling.
7. **Case studies without dates.** "In Russia there was a war" is worthless. "Feb 2022 invasion of Ukraine; April and May 2026 truces; September 2026 US-mediated peace talks under a Trump proposal" is a usable evidence point.
8. **State-only analysis.** Every strong essay includes non-state actors (Nvidia, TSMC, ICC, Amnesty, BRICS Pay, Zelensky as individual).
9. **Ignoring counter-perspectives.** Even a case that seems clear (Ukraine as victim) needs the counter-perspective (Mearsheimer realist reading, Global South ambivalence).
10. **Forgetting to link power to sovereignty (Chapter 3).** These two concepts are the SA1 twins.
