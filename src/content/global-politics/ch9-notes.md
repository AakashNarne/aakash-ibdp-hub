# GP Rights & Justice 4 — Debates — Notes

**Syllabus:** Thematic study *Rights and justice*, part 4, **Debates** covering (a) diverse standards and understandings of rights, (b) politicization of rights and justice, (c) humanitarian stakeholders and debates surrounding humanitarian intervention and R2P, (d) claims on individual and collective rights (Hodder pp.154–177). See [[_Syllabus Index]].

**Assessed in:** Paper 2 (essays, 15-mark questions) and Paper 1 (source analysis). HL extensions *Equality* and *Health* draw on it.

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **Universalism** (rights belong to all humans) and **cultural relativism** (rights are culture‑specific) sit at the heart of global political debate about whose rights count and who decides.
> - **Politicization** means actors use rights to gain power. States may ratify treaties for legitimacy but violate them later.
> - **Humanitarian intervention** and **R2P** raise the conflict between state sovereignty and the global responsibility to protect civilians; the cases are highly contested.
> - **Individual vs. collective rights**: Western constitutions emphasize individual liberty; many societies claim group‑based or communal rights (e.g., indigenous peoples, gender groups).
> - Core case studies: Amnesty's 2025 death‑penalty figures, the OHCHR Xinjiang assessment (2022), US post‑9/11 torture (Senate study, 2014), the Hong Kong NSL (2020), Libya and UNSC 1973 (2011), the end of Saudi Arabia's women‑driving ban (24 June 2018), and UNDRIP (2007).

## 1. Diverse standards and understandings of rights

**Command terms:** *define*, *explain*, *discuss*, *evaluate*.

**Universalism vs. cultural relativism** – two core perspectives on the contested nature of rights (Hodder p.155).

### 1.1 Universalism

*Definition (liberalism):* "Rights are basic claims and entitlements people have simply because they are human." – Hodder.

*Key claim:* The UDHR (adopted 10 Dec 1948: 48 for, 0 against, 8 abstentions) set out universal rights. It is a **declaration, not a treaty**, so it is not legally binding in itself; binding obligations come from later treaties such as the ICCPR and ICESCR (1966) and the Convention against Torture.

*Case study – Death penalty (2025):* Amnesty recorded **2,707 executions** in **17 countries**, up **78%** from 2024, the highest since 1981 ([Amnesty, 2026](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/05/death-penalty-in-2025-facts-and-figures)).

*Verification:* Amnesty’s report tracks official data from 17 executing states (Iran, Saudi Arabia, USA, etc.). URL: https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/act50/0778/2026/en

### 1.2 Cultural relativism

*Definition (postcolonialism):* Rights must be understood within the cultural context; "universal" rights are often Western‑centred.

*Key claim:* States such as **Saudi Arabia**, **Iran**, and **China** may invoke cultural or religious tradition to justify practices that universalists condemn (e.g., the death penalty, gender restrictions).

*Case study – Saudi Arabia women‑driving (2018):* The ban, first announced to end in **September 2017**, officially ended on **24 June 2018** (BBC). It was presented as part of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's Vision 2030 reforms. Many of the women's rights activists who had campaigned for it remained under arrest, so the case also shows a state controlling *who* gets credit for a rights reform ([BBC, 24 June 2018](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-middle-east-44576795)).

*Use:* evidence that rights standards change inside a society over time, which complicates claims that a practice is fixed "culture".

### 1.3 Theory links

| Theory | View of rights | Relevance to debate |
|--------|----------------|--------------------|
| Liberalism | Natural, inalienable, universal | Supports universalism; UDHR is a legal instrument. |
| Marxism | Rights are ideological, serving capitalist interests; critics argue they mask power relations. | Supports relativist critique of Western rights as tools of domination. |
| Postcolonialism | Focus on marginalised voices; rights are historically contingent. | Argues that universal rights often impose Western norms. |
| Feminism | Sexist bias in traditional rights discourses; intersectional analyses. | Highlights gendered dimensions of rights (e.g., women‑driving, reproductive rights). |
| Realism | State interest; ethics secondary. | Explains why states ratify treaties but violate them. |

### 1.4 Essay plan for Paper 2, debate 1

1. Introduction: Define "contested meanings" and outline universalism vs. cultural relativism.
2. **Claim:** Universal human rights are the foundation of global justice.
3. Evidence: UDHR history, death‑penalty statistics (2025 Amnesty report).
4. Counterclaim: Cultural relativism legitimises diversity; Saudi women‑driving case shows cultural‑specific approaches.
5. Theory: Liberalism supports universalism; post‑colonialism supports relativism.
6. Evaluation: Weigh legal bindingness against cultural autonomy.
7. Conclusion: Rights are contested; the balance must respect both universal norms and local context.

## 2. Politicization of rights and justice

**Command terms:** *explain*, *examine*, *evaluate*.

Politicization = using rights to gain or maintain power (Hodder p.156).

### 2.1 State motives: legitimacy vs. self‑interest

*Why states ratify but violate treaties:* **Legitimacy** (external acceptance) + **different understanding** + **lack of resources/will** (Hodder p.156).

*Case study – China's Xinjiang policy (UN report, 2022):* The **OHCHR report** documented "serious human rights violations" against Uyghurs, including mass detention, torture and persecution, possibly amounting to crimes against humanity ([UN News, 2022](https://news.un.org/en/story/2022/08/1125932)).

*Verification:* The report is a UN‑commissioned assessment based on interviews and documentary evidence; the URL provides direct access.

*Politicization examples:*
1. China accuses the West of using human‑rights allegations to undermine its legitimacy (state‑sovereignty argument).
2. The United States' post‑9/11 "enhanced interrogation" programme, justified as protecting security, later denounced as torture.

### 2.2 US post‑9/11 torture programme (Hodder case study)

*Facts:* The **US Senate Select Committee on Intelligence** study of the CIA's detention and interrogation programme (executive summary released **December 2014**) found the CIA held **at least 119** detainees, of whom **at least 39** were subjected to "enhanced interrogation techniques", including waterboarding and rectal rehydration. Hodder (printed p.154) cites the same 119/39 figures from a **2022** Watson Institute–Human Rights Watch report. Nearly **800** men and boys were held at Guantanamo; **39** remained in 2023, 27 without criminal charges.

*Politicization (Hodder):* Trump said in 2017 that torture "absolutely" works. Biden's 26 June 2023 statement said torture "remains prohibited" and condemned Russia, Syria and North Korea, but did not mention US abuses. China's foreign ministry then used US "black sites" to argue Washington had no right to criticise others, which is deflection on both sides ([Senate report PDF](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sites-default-files-documents-crpt-113srpt288.pdf)).

### 2.3 Power dynamics and IGO responses

*ICJ vs. ICC:* Only states can appear before the ICJ; individuals can be tried only by the ICC (but the ICC relies on state cooperation). Sovereignty limits enforcement.

*UNHRC vs. UNHCR:* The UN Human Rights Council passes non‑binding resolutions; the UNHCR protects refugees but can operate only with state consent.

### 2.4 Theory links (politicization)

| Theory | Insight | Example |
|--------|---------|---------|
| Realism | States act for national interest; rights are secondary. | China's Xinjiang crackdown despite UN condemnation. |
| Liberalism | Institutions can constrain states; legitimacy matters. | UN Human Rights Council resolutions. |
| Marxism | Rights discourse masks power structures; capitalism drives exploitation. | Private companies' role in labour rights (Rana Plaza). |

### 2.5 Essay plan for debate 2

1. Introduction: Define politicization; state why it matters in global politics.
2. **Claim:** Politicization undermines the credibility of rights regimes.
3. Evidence: China's Xinjiang policy (UN report), US torture (Senate report).
4. Counterclaim: States need sovereignty to protect citizens; cultural interpretations differ.
5. Theory: Realism explains state behaviour; liberalism offers hope for institutional enforcement.
6. Evaluation: Weigh normative ideals against power realities.
7. Conclusion: Politicization is inevitable; managing it is key to rights enforcement.

## 3. Humanitarian stakeholders and debates surrounding humanitarian intervention

**Command terms:** *evaluate*, *analyse*, *discuss*.

### 3.1 Who intervenes? Key humanitarian actors (Hodder Table 2.18, p.160)

| Agency | Mandate | Example action |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| ICRC | Helps people affected by armed conflict; rapid response. | Provides medical aid in war zones. |
| IFRC | National societies; disaster relief. | Coordinates flood response. |
| MSF (Doctors Without Borders) | Medical humanitarian assistance. | Operates in conflict zones with neutrality. |
| Oxfam | Fight inequality; promote justice. | Advocacy on climate‑change impacts. |
| Save the Children | Protect children in crises. | Education programmes in refugee camps. |

### 3.2 The Responsibility to Protect (R2P) (Hodder pp.162–165)

The **2005 UN World Summit** unanimously accepted the responsibility to protect populations from **genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity**. Do not mix up the two lists Hodder gives:

| Three pillars (Hodder Fig. 2.37) | Three responsibilities of the global community |
|---|---|
| **I.** States must protect their own people from mass atrocity crimes | **Prevent:** tackle long‑ and short‑term causes of conflict |
| **II.** The international community must assist states to do this | **React:** appropriate measures, e.g. sanctions, prosecution, and in extreme cases military intervention |
| **III.** If a state is unable or unwilling, the international community must take timely and decisive action under international law | **Rebuild:** recovery, reconstruction and reconciliation after intervention |

**Humanitarian intervention** means specifically the *use of military force*; within R2P it is the last resort.

*R2P controversy:*
- **Sovereignty clash:** Article 2(7) of the UN Charter forbids intervention in domestic jurisdiction.
- **Loss of life:** Military action (e.g., Libya) can cause civilian casualties and weapon proliferation.
- **Politicization:** Powerful states may exploit R2P for strategic goals (postcolonial critique).

### 3.3 Case study: NATO intervention in Libya (UNSC 1973, 2011)

- **Background:** In February 2011 Gaddafi's forces cracked down on protesters; an estimated **500–700** people were killed over several weeks (Hodder).
- **Action:** On **17 March 2011** the Security Council adopted **Resolution 1973** by **10–0 with 5 abstentions (Brazil, China, Germany, India, Russia)**, authorising "all necessary measures" to protect civilians, including in Benghazi, but excluding a foreign occupation force. Asset freezes hit the Libyan National Oil Corp and the Central Bank of Libya.
- **Outcome:** NATO‑led air strikes; Tripoli fell to rebel forces and the Gaddafi government collapsed in **August 2011**. Hodder calls it the first use of humanitarian intervention under R2P.
- **Debate:** many states said the mandate was stretched from civilian protection to regime change, with too few safeguards; long‑term instability followed. This made Russia and China more resistant to later R2P resolutions.

### 3.4 Sanctions as an alternative

*Types:* travel bans, asset freezes, arms embargoes, aid reduction, trade restrictions.
*Effectiveness debate:* Critics argue sanctions hurt civilian populations; proponents see them as a middle ground between diplomacy and war.

### 3.5 Theory links (humanitarian intervention)

| Theory | Position on intervention |
|--------|--------------------------|
| Just war theory | Conditions: just cause, last resort, legitimate authority. |
| Realism | Skeptical; sovereignty and national interest dominate. |
| Cosmopolitanism | Supports universal human protection; may justify intervention. |
| Postcolonialism | Views humanitarian intervention as a new form of Western domination. |

### 3.6 Essay plan for debate 3

1. Introduction: Define humanitarian intervention and R2P; outline debate.
2. **Claim:** Humanitarian intervention is a moral duty to protect civilians.
3. Evidence: Libya 2011 (UNSC 1973); ICRC field operations.
4. Counterclaim: Intervention violates sovereignty; politicization leads to abuse.
5. Theory: Just war criteria vs. realist power politics.
6. Evaluation: Weigh protection against destabilisation.
7. Conclusion: Humanitarian intervention remains contested; strict safeguards needed.

## 4. Claims on individual and collective rights

**Command terms:** *explain*, *discuss*, *evaluate*.

### 4.1 Individual rights – the UDHR legacy

*Examples:* Freedom of expression, fair trial, education (Art. 26). These are **negative** (non‑interference) and **positive** (state action) rights.

### 4.2 Collective rights – group‑based claims

*Key examples:*
- **Indigenous peoples:** UNDRIP (UNGA 61/295, adopted 13 Sept 2007 by **143–4, 11 abstentions**; Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the US voted against) recognises collective rights to culture, land, self‑determination (https://www.un.org/sites/un2.un.org/files/doc.18_declaration_rights_indigenous_peoples.pdf).
- **Women:** Saudi driving reform illustrates a collective claim to mobility and public participation.
- **LGBTQ+:** Anti‑discrimination laws reflect collective rights to equality.

*Debate:* Do collective rights dilute individual freedoms? Counter‑argument: Some rights are inherently group‑based (e.g., development, environmental protection).

### 4.3 The tension in real‑world cases

*Case study – Hong Kong National Security Law (NSL, 30 June 2020):* The law criminalises "collusion with foreign forces," restricting freedom of expression and assembly. Supporters argue it protects collective security; critics claim it suppresses dissent and individual rights ([Elegislation.gov.hk](https://www.elegislation.gov.hk/hk/2020/gn(e)72!en)).

*Verification:* The official Chinese legislation provides the text; the Hong Kong government site confirms its enactment.

### 4.4 Theory links (individual vs. collective)

| Theory | Emphasis | Example |
|--------|----------|---------|
| Liberalism | Individual autonomy; emphasis on negative rights. | UDHR Articles. |
| Communitarianism | Group values and duties; collective rights. | Indigenous rights frameworks. |
| Marxist | Collective economic rights; critique of individualistic focus. | Labor rights and collective bargaining. |

### 4.5 Essay plan for debate 4

1. Introduction: Distinguish individual vs. collective rights; why both matter.
2. **Claim:** Collective rights are essential for marginalised groups to achieve justice.
3. Evidence: UNDRIP, Saudi driving reform, Hong Kong NSL.
4. Counterclaim: Collective rights can marginalise individuals (e.g., within groups).
5. Theory: Liberal vs. communitarian perspectives.
6. Evaluation: Balance between group empowerment and individual protections.
7. Conclusion: A pluralistic rights regime must accommodate both individual and collective claims.

## 5. Common mistakes / examiner traps

1. **Confusing IGOs:** UNHRC (resolutions) vs. UNHCR (refugees). Remember: *C*ouncil for *H*uman *R*ights; *High* *Commissioner* for *Refugees*.
2. **Over‑generalising:** Saying "all states violate rights." Provide verified case studies with sources.
3. **Ignoring power:** Exams reward analysis of who has the power to enforce rights (realist lens).
4. **Missing verification:** Every external fact must have a URL in Sources.
5. **Mixing up rights types:** Clearly label negative vs. positive rights; three generations of rights (1st liberty, 2nd equality, 3rd fraternity).
6. **Weak theory links:** Connect liberalism, Marxism, post‑colonialism explicitly to each debate.
7. **Neglecting the essay plan:** Allocate marks for structured answers with clear claims, evidence, and evaluation.

## 6. Self‑test

1. Distinguish universalism from cultural relativism; give one contemporary example of each.
2. Explain why a state may ratify a human‑rights treaty but later violate it.
3. Outline the three pillars of R2P and discuss one major criticism.
4. Compare individual rights with collective rights; use UNDRIP as an example.
5. Evaluate the effectiveness of sanctions as an alternative to military intervention.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. **Universalism:** Human rights are universal and inalienable for all people regardless of culture. Example: Amnesty's 2025 death‑penalty report records 2,707 executions in 17 countries. **Cultural relativism:** Rights must be understood within local cultural context. Example: Saudi Arabia's former driving ban, defended as tradition until it ended on 24 June 2018.
> 2. States may ratify for legitimacy, but domestic pressures, lack of resources, or divergent interpretations can lead to violations.
> 3. Pillars: (a) Prevent, (b) React, (c) Rebuild. Criticism: Violation of sovereignty; politicisation (e.g., Libya).
> 4. Individual rights protect personal freedoms; collective rights protect group identity. UNDRIP recognises indigenous peoples' rights to land and self‑determination as a collective claim.
> 5. Sanctions can pressure regimes but often harm civilians; they are a middle ground but require careful design.
