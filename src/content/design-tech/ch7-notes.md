# DT B2.1 — The Design Process — Notes

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - The IB design thinking model has five sections: **empathise → define the project → ideation and modelling → design a solution → present a solution**.
> - Research continues throughout the process; it is not a single step that ends once the brief is written.
> - Convert evidence into a clear problem, user requirements and measurable **design parameters/specifications** before comparing concepts.
> - Model, test, study the evidence and refine. A prototype only tests the features and conditions it represents.
> - Communicate both the proposal and its rationale: the audience should understand how it answers the need and what is required to make it.

## 1. Scope and command terms

This note covers the B2.1 design-process sequence from the current Design Technology textbook and the supplied SA2 scope. The IB model has five sections; the detailed B2.1 learning points describe research, analysis, decision-making, iterative modelling and communication within that process. See [[_Syllabus Index]].

| Command term | What a complete answer does |
|---|---|
| **State / identify** | Name a stage, method, output or decision. |
| **Outline** | Give the main steps or features in sequence. |
| **Explain** | Connect a stage or method to evidence and the decision it supports. |
| **Analyse** | Trace how research, constraints, criteria and feedback affect later choices. |
| **Evaluate / justify** | Judge a choice against the brief and evidence, address trade-offs, then support a conclusion. |

## 2. The five sections (B2.1.1)

| Section | Purpose and typical actions | Useful output | Decision that follows |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Empathise** | Understand users, tasks, needs, wants, limitations and context. Use research to discover the problem rather than assume it. | User evidence, observations, needs and initial opportunities. | Which need is important and sufficiently supported to define? |
| **Define the project** | Reframe the problem from the user's perspective; clarify user, context, constraints and success criteria. | Focused design brief and measurable parameters. | What must the solution achieve, and what is out of scope? |
| **Ideation and modelling** | Generate alternatives, communicate concepts and make models appropriate to the question being explored. | Annotated sketches, alternative concepts, mock-ups/models and feedback. | Which concepts merit further development against the parameters? |
| **Design a solution** | Develop a selected direction through detail design, testing and repeated refinement. | Refined proposal, test evidence, revisions and rationale. | Does the evidence support the solution, or is another iteration needed? |
| **Present a solution** | Communicate the solution, key features and the reasoning behind design decisions. | A clear, annotated proposal with relevant technical details. | Can the intended audience understand, assess and act on the proposal? |

These sections are connected rather than a rigid one-way checklist. If research reveals that the wrong problem was defined, return to the brief. If a prototype fails an important criterion, revise the model or concept, and revisit the need if necessary. Record **what changed, why, and what evidence prompted the change**.

## 3. Research, users and the problem (B2.1.2–B2.1.8)

### Research is ongoing (B2.1.2)

Research identifies opportunities, clarifies user needs and tests whether a proposed solution is feasible in the real world. Continue researching as the project develops: new concepts raise new questions, tests reveal new information, and context or stakeholder feedback may change the design decision. Do not treat a single interview or a persona as conclusive proof.

### Primary and secondary research (B2.1.3–B2.1.4)

| Type | Meaning | Design examples | Limitation to assess |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary research** | First-hand data collected for, or directly relevant to, the design context. | User observation, interviews, surveys, task trials, measurements, feedback on an artifact or prototype. | Can be time-consuming; sample and method may be biased or too narrow. |
| **Secondary research** | Existing information collected or analysed by others. | Standards, published studies, market reports, competitor information and existing product reviews. | May be outdated, use a different population or answer a different question. |

Data from either type may be qualitative (descriptive accounts, experiences or preferences) or quantitative (counts, measurements or numerical comparisons). State how the data was obtained and how it informs the decision; raw information still needs careful analysis.

### Personae and user profiles (B2.1.5)

A **persona** is a fictional profile based on research patterns among a user group. It can help a team consider goals, needs, behaviours and context, but it is not a real interviewee and does not represent every individual. Build a persona from varied evidence, avoid invented quotations or unsupported demographics, and update it when research changes. Demographic information alone is not enough to establish user needs.

### User observation, journey and storyboard (B2.1.6)

Observe users performing a task to identify the sequence of actions, context, decisions, interruptions and pain points. A **user journey** maps the stages of a user's interaction with a product or service. A **storyboard** uses ordered panels or scenes to communicate what the user does, thinks or encounters in context. Use these tools to identify opportunities; do not present an inferred emotion or cause as directly observed fact.

### Product analysis (B2.1.7)

Analyse an existing product to understand its function, performance and features, then identify opportunities for a new or improved design. Depending on the product and question, use:

- **Market assessment** to examine current trends, users and likely directions.
- **Comparison / benchmarking** to compare products or versions against stated measures.
- **SWOT analysis** to organise strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.
- **Usability testing and user feedback** to locate difficulties, preferences and task failures.
- **Market-performance evidence** such as engagement or repurchase data, where relevant and properly sourced.

Choose methods that produce evidence related to the design problem. A competitor's popularity does not by itself prove that its design is effective or appropriate for the intended users.

### Define the problem and parameters (B2.1.8–B2.1.9)

A **design brief** states the project purpose, intended user, problem or opportunity, context and expected outcome. It aligns the project without prematurely prescribing a single solution. Consider user requirements, budget, time, safety/legislation, market, production scale and environmental constraints where relevant.

**Design parameters/specifications** translate the brief and user evidence into criteria that can guide and evaluate concepts. Make them relevant, clear and measurable where possible (for example, a stated dimensional range, task outcome or cost ceiling). Separate a requirement from a proposed feature: “must be usable with one hand” is a requirement; a particular button shape is one possible response. Avoid inventing thresholds before research or presenting assumptions as measured results.

## 4. Ideation, modelling and iteration (B2.1.10–B2.1.13)

### Ideation and modelling

**Divergent thinking** generates varied concepts before selection; **convergent thinking** compares them against the brief, user evidence, parameters and constraints. Useful ideation approaches include brainstorming, mind maps, SCAMPER, Six Thinking Hats or a morphological chart. Select a method to fit the design question; quantity alone does not make an idea suitable.

A **model** is a representation used to explore, communicate or test an idea. A **mock-up** commonly explores form, layout or fit; a **prototype** represents some intended functions or interactions so they can be tested. These terms can vary across contexts, so state what the particular model represents and what it can test.

| Fidelity / model | Useful for | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Annotated sketch or wireframe | Rapidly exploring form, layout, features or user flow. | Does not demonstrate physical performance or usability by itself. |
| Low-fidelity physical mock-up | Approximate size, arrangement, grip or spatial fit. | Material and construction may not represent final behaviour. |
| Digital model | Geometry, dimensions, appearance or relationships between components. | A visual model may not prove interaction, strength or manufacturability. |
| Functional or higher-fidelity prototype | Testing defined functions, interactions or realistic user tasks. | Costlier; evidence applies only to the features and conditions represented. |

### Iterative analysis and evaluation (B2.1.11–B2.1.12)

An iteration should be an evidence-led change, not merely another version. A useful **model–test–study–refine** loop is:

1. **Plan/model:** choose the question and make a model suited to it.
2. **Test:** use a defined procedure, task, user group and context.
3. **Study:** record results and compare them with the relevant parameter.
4. **Refine:** decide what to change, keep or investigate next; then repeat if needed.

Document the iteration: criterion → test evidence → finding → decision → change → next test. If results conflict or the model cannot answer the question, improve the method rather than overstating the conclusion.

## 5. Communicate detail and present the solution (B2.1.14–B2.1.15)

### Physical and technical details (B2.1.14)

To manufacture a product, communicate the information needed to interpret it consistently. Depending on the product, this may include appropriately scaled and dimensioned drawings, tolerances, materials, surface finishes, assembly/joining details, components, manufacturing notes and relevant standards. Use clear views, labels and units; a persuasive concept image alone is not a manufacturing specification.

### Presenting the proposal (B2.1.15)

A presentation should make the need for the solution and the key features that address it clear. Link annotations to user requirements and parameters, explain important decisions, and identify materials/processes or technical information needed for the next stage. A strong presentation communicates **what the solution is, who it is for, how it works, why it was chosen, and what evidence supports it**.

### Worked case — Guggenheim Museum Bilbao

Metcalfe describes how Gehry Partners began using CATIA in 1993 to model the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao's complex exterior; the museum opened in 1997. Its exterior used about **33,000 titanium plates**, including special panels **0.4 mm** thick (Metcalfe, PDF pp. 108–109). The textbook explains that digital modelling supported the complex geometry, structural calculations and automated cutting. Use this case to show how detailed representations communicate a design for analysis and construction; do not imply that CAD alone ensured project success.

## 6. Integrated worked application — school water bottle (illustrative)

This is a practice scenario, not a report of research already carried out.

1. **Empathise:** observe and interview a varied group of students about opening, drinking from, carrying and cleaning bottles in school settings.
2. **Define:** use the evidence to write a brief and prioritise criteria such as leakage, operation, cleanability and carrying. Do not set unsupported thresholds.
3. **Ideate/model:** sketch more than one closure and handle concept; make quick mock-ups to test grip or arrangement.
4. **Design/test/refine:** build a prototype suited to the chosen question, give users consistent tasks, record observed performance and comments, and compare results to criteria.
5. **Present:** show the refined proposal with annotated features, user rationale, relevant dimensions/materials/processes and any evidence still needed before manufacture.

If users report that a closure looks easy but repeatedly struggle to open it, treat the conflict between stated opinion and observed task performance as evidence to investigate. Do not claim the imagined outcome occurred.

## 7. Compact exam-response structure

1. Name the stage or design decision and define its purpose.
2. Apply it to the stated user, task and context.
3. Identify the relevant research, parameter, model or output.
4. Explain how evidence informs the next decision or iteration.
5. Evaluate a limitation or trade-off and justify a practical response.

## 8. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Describing the process as a rigid one-way cycle. | Explain when new evidence should send the designer back to research, definition or ideation. |
| Saying “empathise” means imagining what users want. | Use evidence from users and their context. |
| Treating research as complete after the brief. | Show how further questions and tests arise throughout design. |
| Confusing primary and secondary research. | State whether evidence is first-hand or already collected by another source. |
| Treating a persona as a real user or universal profile. | Explain that it summarises a researched group and has limits. |
| Selecting a concept because it looks best. | Compare alternatives against user needs, parameters and constraints. |
| Calling every repeated version an iteration. | State what evidence triggered the change and what was learned. |
| Treating a sketch or model as proof of performance. | Match model fidelity to the question and identify what remains untested. |
| Listing product-analysis methods without a decision purpose. | Explain what each method reveals about the particular product/problem. |
| Presenting a final drawing without technical information or rationale. | Annotate how the proposal meets requirements and what is needed to make/evaluate it. |
| Claiming a historic case followed today's IB labels exactly. | Use it to illustrate a relevant principle, not to prove current terminology. |

## 9. Self-test

1. Name the five sections of the IB design process in order.
2. Distinguish primary from secondary research and give one example of each.
3. Explain how user observation and a storyboard can reveal a design opportunity.
4. What makes an iteration evidence-led, and what should be recorded?
5. Name three kinds of technical detail that may be needed to communicate a product for manufacture, and explain why a concept image alone is insufficient.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Empathise; define the project; ideation and modelling; design a solution; present a solution.
> 2. Primary research is first-hand data relevant to the design context, such as observing users test a prototype. Secondary research uses existing material collected or analysed by others, such as a published market report or standard.
> 3. Observation records user actions and context during a task; a storyboard shows the sequence of interaction. Together they can make pain points or unmet needs visible, which should then be checked against further evidence.
> 4. A test or feedback finding prompts a justified change or decision. Record the criterion, method, evidence, finding, change and next test.
> 5. Examples: dimensions/tolerances, materials, finishes, assembly details, manufacturing notes or standards. These remove ambiguity for production; a concept image may omit dimensions, hidden construction and process information.
