# DT A2.1 — User-Centred Research Methods — Notes

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - Choose a method to answer a **design question** about a particular user, task and context; do not select a method just because it is familiar.
> - Observation reveals what users do; interviews help explore why; surveys can compare patterns across a larger sample; user trials test a proposed solution.
> - Combine evidence when useful. **Triangulation** means comparing sources or methods; disagreement is a prompt to investigate, not hide.
> - Check sample relevance, validity, reliability, bias, accessibility and ethics before relying on findings.
> - Personae and scenarios organise research evidence; they are not substitutes for users and must not be treated as proof that every user is the same.

## 1. Scope and command terms

This note covers all five sections in Metcalfe's A2.1 sequence: (1) end-user needs, (2) user involvement, (3) understanding users through multidisciplinary teams, (4) methods and profiles for understanding populations, and (5) personae, scenarios, use cases and population stereotypes. Use [[_Syllabus Index]] for the official course coverage map.

| Command term | What a complete answer does |
|---|---|
| **Describe** | State what the method involves and what data it can produce. |
| **Explain** | Link the chosen method to the user, task, context and design question. |
| **Compare** | Use common criteria (data, sample, time, bias, validity) to compare methods. |
| **Evaluate** | Balance strengths and limitations and justify whether the evidence is adequate for the decision. |

## 2. End-user needs and limitations (A2.1.1)

User-centred design begins by understanding users' **needs, wants and limitations** before proposing features. A need is a problem or task the user must address; a want is a desired quality or preference; a limitation may arise from ability, experience, resources, context or the environment. Separate what users say they want from evidence about what they need to accomplish.

Frame each inquiry around the user, task, product and environment. Ask what the user is trying to achieve, what currently obstructs them, what capabilities and constraints matter, and how the setting changes the task. A design feature that is useful in one context may add cost or complexity without value in another.

## 3. User involvement throughout design (A2.1.2)

In UCD, involve users at multiple stages rather than asking for opinions only at the beginning. Metcalfe organises the cycle as **research → concept → design → implementation → launch**:

| Stage | User-centred activity | How user input affects the next decision |
|---|---|---|
| **Research** | Define the problem and criteria; observe tasks and investigate the market/context. | Establishes evidence-based needs and opportunities. |
| **Concept** | Develop initial concepts with design, user-research and technical expertise. | Turns identified needs into alternatives for users and the team to consider. |
| **Design** | Make drawings/models and examine scale, appearance, feel and usability; iterate while changes are relatively quick. | User and team feedback refines or rejects concepts. |
| **Implementation** | Test a model with a wider range of end-users and observe their experience. | Test findings feed back into design and supporting instructions. |
| **Launch** | Introduce the product and seek wider customer feedback, including usability concerns and improvement ideas. | Post-launch evidence may prompt later revisions. |

Keep a traceable link between the evidence, the design decision and the next iteration. This is a cycle, not a guarantee that every product follows identical steps.

Users do not have to make every design decision. Designers interpret evidence alongside safety, technical feasibility, production, environmental and commercial constraints, and should explain how trade-offs were made.

## 4. Understanding users through multidisciplinary teams (A2.1.3)

Complex design problems benefit from **multidisciplinary teams**: people with different relevant expertise collaborate to understand the user, task and environment. Depending on the project, contributions may come from end-users, designers, psychologists, sociologists, anthropologists, engineers, human-factors specialists, materials/manufacturing experts, data analysts, marketers or environmental specialists. Select roles to match the problem; a long list of professions is not a substitute for explaining what each contributes.

### Worked application — Mercury programme spacesuit

Metcalfe describes development of an early NASA Mercury-programme spacesuit in the late 1950s as a multidisciplinary effort involving engineering, materials science, medicine, design and biology (PDF pp. 50–51). The example illustrates why a demanding user and environment require several kinds of expertise: the design had to consider thermal conditions, life support, biomedical monitoring, durability and astronaut needs together. In an exam, connect each discipline to a specific requirement and explain the coordination challenge; do not claim the case followed every current IB process label exactly.

## 5. Understanding user populations: research questions and method selection (A2.1.4)

User-centred research reduces the risk of designing around unsupported assumptions. Begin by defining:

1. **Intended user population:** who will use the product, including relevant variation in age, ability, experience and context.
2. **Task or problem:** what users need to do and what currently makes it difficult.
3. **Context of use:** where, when and under what physical, social or environmental conditions the task happens.
4. **Unknown:** what the designer needs to find out before deciding.
5. **Design consequence:** what decision the evidence could change.

A research question should be specific enough to guide method choice. For example, “Which bottle is best?” is too broad. “Can students with different hand strengths open the proposed lid one-handed during a normal school day?” identifies a user, task and context that can be investigated.

### Understanding user populations

Build a **user profile** from characteristics that matter to the design problem, such as task, age range, ability, experience, culture, location or access to resources. Do not collect demographic detail simply because it is available: explain how a characteristic changes needs, use or constraints. A population is not uniform; sample for relevant variation, describe who is missing and avoid treating group-level patterns as facts about every person.

## 6. Primary and secondary research

**Primary research** gathers first-hand information for the design problem, such as observation, interviews, questionnaires or user trials. **Secondary research** analyses existing sources collected by others, such as standards, published studies or market information.

- Primary research can fit the design context closely, but takes time and may involve small or biased samples.
- Secondary research can provide background efficiently, but may be outdated, use a different population or measure a different problem.
- Either may be **qualitative** (descriptive evidence about experiences and meanings) or **quantitative** (counts, measurements or numerical comparisons). A questionnaire can collect both, depending on its questions.

## 7. Choosing and evaluating methods

| Method | Best suited to | Useful output | Main limitations / controls |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Observation / field research** | Seeing behaviour, workarounds and context during real tasks. | Actions, sequence, errors, interruptions, environmental constraints. | Observer effect and interpretation bias; observe more than one user/context and record consistently. |
| **Task analysis** | Finding difficult, redundant or error-prone steps in a task. | Task sequence, decisions, delays and points of friction. | May miss emotional or wider-life context; analyse a real task with users. |
| **Interview** | Exploring experiences, motivations and reasons behind behaviour. | Detailed verbal evidence and follow-up questions. | Small samples, recall and social-desirability bias; use neutral prompts and record responses systematically. |
| **Survey / questionnaire** | Comparing stated experiences or preferences across more people. | Frequencies, ratings and open-ended comments. | Leading wording, non-response and sampling bias; pilot questions and report who responded. |
| **Focus group** | Exploring shared or differing views through group discussion. | Ideas, reactions and language used by participants. | Dominant speakers, conformity and moderator effects; create space for quieter voices and do not treat consensus as population-wide proof. |
| **User trial / usability test** | Testing a prototype against a defined user task. | Task completion, time, errors, observed problems and comments. | Small sample or artificial context; use realistic tasks, comparable instructions and an appropriate prototype. |
| **Secondary research** | Establishing background, standards, existing solutions or known constraints. | Published evidence and context for primary research. | May be mismatched or outdated; assess source, population, method and date. |

### Method selection in practice

A useful sequence is to explore broadly, narrow the question, then test a candidate solution:

- **Early discovery:** observation and open-ended interviews can reveal needs or problems designers had not anticipated.
- **Clarification:** task analysis can break a complex activity into steps and identify where a difficulty occurs.
- **Comparison:** a carefully designed survey or structured interview can compare a defined issue across participants.
- **Evaluation:** a user trial can test whether a prototype meets a stated criterion during a realistic task.

This is a flexible sequence, not a rule that every project must use every method. Explain why the method fits the design question and what its evidence cannot show.

## 8. Quality of evidence

- **Validity:** whether the method measures what the designer intends to investigate. A test of appearance does not prove durability; a stated preference does not prove successful task performance.
- **Reliability:** whether the process is consistent enough that comparable conditions produce comparable evidence. Use clear task instructions, consistent recording and suitable equipment.
- **Sampling:** select participants who reflect the intended users and relevant variation. State who is missing and how that limits conclusions.
- **Bias:** leading questions, convenience sampling, researcher expectations, non-response, observer effects and social desirability can distort results.
- **Triangulation:** compare different methods or evidence sources. If observation and interview disagree, investigate whether users behave differently from what they recall or report.
- **Ethics:** explain the research purpose, gain informed consent, protect privacy, avoid harm, make participation accessible and represent participants fairly.

A large sample does not automatically make research representative. A small sample may be useful for exploring problems, but it is not enough to claim that a result applies to an entire population.

## 9. Personae, scenarios, use cases and stereotypes (A2.1.5)

A **persona** is a research-based representation of a user group, built from patterns in user evidence. It may summarise goals, context, abilities, needs and frustrations. It helps a design team keep a user perspective visible, but it does not represent every individual in the group.

A **scenario** describes a context and sequence in which a user interacts with a product. It can help designers explore tasks, interruptions, settings and potential problems before or during prototyping.

A **use case** is a written description of how a user interacts with a product from the user's perspective. It makes the user's actions and the product's response explicit, helping a team inspect task flow and usability. A scenario gives the broader context; a use case focuses on the interaction sequence. Neither replaces testing with real users.

A **population stereotype** is an assumption about how members of a group will behave or understand a product. Designers should test assumed conventions with diverse users; a familiar control for one group may be unfamiliar to another.

Metcalfe illustrates population stereotypes with differences in control conventions and driving side, and reports that over 60% of the world favours right-hand traffic. Treat that as a textbook-reported figure (PDF p. 55), not a current statistic to quote without checking a suitable transport source. A familiar convention can make an operation intuitive, while a mismatch may cause confusion—especially under stress. Designers should test conventions in the product's actual cultural context rather than assume one is universal.

> [!warning] Evidence check
> Do not invent demographic details, quotations or preferences for a persona. Label unknowns as unknown, base the profile on research, and revise it when new evidence appears.

## 10. Worked application — school water bottle redesign

This is an **illustrative research plan**, not a report of research already performed.

**Design question:** Can the intended students open, drink from, carry and clean the proposed bottle safely and conveniently during school use?

1. **Observe** students using existing bottles in class and during sport. Record steps, spills, workarounds and context; do not infer motivation from observation alone.
2. **Interview** students about difficulties and priorities. Use open prompts first, then follow-up questions about specific tasks.
3. **Sample deliberately:** include students with different ages, hand strength, dexterity and routines. Explain why the sample does or does not represent intended users.
4. **Create measurable criteria after discovery:** possible outcomes include opening errors, leakage during a specified task, ease of cleaning or successful one-handed operation. Define how each will be recorded before the trial.
5. **Test prototypes** using comparable instructions and realistic tasks. Combine observed performance with participant comments.
6. **Evaluate evidence:** an interview may show a preference, while a trial may reveal difficulty. Report both, investigate the discrepancy and revise the design or research question.

## 11. Compact exam-response structure

1. State the research question and intended users.
2. Name the method and the evidence it will collect.
3. Explain why it fits the task and context.
4. Identify a sample, procedure and one validity/reliability/ethics control.
5. Evaluate a limitation and how a second method or improved procedure could address it.
6. Explain what design decision the evidence will inform.

## 12. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Saying only “use a survey.” | State the question, respondent group, response type and design decision informed. |
| Assuming a bigger sample is automatically representative. | Discuss who was sampled, who was missed and how recruitment may bias results. |
| Treating interviews as proof of actual behaviour. | Compare what users report with observation or task trials. |
| Treating a persona as a real individual or universal user. | Explain that it summarises evidence about a group and has limits. |
| Calling qualitative data unreliable by definition. | Explain how structured recording, transparent analysis and triangulation can strengthen credibility. |
| Changing instructions between test participants. | Standardise procedures when comparing performance, while noting context where relevant. |
| Ignoring accessibility or ethics. | Include accessible participation, consent, privacy and potential harm in the method plan. |
| Claiming results before conducting research. | Label plans and hypothetical examples; report only findings actually observed. |

## 13. Self-test

1. Distinguish primary and secondary research.
2. Explain one strength and one limitation of observation for a design problem.
3. Define validity and reliability in a user-research context.
4. Why might a designer combine interviews with a user trial?
5. Explain how a persona can help design while still creating a risk of stereotyping.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Primary research gathers first-hand evidence for the design problem; secondary research analyses existing evidence collected or published by others.
> 2. Observation reveals behaviour and context, including workarounds; it may be affected by the observer or limited to the observed settings and can be open to interpretation.
> 3. Validity asks whether the method measures the intended issue; reliability asks whether the procedure is consistent enough to produce comparable results.
> 4. Interviews explore users' reasons and experiences; a trial checks what happens when users perform a task. Comparing them can reveal gaps between reported preference and observed performance.
> 5. A persona makes researched patterns visible to a design team, but may flatten differences or reinforce assumptions. Base it on diverse evidence, identify limits and validate decisions with real users.
