# Design Technology SL — Assessed Work Mark Lessons

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - This booklet assesses the **design project/internal assessment**, not a timed written exam.
> - The strongest sample made a traceable chain from user evidence and task analysis to the redesign, specifications, testing and final communication.
> - Iteration earns credit when it is tested against specifications and user feedback—not when a model merely looks finished.
> - Keep the redesign focused on the existing product and the user’s demonstrated problem.
> - The examiner comments are guidance and have not been standardized; treat individual project marks as exemplars, not targets.

## Sample marks

|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | Prosthetics (title in booklet) | 8 | 5 | 6 | 6 | 5 | **30** |
| 4 | Trowel | 5 | 5 | 4 | 6 | 4 | **24** |
| 3 | Math play set | 6 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 2 | **18** |
| 2 | Wheelchair disability desk | 4 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | **16** |
| 5 | FPV Drone racing tool | 4 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 2 | **14** |

The score pattern is informative: Example 1’s strongest results were in ideation/modelling and designing a solution (full marks in C and D); lower samples repeatedly lost marks where the need, task analysis, design choices and testing did not line up. **Source inconsistency:** Example 1 is titled “Prosthetics”, but its commentary describes bicycle braking systems. Use its reported marks and general process feedback cautiously; do not reuse its bicycle-specific details as if they describe the titled prosthetics work.

## What stronger work made visible

- **Empathize:** explain a real primary persona and context using primary research; show the user’s actual task and issues through an evidence-based storyboard; analyse an appropriate range of existing products and evaluate feature strengths/weaknesses.
- **Define:** derive a focused redesign opportunity from the task analysis. Make every design specification traceable to research and user requirements; use measurable/qualitative detail where it is relevant.
- **Ideate:** present three strong, feasible redesign concepts with key features; compare them against the specifications through testing and user feedback; justify which idea advances.
- **Design:** show multiple prototype/test/refinement cycles against the specifications and user needs. Explain what each test changed and why.
- **Present:** communicate the final redesign so another person could understand or manufacture it—clear views, dimensions, proportions, assembly/components and annotations—and compare it directly with the original product and problem.

## What not to do

- Do not propose an entirely new product when the task requires redesigning an existing product; the FPV-drone example lost marks when its problem statement implied creating a new product instead of redesigning the relevant existing product.
- Do not let the storyboard or prototype solve a different issue from the one established by user evidence.
- Do not include product comparisons that are not relevant to the user’s task, or list features without evaluating what works and fails.
- Do not state dimensions, weights or other requirements without tracing them to research, task analysis or user needs.
- Do not submit ideas without testing/feedback against specifications, or claim iteration without showing how evidence led to a change.
- Do not leave the final drawings ambiguous: weak/unclear drawings and missing orthographic/manufacturing detail were recurring limitations in lower-mark work.
- Follow the current task-specific word and evidence rules; the sample guidance includes storyboard, page and annotation conventions, but check your teacher’s current instructions before applying them.

## Common assessment traps

- **Unbroken evidence chain:** every stage should answer the same user problem; a mismatch at the start weakens all later criteria.
- **Research volume versus research quality:** several sources or products are not enough unless their findings are relevant, evaluated and used to justify decisions.
- **Features versus outcomes:** describe how a feature works, how it meets a requirement, and what testing showed.
- **Prototype versus proof:** show test criteria, feedback, results and resulting refinements; avoid anecdotal claims alone.
- **Presentation versus assessment:** polished presentation helps communication, but the booklet notes that delivery style/medium is not itself the assessed strand.

## Self-test

1. What was the highest overall project mark in the booklet, and which two criteria were full marks?
2. What common mismatch reduced the FPV-drone example’s marks?
3. What must connect a design specification to the user?
4. What evidence makes a prototype iteration assessable rather than merely a claim?
5. Why is final presentation not just a polished rendering?

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Example 1, 30/33; Criteria C (Ideation and modelling) and D (Designing a solution) earned 6/6 each.
> 2. It drifted toward creating a new product and its concepts/testing did not align clearly with the identified existing product, user problem and task analysis.
> 3. Research and task-analysis evidence, with requirements clearly tied to user needs and made sufficiently specific to test.
> 4. A stated requirement, test method/result or user feedback, and a visible refinement made because of that evidence.
> 5. The solution needs clear, sufficiently detailed communication and a direct comparison with the existing product/problem, not appearance alone.
