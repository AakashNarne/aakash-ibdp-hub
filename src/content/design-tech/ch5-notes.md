# DT C1.2 — Inclusive design

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **Inclusive design** makes mainstream products usable by the **widest possible audience, regardless of age or ability**. It extends **UCD** to people who are often forgotten: those who are pregnant, elderly, disabled or injured.
> - **Universal design** means "usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, **without the need for adaptation or specialised design**". It is *not* "one size fits all".
> - Inclusive design is **not always possible**. There is **no "average" person**, because body dimensions are not linearly correlated, so designers use the **5th–95th percentile** range, **adjustability** and specific user groups.
> - **Designing for extremes** (users with physical, sensory or cognitive impairments) often produces better products for everyone. Examples include the **OXO Good Grips** peeler (MoMA, 1994) and voice control.
> - Scale of need: the WHO estimates **1.3 billion people (16%, 1 in 6)** have a significant disability.

## 1. Inclusion (C1.2.1)

**Inclusive design**: ensures products address the needs of the **widest possible audience, regardless of age or ability**, focusing on universally acceptable products. It breaks down barriers so experiences are accessible and usable by everyone, whatever their physical or cognitive differences. (Metcalfe, printed p. 35.)

**Terminology.** Metcalfe says inclusive design principles are "sometimes called" **Universal Design**, **Universal Usability Design**, **Accessible Design** and **Design for All**. These principles are increasingly built into **building codes**. To be precise in an exam:

| Term | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Accessible design** | Removing barriers so people with disabilities *can* use something (often meeting a legal minimum) |
| **Inclusive design** | Designing mainstream products for as many people as possible, involving diverse users in the process |
| **Universal design** | The ideal: one design usable by all **without adaptation or specialised design** |

**Universal design definition** (Center for Universal Design): "the design of products and environments to be usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialized design." Its intent is to make products, communications and the built environment more usable by as many people as possible **at little or no extra cost**. It is often misread as "one size fits all", which falls short of its full intention.

> [!warning] Conflict: when was "universal design" coined?
> Metcalfe (printed p. 35) says the term was "originally used by Ron Mace (1998)". NC State University's history page cites design historian Bess Williamson (*Accessible America*): **Ronald L. Mace coined "universal design" in 1985**. Mace was an architect and wheelchair user who founded the Center for Accessible Housing in 1989, later the **Center for Universal Design**. The seven Principles of Universal Design (version 2.0) date from **1997**, and Mace died in 1998, which may explain the textbook's date. **Use "Ron Mace, 1980s (1985)"** and note the textbook's date if asked.

### The seven principles of universal design

| Principle (NC State, 1997) | Metcalfe's wording | Meaning and quick example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Equitable use** | equity in use | Useful and marketable to people with diverse abilities; the same means of use for all (automatic doors) |
| 2. **Flexibility in use** | flexibility in service | Accommodates a range of preferences and abilities (left- and right-handed scissors) |
| 3. **Simple and intuitive use** | simple and intuitive operation | Easy to understand whatever the user's experience, language or concentration (pictogram signs) |
| 4. **Perceptible information** | communicate unambiguous information | Communicates through several senses (visual and audible alarms) |
| 5. **Tolerance for error** | allowing tolerance for error | Minimises hazards of accidental actions ("undo", guarded switches) |
| 6. **Low physical effort** | need low physical effort | Usable with minimum fatigue (lever door handles) |
| 7. **Size and space for approach and use** | provide space for operators and use | Space for reach and manipulation whatever the user's body size, posture or mobility (wide doorways) |

### Legislation
In many industrialised nations, laws require people with disabilities to be considered. **Human-factors research** then turns those requirements into design data. Countries listed in Metcalfe:

- **Australia**: Disability Discrimination Act 1992
- **Canada**: Ontarians with Disabilities Act 2001
- **USA**: Americans with Disabilities Act 1990
- **South Africa**: Promotion of Equality and Prevention of Unfair Discrimination Act 2000
- **India**: Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act 1995

In **developing countries** formal laws may not exist. Informal guidelines and international standards promoted by **NGOs and government agencies** may still address disability to some degree.

### Everyday inclusive design (textbook examples)
- **Currency:**
  - coins of different size, thickness and edge detail
  - banknotes of different sizes, colours and textures
  - **raised "Braille" dots on polymer notes** (Australia, Canada, UK)
  - **intaglio** printing on paper notes
  - AI note-reader apps (CashReader, MCT Money Reader)
- **Tactile paving** and **raised dots on platform edges**, detectable by cane or foot.
- **Smartphones:**
  - text messaging and vibration for people with hearing impairments
  - **text-to-speech** for visual impairment and dyslexia
  - **translation apps** such as Google Translate for access to healthcare and services
- **Rumble strips** (audio-tactile lane markings), which alert drowsy or distracted drivers.

## 2. Inclusive design is not always possible (C1.2.2)

- The **50th percentile** (median) is the "average" for **one** body dimension. Body dimensions are **not linearly correlated**: someone with short arms does not necessarily have short legs. So there is **no truly "average" person**. Designing for the 50th percentile alone excludes many people, especially at the extremes and across **multiple dimensions**.
- **Historical data example:** Union soldiers aged 21–49 linked to the **1850 US census** had an average height of **5 ft 8 in (about 1.73 m)**. Probably no soldier was exactly that height, but the full distribution was useful for sizing clothing and weapons (Wilson & Pope, 2003, cited in Metcalfe).
- **What designers do instead:**
  1. Use **comprehensive anthropometric data** for the full range of body dimensions.
  2. Add **adjustable features** so users can customise the fit (adjustable chairs, steering columns).
  3. Identify **specific user groups** (by gender, age, physical limitations) and tailor the design.
  4. Design for a **range of percentiles, typically the 5th to 95th**, which still leaves out about 10% of users.
- **Why full inclusion can fail:**
  - **Cost.** Extra features or adjustability raise the price.
  - **Conflicting needs.** For example, tactile paving helps blind users but can be hard for some wheelchair or walker users.
  - **Technical limits.**
  - **Safety rules.**
  - The resulting **trade-offs** must be justified.

> [!example] Diagram 1: normal distribution and percentiles (describe it)
> Draw a bell curve. The horizontal axis is a body dimension, e.g. "stature (mm)", and the vertical axis is "relative frequency". Mark the **50th percentile** at the peak (mean = median). Shade the tails beyond the **5th** and **95th** percentiles and label them "excluded (~5% each)". Underneath, write: "design range 5th–95th = 90% accommodated for ONE dimension; fewer when several dimensions are combined."

**Which percentile?** Use the **5th** for **reach** (minimum reach in a workspace, so the smallest users can reach) and for **strength** (forces to operate controls). Use the **95th** for **clearance** (doorways, headroom, escape hatches).

## 3. Design for extremes (C1.2.3)

**Design for extremes**: developing solutions for users with **physical, sensory and cognitive impairments** that turn out to suit the general population too.

| Advantage (Metcalfe) | Example |
|---|---|
| **Inclusive design**: benefits a wider audience | Voice commands for visually impaired users help anyone who needs hands-free use |
| **Innovative solutions**: unexpected perspectives | Extreme users reveal problems mainstream users put up with |
| **Future-proof designs** | Ageing populations will need today's "extreme" features |
| **Improved user experience** for everyone | Bigger grips and clearer displays help all users |
| **Inspiration**: designers rethink assumptions | Moves designers out of their comfort zones |
| **Improved accessibility** | Products become usable by more people |

**Limitations:** it may add cost or complexity; it still excludes people beyond the chosen extremes; and "extreme" users differ a lot, so the designer must choose which extremes to target.

> [!example] Case study 1: OXO Good Grips peeler (1990s)
> - **Origin:** **Sam Farber** saw his wife, who had **arthritis**, struggling to hold a vegetable peeler.
> - **Design:**
>   - a martensitic stainless-steel, half-moon-shaped slotted **swivel blade** in a rigid nylon frame
>   - an **oversized non-slip handle** made of the thermoplastic elastomer **Santoprene**
>   - a "soft spot": a scooped region with **fins** for the thumb and forefinger, so only a **light grip** is needed
> - **Outcome:** easier grip and less hand strain for people with arthritis, and more comfortable for everyone. It became a mainstream product, not a "disability aid". Inducted into the **Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York, in 1994**.
> - **Exam use:** design for extremes leading to universal benefit; ergonomic material choice (Santoprene).

> [!example] Case study 2: Xbox Adaptive Controller (Microsoft, announced 16 May 2018)
> - **Target extreme users:** gamers with **limited mobility**.
> - **Process:** co-developed with **AbleGamers, the Cerebral Palsy Foundation, Craig Hospital, SpecialEffect and Warfighter Engaged**, working directly with gamers.
> - **Design:** works with **adaptive switches users may already own** and has **two large built-in buttons** that can be reprogrammed to any standard input (flexibility in use, low physical effort).
> - **Cost as an inclusion issue:** **USD 99.99**, which Microsoft said was "significant value over the customized alternatives that exist today".
> - **Exam use:** inclusive design delivered through UCD; adaptability rather than one fixed design; affordability.

> [!example] Scale of need: WHO (7 March 2023)
> An estimated **1.3 billion people, 16% of the world's population (1 in 6)**, have a significant disability. The number is **growing** as people live longer and noncommunicable diseases increase. People with disabilities find inaccessible, unaffordable transport **15 times** more difficult. For designers, that is a very large user group and market that exclusive design ignores.

## 4. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| "Universal design = one size fits all" | It aims for usability by all **without adaptation**, often through flexibility and adjustability |
| Designing for the "average person" | There is no average person across several dimensions; use 5th–95th percentiles and adjustability |
| Using the 95th percentile for reach | Reach and strength use the **5th**; clearance uses the **95th** |
| Treating inclusive design as only for disabled people | It includes the elderly, pregnant, injured, children, left-handed people, non-native speakers… and benefits everyone |
| Claiming inclusive design is always achievable | C1.2.2: cost, conflicting needs and technical limits mean trade-offs |
| Quoting "Ron Mace 1998" as fact | Note the conflict: Mace coined the term in the **1980s (1985)**; the 7 principles date from 1997 |

## 5. Self-test

1. **Define** inclusive design and **distinguish** it from universal design.
2. **Explain** why designing for the 50th percentile can make a product less inclusive.
3. **State** which percentile is used for minimum reach in a workspace, and **justify** your answer.
4. **Explain**, using the OXO Good Grips peeler, how designing for extremes benefits the general population.
5. **Evaluate** whether inclusive design is always achievable for a designer.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Inclusive design: designing mainstream products to meet the needs of the widest possible audience regardless of age or ability, extending UCD to forgotten groups. Universal design is the ideal of one design usable by all people, as far as possible, without adaptation or specialised design.
> 2. Body dimensions are not linearly correlated, so no one is "average" in every dimension. A design based on the median for one dimension excludes around half the population on that dimension, and even more when several dimensions combine.
> 3. The 5th percentile. If the smallest users can reach the control, everyone larger can too.
> 4. Sam Farber designed the oversized, non-slip Santoprene handle with a finned soft spot for his wife's arthritis. It needs only a light grip and reduces strain, which makes it more comfortable for all users. It became a mainstream best-seller and entered MoMA in 1994.
> 5. Arguments for: legislation (e.g. ADA 1990, DDA 1992), a huge user group (WHO: 1.3 billion), and better products for everyone. Arguments against: cost, conflicting needs between user groups, technical and safety limits, and the 5th–95th range still excluding about 10%. Judgement: full inclusion is an ideal. Designers should maximise inclusion through adjustability, extreme-user research and multi-sensory information, and justify any trade-offs.
