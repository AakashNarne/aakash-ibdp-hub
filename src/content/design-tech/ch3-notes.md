# DT B1.1 — User-centred design

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - **User-centred design (UCD)** keeps the needs, preferences and limitations of **end-users** at the centre of the *whole* development process, through an **iterative** loop of research → design → test with users → refine.
> - Match the research method to the stage: **field research** and **open-ended** questions early (discover issues); **structured interviews** and **fixed-response questionnaires** (e.g. **SUS**) later (measure and compare).
> - **Personae** are fictional but research-based profiles of a user population (primary, secondary); an **anti-persona** is a group the design is *not* for, or who could misuse it.
> - The **five E's** turn "usable" into measurable objectives: **E**ffective, **E**fficient, **E**ngaging, **E**rror tolerant, **E**asy to learn.
> - **Task analysis** (HTA = tree of goals and sub-tasks; CTA = the thinking behind each step) finds pain points; **poka-yoke** designs mistakes out.

**Syllabus scope** (from [[_Syllabus Index]]): B1.1.1 understanding needs / planning a UCD inquiry; B1.1.2 UCD as a design process (research methods); B1.1.3 personae; B1.1.4 usability objectives; B1.1.5 task analysis.
Builds on: [[DT A1.1 — Ergonomics — Notes]] (percentiles, anthropometrics). Leads to: [[DT C1.2 — Inclusive design — Notes]], [[DT C1.1 — Responsibility of the designer — Notes]].

## 1. Planning a UCD inquiry (B1.1.1)

**User-centred design (UCD)**: a design approach that focuses on understanding the needs, preferences and limitations of end-users throughout product development, actively engaging users in an iterative process and using their feedback so that products are **usable, accessible and enjoyable**. (Metcalfe, printed p. 13.)

The textbook groups the questions a designer plans to ask (in surveys, focus groups, interviews) into four areas:

| Area | Example planning questions |
|---|---|
| **User experience** | What do users expect? Is this a new problem? How do current products solve it? Which features are product-critical? |
| **Understanding user needs** | Who is the target market? What critical functions must users achieve? What problems do current products cause? |
| **Context of use** | What environmental or contextual factors affect how users interact with the product? |
| **Usability and accessibility** | What accessibility issues need consideration? How can the product be intuitive and easy to learn? |

When planning the UCD process the designer must decide:
1. the best methods for **gathering and incorporating user feedback**;
2. techniques for **data collection, analysis and presentation**;
3. how **insights, decisions and iterations** will feed back into the overall design process.

> [!example] Diagram 1: the UCD loop (describe it in words)
> Draw four boxes in a circle with arrows clockwise: **Understand users & context** (research) → **Specify requirements** (personae, five E's objectives) → **Design solutions** (sketches, prototypes) → **Evaluate with users** (usability tests, SUS) → back to the first box. Label the arrow back "iterate until benchmarks are met". Put "USER" in the centre to show users are involved at every stage, not only at the start.

## 2. User-centred research methods (B1.1.2)

### 2.1 Field research
Observing people **in their natural environment**, usually early in the design process. Techniques: **field trials**, **ethnographic interviews** (ethnography = the scientific description of peoples and cultures, their customs and differences), **observation of everyday activities**.

Because it happens where the product is really used: data is obtained **in the context of use**; **previously unrecognised issues** are discovered; there are **no artificial effects** of laboratory testing.

### 2.2 Observation
Watching users as they use a product, in a **laboratory** (controlled) or in the **field**. Purpose: find how a product is actually used and what usability problems occur. The **observer effect** — users behave differently because they are being watched — must be recognised and minimised.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Can uncover previously unrecognised usability problems | Data can be complex to analyse |
| Product tested under actual conditions of use | Environmental "noise" may hide small effects |
| | Usually only possible on finished products/systems |

### 2.3 Interviews
The observer questions the user directly. They can be **unstructured**, **semi-structured** or **structured**.

| | Unstructured | Structured (standardised) |
|---|---|---|
| Questions | Mainly **open-ended** ("What features did you find most useful?") | Mainly **closed** / fixed responses ("Did you like the positioning of the sound controls?") |
| Purpose | Explore, uncover unexpected issues | Fair, objective, consistent evaluation |
| Advantages | Interviewee can ask for clarification | Better at predicting actual user experience; consistent across participants (more reliable, valid); easier to collect, analyse and compare |
| Disadvantages | Time-consuming; small groups may miss issues; not anonymous, so answers may try to please the interviewer | Predetermined questions limit flexibility and nuanced follow-up |

A **Likert scale** (Rensis Likert, 1932) asks for a rating, typically 1–5 (e.g. strongly disagree → strongly agree). It can be used inside a structured interview or as a stand-alone questionnaire.

### 2.4 Focus groups
Small groups of **8–12 participants** led by a **facilitator**, who introduces the topic, keeps discussion going, makes sure everyone can speak, and steers back on topic without stifling interaction.
- **Advantages:** uncover previously unrecognised usability problems; usable throughout the process but **especially useful early**.
- **Disadvantages:** data mainly **qualitative**; group dynamics can be disruptive; small groups may not represent all issues.

### 2.5 Questionnaires
Self-administered lists of questions completed **without outside interference**.

- **Fixed-response** questions → **quantitative** data (choices or a rating scale such as Likert). They must be tested for **reliability** (repeatability of responses) and **validity** (does the question measure what it is meant to?). Usually used in **later** evaluation.
- **Open-ended** questions → **qualitative** data in respondents' own words. They can reveal unexpected issues but take longer to analyse. Usually used **early**, to find the important issues.

| Advantages of questionnaires | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cover a wide geographic area | Without anonymity, answers may be inaccurate |
| Interviewer observation bias removed | Only a fraction are returned |
| Large samples possible | Returns may be biased (only interested people reply) |

#### The System Usability Scale (SUS)
A standard, validated 10-item Likert questionnaire developed by **John Brooke (1986)**. Odd items are positively worded ("I thought the tool was easy to use"), even items negatively worded ("I found the tool unnecessarily complex").

**Scoring (0–100):** odd items → (response − 1); even items → (5 − response); add the ten values; multiply by **2.5**.

> [!example] Worked SUS calculation (textbook data, printed p. 18)
> Responses: Q1 = 4, Q3 = 1, Q5 = 4, Q7 = 3, Q9 = 3; Q2 = 5, Q4 = 5, Q6 = 2, Q8 = 3, Q10 = 4.
> - Odd: (4−1) + (1−1) + (4−1) + (3−1) + (3−1) = 3 + 0 + 3 + 2 + 2 = **10**
> - Even: (5−5) + (5−5) + (5−2) + (5−3) + (5−4) = 0 + 0 + 3 + 2 + 1 = **6**
> - SUS = 2.5 × (10 + 6) = 2.5 × 16 = **40**
> - **Interpret:** the average SUS across industries is about **68**, so 40 signals serious usability problems.
> - Note: the textbook's intermediate line prints "3+0+3+3+2…", which would sum to 17; the bracketed terms give 16, and the printed final answer (40) matches 16.

### 2.6 Method of extremes
Designs around the **extremes** of the user population (plus the mean). The textbook's examples: doorways, ladders, step heights and escape hatches use the **95th percentile male** (clearance); forces to operate control-panel buttons use the **5th percentile female** (strength). This accommodates the greatest number of users, but **excludes anyone beyond the chosen extremes**. Designing for extremes often prioritises people with disabilities; **universal design** goes further, aiming for products usable by everyone **without adaptation**. → see [[DT C1.2 — Inclusive design — Notes]].

## 3. Personae, anti-personae and use cases (B1.1.3)

A **user population** is a group defined by common goals or needs. A **persona** is a **fictional but research-based** profile that represents that population. It is not hypothetical: it embodies ethnographic research and field-trial observations — what people were *seen* to do, not just what they *say* they would do.

Personae let designers:
- prioritise design activities and focus development on user goals;
- understand customer motivations and spot gaps in a market;
- test and validate concepts against requirements without endless usability tests;
- avoid surveying the whole user community;
- share one consistent reference across the team, reducing disagreement.

A profile usually includes a name, photo and details such as age, health, gender, interests, activities, life goals, education, motivation, employment, expectations, marital status and affiliations.

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Primary persona** | The main user population; the design must satisfy these requirements |
| **Secondary persona** | Related groups whose extra needs are accommodated where possible |
| **Anti-persona** | A group the design is *not* for — Nielsen Norman Group: "a representation of a user group that could misuse a product in ways that negatively impact target users and the business" |

**Anti-persona examples (textbook):** McDonald's uses one so its marketing does not target or misinform vegans; anti-personae help defend systems against hackers (data breaches, identity theft, denial-of-service attacks); they are also used to test safety issues and risk of product damage. Trainline lets non-purchasers plan journeys so even non-paying users have a positive experience.

**Criticisms of personae:** the size of the group a persona represents cannot be verified; its validity cannot be independently assessed; the method still lacks peer-reviewed validation; ambiguity can cause team conflict; personae can become **stereotypes**.

A **use case** is a written description of how a user will interact with a product, **from the user's perspective**, giving insight into usability as experienced.

## 4. Usability objectives: the five E's (B1.1.4)

Splitting usability into five measurable goals lets designers test each part separately, then judge the whole. Each needs a **precise objective with a measurable outcome** (textbook examples):

| E | Focus | Example measurable objective |
|---|---|---|
| **Effective** | Functionality | Does the product meet the performance requirements of most users? |
| **Efficient** | Memorability / speed | 85% of registrations completed without any omissions |
| **Engaging** | Satisfaction | 90% of users report high confidence on a Likert test |
| **Error tolerant** | Errors | All forms completed correctly thanks to an error-checking confirmation step |
| **Easy to learn** | Learnability | Intuitive design guides users predictably without excessive help add-ons |

The data shows where to improve, guides the next iteration, and shows **when benchmarks are achieved** (the process is complete). It also reveals that different user groups prioritise different E's.

## 5. Task analysis and poka-yoke (B1.1.5)

**Task analysis**: a strategy to develop empathy and understand how users perform a task to reach their goal, by breaking complex interactions into small units to find **cognitive overload, ambiguous instructions or inefficient workflows**.

- **Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA)**: breaks a task into a **hierarchy of goals, sub-goals, operations and plans** — a tree diagram.
- **Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA)**: studies the **thinking** — knowledge, skills, decisions — needed for each step. HTA and CTA are often combined.

**Steps:** (1) identify the tasks users must complete (observation, interviews, surveys); (2) break each into smaller chunks; (3) evaluate each step for actions, decisions, problems, pain points; (4) document with flowcharts, diagrams, notes; (5) **retest** the modified product with users.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| More intuitive, user-friendly interfaces | Time-consuming for complex tasks |
| Identifies pain points early | Requires expertise in user behaviour |
| Removes unnecessary steps (efficiency) | Subjective (pain points depend on interpretation) |
| Anticipates errors, so fewer mistakes | Limited scope: one task, not the whole experience |

> [!example] Diagram 3: HTA tree (describe it)
> Top box: **0. Make a cup of tea**. Below it, sub-goals: **1. Boil water**, **2. Prepare cup**, **3. Brew**, **4. Serve**. Under "1. Boil water": 1.1 fill kettle, 1.2 switch on, 1.3 wait for click. Write a **plan** beside each level, e.g. "Plan 0: do 1 and 2 in any order, then 3, then 4". Circle any step where users hesitate or make errors — those are the pain points to redesign.

**Poka-yoke** ("mistake-proofing"), developed by a Toyota engineer in the 1960s, shapes behaviour so errors cannot happen. Textbook examples: batteries that only fit the correct way; child-proof push-and-twist caps; differently coloured and sized diesel nozzles; a microwave that only runs with the door shut; surgical sponge checklists; limit switches on lathes. A **chain saw** will not start without the choke, switch and three triggers set, and it needs both hands to hold, so no hand is free to touch the chain; a kick-back pushes the wrist onto the automatic chain brake.

## 6. Case study: Xbox Adaptive Controller (Microsoft, announced 16 May 2018)

- **Users involved from the start:** Microsoft developed it in partnership with **The AbleGamers Charity, The Cerebral Palsy Foundation, Craig Hospital, SpecialEffect and Warfighter Engaged**, and "worked closely… directly with gamers who have limited mobility". That is UCD: research with real users in their context, not designer assumptions.
- **Design response to user needs:** it works with **adaptive switches users may already own** and has **two large built-in buttons** that can be reprogrammed to any standard controller input (flexibility, error tolerance).
- **Affordability as a requirement:** priced at **USD 99.99**, which Microsoft said offered "significant value over the customized alternatives that exist today".
- **Exam use:** illustrates field research, co-design with user organisations, personae of gamers with limited mobility, and the overlap between UCD and inclusive design.

## 7. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Treating UCD as "ask users once at the start" | Stress **iteration**: users are involved at every stage, and designs are retested |
| Calling a persona "a made-up customer" | Say it is **fictional but based on ethnographic research** |
| Confusing an anti-persona with a secondary persona | Secondary = extra users to accommodate; anti = **not** intended, or likely to misuse |
| Using qualitative and quantitative interchangeably | Open-ended/focus groups → qualitative; fixed-response/Likert/SUS → quantitative |
| Forgetting the observer effect | Name it and say how to minimise it (unobtrusive observation, acclimatisation) |
| SUS: adding raw scores | Convert first (odd: −1; even: 5 − x), sum, then **× 2.5** |
| Mixing up the five E's labels | Effective, Efficient, Engaging, Error tolerant, Easy to learn — **not** "eco-friendly" |

## 8. Self-test

1. **Define** user-centred design and **state** two questions a designer might plan to ask about the context of use.
2. **Distinguish** between structured and unstructured interviews, giving one advantage of each.
3. A user's SUS responses are Q1–Q10 = 5, 2, 4, 1, 5, 2, 4, 1, 4, 2. **Calculate** the SUS score and **comment** on it.
4. **Explain** how an anti-persona can be used by designers, with an example.
5. **Outline** the steps of a task analysis and **state** one limitation.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. UCD is a design approach that keeps end-users' needs, preferences and limitations at the centre of the whole development process, through iterative user feedback, to make products usable, accessible and enjoyable. Context questions: Where and in what conditions will it be used (light, noise, weather)? What else is the user doing at the same time?
> 2. Unstructured: open-ended questions, answers in users' own words; advantage — interviewee can ask for clarification and unexpected issues emerge. Structured: fixed/closed questions in a set order; advantage — consistent, comparable, easier to analyse, and better at predicting actual user experience.
> 3. Odd: (5−1)+(4−1)+(5−1)+(4−1)+(4−1) = 4+3+4+3+3 = 17. Even: (5−2)+(5−1)+(5−2)+(5−1)+(5−2) = 3+4+3+4+3 = 17. SUS = 2.5 × 34 = 85. This is well above the ~68 average, so usability is good.
> 4. An anti-persona represents a group the product is not for, or who could misuse it. Designers test against it for safety, security or marketing problems, e.g. modelling a hacker to design defences against data breaches, or McDonald's ensuring marketing does not target vegans.
> 5. Identify tasks → break into sub-tasks → evaluate each for problems → document (flowcharts, HTA diagram) → retest with users. Limitation: time-consuming, subjective, or limited to single tasks rather than the whole experience.
