# DT C1.1 — Responsibility of the designer

> [!tip] If you remember nothing else
> - A designer is responsible to **clients, the community and the environment**, and must balance these when they compete. It is a continuing commitment (**Kaizen**, continuous improvement, the **PDCA** cycle), not a one-off.
> - **Environmental responsibility** means thinking about the whole life cycle, from material sourcing to disposal. The **Brundtland Report (1987)** popularised **sustainable development**; the **circular economy** aims for durable, repairable, recyclable products.
> - **Safety** comes from **standards and legislation** (e.g. ASTM F963, ISO 8124 for toys) and **standardisation and interchangeability** (weights and measures, bolt property classes).
> - **Planned obsolescence** deliberately limits lifespan to drive repeat sales. The four types are **style**, **functional**, **technological** and **social**. It boosts sales and choice but increases waste.
> - Real consequences: France's DGCCRF fined Apple **EUR 25 million** (7 Feb 2020) over iPhone slowdowns. The EU made **USB-C** the common charger from **28 Dec 2024**.

**Syllabus scope** (from [[_Syllabus Index]]): C1.1.1 responsibility to clients, community and environment; C1.1.2 safety and standards; C1.1.3 obsolescence (planned, style, functional, technological).

## 1. The designer's responsibility (C1.1.1)

Designers **translate user needs and business goals** into tangible, usable and aesthetically pleasing products, through research, prototyping, testing and iteration. Metcalfe describes them as a "bridge between creativity and technical feasibility".

They are responsible for the **rational use of**:
- materials
- manufacturing processes
- energy
- recyclability and reusability

Doing this requires an up-to-date understanding of the relevant science and technology.

| Stakeholder | What the designer owes them | Typical conflict |
|---|---|---|
| **Client** | A product that meets the brief, cost and market goals | Lowest cost vs durability or repairability |
| **Community / users** | Safety, health, accessibility, honest information | Fast, cheap production vs worker and user safety |
| **Environment** | Minimal resource use, pollution and waste across the life cycle | Short replacement cycles vs resource depletion |

### Kaizen and the PDCA cycle
- **Kaizen** is Japanese for "continuous improvement" or "change for the better". Small, gradual changes add up to big improvements. Kaizen means understanding customer needs and testing improvements on a small scale before rolling them out company-wide.
- It was introduced in Japan in the **1950s** during post-war reconstruction by **W. Edwards Deming**. Deming built on **Walter A. Shewhart's** statistical **PDSA** cycle (Plan, Do, Study, Act), which became known as **PDCA** (Plan, Do, Check, Act).

> [!example] Diagram 1: PDCA cycle (describe it)
> Draw a circle divided into four quarters, with arrows running clockwise: **Plan** (find the problem, plan a change) → **Do** (try it on a small scale) → **Check** (measure the results against the plan) → **Act** (adopt it company-wide, or adjust). Add a wedge under the circle, labelled "standardise", to show that each turn lifts quality up a slope, which is continuous improvement.

### Environmental sustainability
- The **Brundtland Report**, *Our Common Future*, was published in **1987** by the UN **World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED)**, chaired by **Gro Harlem Brundtland**. It popularised **sustainable development**.
- **Circular-economy principles** consider the whole product life cycle. They lead to durable, repairable and recyclable products, with positive impacts on **people, planet and profit** (the Triple Bottom Line).
- **Eco-friendly product examples (textbook):**
  - organic-cotton reusable bags
  - recycled PET bottles
  - solar devices
  - biodegradable or compostable packaging
  - recycled clothing

**Harmful products and materials the designer should avoid (textbook):**

| Harm | Detail |
|---|---|
| Microplastics | Persist in the environment |
| **"Forever chemicals"**: PFAS, PCBs, dioxin | Resist degradation and accumulate in groundwater. PFAS from firefighting foam has contaminated groundwater |
| CFC and HCFC aerosols | Damage the stratospheric ozone layer |
| Disposable plastics | Much of it reaches the oceans, harming marine life |
| **Fast fashion** | Criticised as up to **8–10% of global CO₂**. About **87%** of textile waste goes to landfill each year |
| Mercury | **Minamata disease** (Japan, 1960s): methylmercury from a chemical factory contaminated the fish people ate |
| Vehicle emissions (lead, sulphur, NOₓ, PM2.5) | Cause respiratory, cardiovascular and neurological harm |

## 2. Safety, standards and standardisation (C1.1.2)

A **product standard** is a documented guideline that specifies a product's technical requirements, ensuring its **safety, quality, consistency and suitability**. Standards give consumers confidence across manufacturers and markets, and give designers a **framework** for materials, manufacturing, performance and testing.

- **Toy safety (textbook example):**
  - **ASTM F963** (USA) covers material properties such as toxicity and flammability, plus age labelling and hazards for children under 14.
  - **ISO 8124-1** covers mechanical and physical properties, with later parts covering flammability and toxicology.
- **Enforcement authorities:** Australia's **ACCC**, the US **Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)**, and Europe's **PROSAFE**. Products carry compliance marks.
- Standardisation is typical of **mature markets**. It assists quality assurance, component supply and R&D costs, and acts as a "common language" for global design.
- **ISO standardisation space (Sanders, 1972):** three axes.
  - **X**: domain (the subject being standardised)
  - **Y**: aspects (terminology, specification, testing, grading, packaging…)
  - **Z**: level (company → national → regional → international)

### Standardisation and interchangeability
**Standardisation** means establishing common practices, methods or formats so things are consistent. Weights and measures are the classic example: before them, a "foot" varied from place to place, which confused trade.

**Interchangeability** means parts from different makers can replace each other.

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| **1776** | **Gribeauval** (France) standardised artillery: cannons, carriages, ammunition |
| **1778** | **Honoré Blanc** extended the system to the flintlock musket, using jigs and gauges so hand-made parts were interchangeable |
| **1816–1818** | **John Hancock Hall**, **Simon North** and **Thomas Blanchard** (USA) mechanised mass production of truly interchangeable parts |
| Today | Standard bolt designs and **property classes** marked on the bolt head guarantee strength and interchangeability worldwide |

> [!example] Case study: EU common charger (Directive (EU) 2022/2380)
> - **USB-C** has been the mandatory common charging port in the EU **since 28 December 2024** for phones, tablets, cameras, headphones, portable speakers, handheld consoles, e-readers, keyboards, mice and more. It has applied to **laptops since 28 April 2026**.
> - Devices can be sold **without a charger** (unbundling), and labels must state power requirements.
> - The European Commission estimates **about 980 tonnes less e-waste per year** and consumer savings of **at least EUR 250 million a year** on unnecessary chargers.
> - **Exam use:** standardisation and interchangeability enforced by legislation, the designer's environmental responsibility, and a curb on technological obsolescence of accessories.

## 3. Obsolescence (C1.1.3)

**Planned (built-in) obsolescence** is a deliberate tactic by manufacturers and designers to give products a **limited lifespan**, forcing repeat purchase. Metcalfe documents it as early as the **1930s**, when General Electric made flashlight lamps more efficient but shorter-lived. It appears in software, phones and cars.

**Design features of planned obsolescence:**
- low durability
- style obsolescence
- not easily or cheaply repaired
- hard to disassemble
- regular release of "improved" models

Cutting costs this way is sometimes called **value engineering**. As markets mature and competition grows, consumers move to **more durable** brands, which puts pressure on lifespans to increase.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Consumers can upgrade to new technology and follow fashion | Constant replacement and inconvenience |
| Volume sales strengthen national economies and jobs | Poor repairability forces early purchase |
| Firms keep a competitive edge by refreshing products | New ways of operating can confuse some users |
| | **Environment:** more waste, pollution, energy and resource use (harms the "Planet" in the TBL) |

### Types of obsolescence

| Type | Meaning | Textbook examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Style (fashion)** | Product still works but is no longer "trendy". Trends are driven by social influence, the desire to fit in, novelty seeking, influencers (TikTok, YouTube, Instagram) and marketing. Short-lived trends are **fads** | Zoot suits (1930s–40s); paisley (1960s hippie culture); bell-bottoms (1960s–70s, back as "boot-cut" in the mid-1990s–2000s); shoulder pads (1930s–40s and 1980s "power dressing"). Fads: troll dolls, Pet Rocks (1975), Beanie Babies, Tamagotchi |
| **Functional** | Less useful because the design or features are outdated, even though it still works; leads to loss of value (depreciation) | Houses where you walk through one bedroom to reach another; wiring too weak for modern appliances; no indoor plumbing; older phone models; makers ending updates or repairs |
| **Technological** | Superseded by a more efficient technology, which can happen before functional obsolescence | Hourglass/sundial → clocks; ice chests → refrigerators; CRT → plasma → LCD/OLED; Edison cylinders (1877) → shellac 78s (1898) → vinyl 45/33 rpm (1948) → tape (1960s) → CD (1980) → streaming; vacuum tubes → transistors; Bessemer (1856) → open hearth (1860s) → BOF/EAF (from 1948); floppy discs → CD-ROM, DVD, USB |
| **Social** (named in the textbook's sample questions) | Product dropped because of social pressure or status, e.g. wanting the latest phone so as not to look out of date | Upgrading a working phone for the newest model |

**The Veblen effect** is named after **Thorstein Veblen (1857–1929)**. It is counter-intuitive consumer behaviour where a **higher price raises demand**, because price is taken as a sign of quality and **conspicuous consumption** brings social prestige. **Value for money** is about cost and performance; **perceived value** also includes prestige and rarity.

> [!example] Diagram 2: repairable vs reliable (Metcalfe's computer comparison)
> Draw two boxes side by side. On the left, a **1950s computer with vacuum tubes**, labelled "unreliable but repairable" (individual tubes can be swapped). On the right, a **2000s computer with integrated circuits**, labelled "reliable but unrepairable" (a whole board is replaced). Add an arrow underneath labelled "integration ↑, repairability ↓", the trade-off designers face.

> [!example] Case study: Apple iPhone slowdowns, France (DGCCRF, 7 February 2020)
> - **What happened:** iOS updates (Next INpact names **iOS 11.2**) included dynamic power management that, mainly when batteries were old, could **slow iPhone 6, SE and 7** models. Users could not roll back the update, so many felt pushed to buy a new battery or phone.
> - **Process:** the association **HOP (Halte à l'Obsolescence Programmée)** complained in **December 2017**. The Paris prosecutor asked the consumer authority **DGCCRF** to investigate.
> - **Outcome:** after nearly two years, Apple accepted a **criminal settlement**. It paid **EUR 25 million** and published a statement on its website **for one month**. The offence settled was **misleading commercial practice** by omission, *not* the specific planned-obsolescence offence.
> - **Exam use:** consequences of planned or technological obsolescence for profitability and consumer trust; the role of legislation and NGOs; evaluating designer responsibility (transparency, repairable batteries).

## 4. Common mistakes / examiner traps

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Treating style and planned obsolescence as the same thing | Style is one *type* or *feature*; planned obsolescence is the overall *strategy* of limiting lifespan |
| Confusing functional and technological obsolescence | Functional: design or features outdated, but it still works. Technological: replaced by a better technology |
| Saying planned obsolescence is "all bad" | Give both sides: upgrades, jobs and choice vs waste, cost and inconvenience; then **evaluate** |
| Mixing up standardisation and interchangeability | Standardisation = common specifications; interchangeability = the *result*, parts swap between makers |
| Calling the Brundtland Report an EU document | It is the **UN WCED, 1987** |
| Saying Apple was convicted of "planned obsolescence" in France | It was a **settlement for misleading practice**, EUR 25 million |

## 5. Self-test

1. **Outline** two responsibilities a designer has towards the environment.
2. **Explain** how the PDCA cycle supports continuous product development.
3. **Distinguish** between functional and technological obsolescence, with an example of each.
4. **Explain** how standardisation and interchangeability benefit designers and consumers, using the EU common-charger rule.
5. **Evaluate** planned obsolescence from the points of view of manufacturers, consumers and the environment.

> [!question]- Answers
> 1. Any two: choose eco-friendly or renewable materials; minimise energy use; design for recyclability, repair and disassembly (circular economy); avoid harmful substances such as PFAS or microplastics; consider the whole life cycle from sourcing to disposal.
> 2. Plan a small change, Do it on a small scale, Check the results against the plan, then Act by standardising it or adjusting. Repeating the cycle gives gradual, measurable improvements (Kaizen) instead of one-off redesigns.
> 3. Functional: the product still works but its design or features are outdated, e.g. a house where you walk through one bedroom to reach another, or an old phone model. Technological: a newer technology replaces it, e.g. CRT displays replaced by LCD/OLED.
> 4. Designers get a framework and can source compatible parts globally. Consumers get compatibility, safety and lower cost. Since 28 Dec 2024 USB-C is the common EU port, so one charger fits many devices and devices can be sold without chargers. The Commission estimates about 980 t less e-waste and at least EUR 250 million saved each year.
> 5. Manufacturers: more repeat sales, competitive refresh, jobs, but a risk to reputation and of fines (Apple, EUR 25 million, France, 2020). Consumers: access to upgrades and trends, but forced replacement and cost. Environment: more waste, resource use and pollution. Judgement: acceptable only where it delivers real improvement and is transparent and repairable; otherwise it conflicts with the designer's responsibility.
