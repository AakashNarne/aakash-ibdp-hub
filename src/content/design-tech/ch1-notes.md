## Section 1.2 — A 1.1.1 Ergonomics

> **A 1.1.1** Ergonomics is the relationship and interaction between people (aspects of the human
> body) and the products, systems and environments they use. *(© IBO 2025)*

Ergonomics, also called **human factors engineering**, studies the relationship between people and
systems, artifacts and environments. It has roots in ancient societies.

**Why designers prioritise it.** Products designed ergonomically are more likely to be
**functional**, more **comfortable**, and **safe and efficient** to use. Products that excel
ergonomically often gain popularity in the marketplace and can **command a premium price**.

### The book's six application areas

Learn these — the exam asks you to cite specific examples.

| Area | What is designed, and how |
|---|---|
| **Office chairs** | **Adjustable height** lets feet rest flat with **thighs parallel to the ground**, maintaining a **neutral spine position**. **Lumbar support** maintains the natural curve of the spine, preventing slouching and relieving pressure on spinal discs. **Armrests** support arms and shoulders, preventing neck and shoulder strain. |
| **Keyboards and mice** | Wrist support, alternative keyboard arrangements, button placement — reduce strain on wrists and hands *and* improve workplace productivity. |
| **Medical devices** | Reduce physical strain on healthcare providers, sustaining performance and reducing error. Prevent accidental injuries and ensure reliable operation. |
| **Automotive** | Seats, steering wheels, control panels. **Head-Up Displays (HUDs)** project driving information onto the windshield so drivers access data **without diverting their gaze from the road** — safety *and* accessibility. Smartphone connectivity keeps hands on the wheel. |
| **Consumer electronics** | Smartphones, tablets, wearables designed to minimise physical strain during extended use. |
| **Household items** | **Kitchen knives** designed around muscle exertion, comfort, efficiency and slicing performance — a chef's knife should feel like an **extension of the hand**. Lightweight non-slip materials, textured surfaces, and **weight distribution (balance)** for control and manoeuvrability. |

## Section 1.3 — A 1.1.2 Anthropometrics

> **A 1.1.2** Anthropometrics involves the measurement of human physical dimensions expressed in
> the percentile range. *(© IBO 2025)*

**Anthropometry** is the study of human body properties such as **height, mass and volume**.

The **WHO** report *Physical status: the use and interpretation of anthropometry* (1995) calls it:

> "the single most universally applicable, inexpensive and non-invasive method available to assess
> the size, proportions, and composition of the human body"

### The two types — and the mistake to stop making

> [!danger] Correction to your old notes
> There are **TWO** types, not four. The book pairs the words: **"structural *or* static"** and
> **"functional *or* dynamic"**. Writing static, dynamic, structural and functional as four
> separate categories in an exam is wrong.

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural (static)** | Measurements such as those made between joints, taken with standardised equipment like **calipers**. Easy to collect **because the subject is not moving**. | Height, weight, body-structure dimensions |
| **Functional (dynamic)** | Data obtained **while the subject is moving**. Harder to gather quantitatively, but often of **greater use** because it shows the **range and ease of movement**. | Reaction times, reach arcs, grip strengths |

### Instruments and data quality

Instruments must be **calibrated** and of **sturdy design** to give **reliable** results.

- **Most reliable and easily gathered:** height and weight.
- **Least reliable:** body fat from **skinfold calipers**, which is often unreliable across a population.

**Clothing.** Anthropometric data is meant to represent the **nude body**. Where cultural
restrictions prevent that, investigators make **allowances for the type and thickness of clothing**.

### Factors affecting the data

The book names **age, gender, ethnicity and disability**, then elaborates three:

- **Gender.** Men are on average taller and heavier, attributed to **hormonal influences**. Male
  bones are larger and denser — **maximum male bone mass is around 50% greater than women's**, and
  women lose bone more quickly with age. Women generally have a **higher percentage of body fat**,
  which matters for interpreting metrics like **BMI**.
- **Ethnicity.** Body proportions differ between groups — people of **African descent tend to have
  higher bone density and fewer fractures than Caucasians**. **Waist circumference** and
  **waist-to-hip ratio**, indicators of metabolic health, vary significantly between groups.
- **Disability.** Conditions affecting mobility such as **cerebral palsy** or **muscular dystrophy**
  produce measurements critical to designing environments and assistive devices. **Wheelchair users**
  may have different upper-body strength and proportions, affecting **biomechanical design**.

*(The book lists age but gives it no section of its own.)*

## Section 1.4 — A 1.1.3 Percentiles

> **A 1.1.3** Percentiles aid in the selection of appropriate anthropometric data to satisfy the
> majority of a user population. *(© IBO 2025)*

**Definition.** Percentiles refer to **100 equal groups** into which a sample population can be
divided according to the distribution of a particular variable. Someone in the **70th percentile**
has scored **as well as, or better than, 70%** of the sample.

**Percentile range** measures **dispersion**. A spread between the 95th and 5th is written
$$P_{95} - P_{5}$$

### The coverage arithmetic — learn these two numbers

**Within a single gender**, the 5th–95th range covers **90%** of people; only the top and bottom
5% fall outside.

**In a mixed population** of half men and half women, the same range covers **95%**, because only
the **top 5% of men** and the **bottom 5% of women** are excluded:

$$
(0.5 \times 5\%) + (0.5 \times 5\%) = 2.5\% + 2.5\% = 5\% \text{ excluded}
$$

### Normal (Gaussian) distribution

A large survey typically produces a **Gaussian** or **Normal distribution** — the **bell curve** —
defined by the **mean** and the **standard deviation**, written **sigma** $$\sigma$$.

- $$\pm 1\sigma$$ bounds approximately **68%** of the population
- $$\pm 2\sigma$$ bounds approximately **95%**

*Figure A 1.1.3a — Normal population distribution, x-axis from $$-3\sigma$$ to $$+3\sigma$$.*

> **Designers cannot accommodate every user — extremes of any population sample will always
> require special consideration.**

### Which percentile, and why

| Choice | Worked example from the book | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **95th only** | Standard **architectural doorway** | The tallest can pass; everyone smaller is catered for automatically |
| **5th only** | **Vehicle controls**, where **ease of reach** is the constraint | Data from those with the **shortest reach** sets the limit |
| **50th (the mean)** | Rarely adequate alone | The **majority of people are excluded** — they lie outside that group. Harder still when users cross **age and/or gender boundaries** |

### Case study — crash test dummies

This is the book's flagship percentile case. Know the dates.

| Year | Development |
|---|---|
| — | **'Sierra Sam'** — a **95th percentile male** dummy used by the **aviation industry** to test **ejection seats, aviation helmets and pilot restraints** |
| **1971** | **General Motors** created **Hybrid I**, modelled on the **50th percentile male** — average male height, mass and proportion |
| **1980s** | First **female dummy** introduced — essentially a **scaled-down male**, failing to account for **anatomical differences** between the sexes |
| **2022** | **Swedish researchers** developed a dummy accurately reflecting **female anatomy**, to capture injuries **women are more likely to sustain** |
| — | **THOR 5th** — a **5th percentile female**, an advanced **biofidelic anthropomorphic test device (ATD)** designed around women's physiology |

**The problem it exposes.** The lack of vehicle data for occupants other than a male driver left
**women and children outside design considerations**, creating a significant gap in safety data.

The **Hybrid III family** now covers a **male, a smaller female and three children** modelled on a
**6-year-old, a 3-year-old and a 12-month-old infant**.

**Children's products.** Designing **computer furniture for primary-school students** requires a
large percentile range — **5th to 95th, across genders and significant age variation** — to
**encourage correct posture, reduce fatigue, ease use and avert long-term health problems**.

## Section 1.5 — A 1.1.4 Product Sizing

> **A 1.1.4** To ensure products are appropriate to a range of percentiles, designers can choose to
> design products to be adjustable and/or to be produced in a range of sizes. *(© IBO 2025)*

**Designing for adjustability** means provision is made within the design for adjustments that
accommodate the **anthropometric variability** between members of the user group. Put more sharply:
**adjustability avoids anthropometric mismatch**.

**Four means of adjustment:** **mechanical, electrical, pneumatic or hydraulic**.

| Product | Adjustment provided |
|---|---|
| **Cars** | Seat height and steering wheel position |
| **Office chairs** | Height and backrest tilt |
| **Ironing board** | Several height positions, widening the population that can use it ergonomically |

*Figure A 1.1.4a — a population distribution curve where the broad population range is accommodated
by device adjustability; the shaded region runs from $$-2\sigma$$ to $$+2\sigma$$.*

### The 5th–95th design range

The commonly chosen **5th–95th percentile** range is a **trade-off** — it includes almost everyone
and excludes only the extremes. Traditionally it is held to give the **best coverage without being
skewed by extremes**.

Catering for a larger percentile range is called **"design for more types"**.

**Products designed to this range:** furniture, household appliances, *"one size fits all"*
free-size clothing. Consumers outside the range must seek **customised solutions**.

**Fashion** uses **both** adjustability and a size range — **drawstrings, elastic bands, belts and
adjustable straps** let consumers customise fit to their body shape.

## Section 1.6 — A 1.1.5 Sizing Considerations

> **A 1.1.5** In design, consideration must be given to work envelopes, reach, clearance,
> adjustability and range of sizes. *(© IBO 2025)*

### The standard adjustability regime

The percentile range generally considered appropriate is the **5th percentile of females to the
95th percentile of males**. Because male and female dimensions overlap, this accounts for **95% of
the user population** — **but only if human dimensions were always in the same proportion.**

**They are not.** A **tall person can have short arms, and a short person proportionally longer
arms**. **Multivariate analysis** accounts for all variations, and shows that **more than 5% of the
population may be excluded on one or more dimensions** even inside a 5th–95th range. The **cost of
accommodating all possible combinations increases dramatically** past that range and is usually not
justified.

> This is the highest-value nuance in A 1.1.5. Most students state the 5th–95th rule; the marks
> are in knowing **why it under-delivers**.

### The workstation

*Figure A 1.1.5a* shows an operator on an **adjustable chair** at an **adjustable screen**. The
**desk may be standard height** — the other adjustable elements together accommodate most users.

Labelled values to memorise:

| Label | Value |
|---|---|
| Viewing distance | **450–600 mm** |
| Viewing angle | marked |
| Wrists | straight |
| Lower back | lumbar support |
| Seat height | adjustable |
| Feet | flat on floor |

### Reach and work envelopes

The limiting factor is the **reach of the individual's arm**. The **reach envelope** for a range of
users is defined as a **three-dimensional space**.

Data comes from **anthropometric tables, dynamic measurement, or software simulations**, and can be
affected by restrictions such as **clothing**. Measurements were traditionally taken **manually**;
**video** has recently proved effective.

*Figure A 1.1.5b* shows operational areas from the **axis of bilateral symmetry** — **normal and
maximum working areas in the horizontal plane**:

| Arc | Radius |
|---|---|
| **Normal** working area | **40 cm** |
| **Maximum** working area | **50 cm** |

**Applications:** desk workspace, kitchen and bathroom design.

## Section 1.7 — A 1.1.6 Physiology

> **A 1.1.6** Physiology is the study of systems and biomechanics within the human body, their
> responses, limitations and capabilities. *(© IBO 2025)*

**Physiological data** is information gathered about the functioning of an individual's **major
organ systems**.

*Figure A 1.1.6a — Physiological data collection:*

| System | Data gathering |
|---|---|
| Heart | Heart activity, blood pressure, heart rates |
| Brain | Responses of the central and peripheral nervous systems |
| Sight | Eye movement, tracking |
| Respiration | Lung capacity and oxygen exchange |
| Hearing | Audiology and balance information |

### Biomechanics

**Definition.** Biomechanics is the study of the **mechanical laws relating to the movement of
living organisms**, particularly animals and the human body.

**The designer's hidden assumptions.** Every device assumes the user can bring **sufficient
pressure** to push a button or toggle a switch, or **sufficient force** to turn a can opener or
corkscrew. These assumptions rest on anthropometric measurements of **strength, dexterity and fine
motor control**.

**When the assumption fails.** **Age-related muscle weakness** and conditions such as **arthritis,
Parkinson's disease and multiple sclerosis** significantly impact assumed capabilities. Accommodating
these users needs **special adaptations** to the original design, or **adaptive technologies that
amplify biomechanical capabilities**.

**Sport.** Biomechanics leads to equipment that **enhances performance, reduces fatigue or prevents
injury** — customised for **elite athletes**, then mass-produced for recreational users:

- More **responsive materials** for tennis racquets and golf clubs
- **Swimsuits creating less drag**
- **Damping technologies in javelins**, reducing vibration from transverse forces during initial acceleration

Also biomechanically engineered: **backpacks, child safety harnesses, tennis racquets**. Applied
across the whole population — **young, elderly and those with a disability** — this is called
**design for inclusion**.

**Helmets.** Protective helmets **add to the load resisted by the neck muscles** — a particular
concern for the **armed services and search and rescue**, where **night vision goggles or a HUD**
may be mounted on the front.

### Case study — packaging and older consumers

Older consumers struggle with **jar lids, soft drink bottle tops, ring pulls and child-resistant
screw caps**. **Reduced muscle strength, dexterity** and **arthritis** are the causes. Better
packaging or **assistive devices providing grip and mechanical advantage** solve it.

Three jar openers, each exploiting a different principle:

| Figure | Device | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| A 1.1.6c | Metal jar opener | **Mechanical advantage from the lever arm length**; **serrated metal contact strip** raises frictional grip force |
| A 1.1.6d | Soft-grip opener | **Cone-shaped flexible rubber moulding** fits a range of lid sizes; relies on **frictional resistance**; soft grip improves the user's feel of the lid |
| A 1.1.6e | Four-in-one opener | **2nd order lever** for mechanical advantage; **rubber-lined metal** gives a strong body with a **high coefficient of friction** |

### Colour perception

- **Visual appeal** — bright vibrant colours make a product stand out; neutral colours create
  **elegance and sophistication**.
- **Contrast and legibility** — high contrast between text and background improves **legibility**,
  from packaging to phone screens.
- **Inclusive design** — users with **colour blindness and other visual impairments** are addressed
  with **easily distinguishable colour combinations**; **colour contrast checkers** assist designers.
- **Branding** — consistent brand colours reinforce **identity and recognition**; **gold, silver and
  black** are perceived to convey **luxury and exclusivity**.
- **Contextual relevance** — **camouflage** to blend into an environment, or **bright colours as a
  safety feature**.

### Visual data

For normally sighted users, visual information is often the most important. To be useful it must be
**unambiguous**, conveying **relevant information when and where needed**, **meaningful and free from
distracting clutter**. Visual clues should indicate how equipment is used — if a door opens one way,
**push or pull should be displayed** *(Figure A 1.1.6f, L-shaped door handle with sign)*.

**Figure A 1.1.6g — the orange lifeboat.** **Orange is the most visible colour to the human eye**,
even in poor visibility or at night, giving **stark contrast against the blue of the sea** so rescue
teams can spot it in **dark, low-light or foggy conditions**.

### Hearing — four design purposes

| Purpose | How it is designed for |
|---|---|
| **Safety** | **Volume limiters** as software or hardware on devices such as headphones, set for children and adults, to reduce **hearing deterioration from high volumes over extended periods** |
| **Privacy** | **Soundproofing** of offices and bedrooms, preventing noise **ingress, egress or both** |
| **Alarms** | **Acoustic properties and intensities** engineered for **effective transmission and penetration across groups**, delivering timely warnings — police and ambulance sirens, fire and smoke alarms |
| **Environments** | **Acoustic treatment of surfaces** reduces **echoes and reverberation** that obscure speech or music |

## Section 1.8 — A 1.1.7 Psychology

> **A 1.1.7** Psychology is concerned with the study of the human mind and involves the study of all
> the human senses that may be involved in sending information to the brain. *(© IBO 2025)*

**Psychological factors** are those that **impact operations**, including environmental conditions
such as **stress, lighting, temperature, humidity, noise and vibration**.

*Figure A 1.1.7a — Psychological factors:*

| Stimulus | Factors affecting |
|---|---|
| Sight | Ease of visibility, readability of computer screens; eye movement and tracking |
| Hearing | Pitch, frequency, volume — mobile phones |
| Touch | Texture, grip, friction, temperature — keyboards, dentistry tools |
| Taste | Ingestion of toxins from children's toys |
| Smell | Aroma, perfume, odours in workspaces |

**Mobile phones.** Design variations in **colour, shape, materials, backlighting and surface
finish** are combined to attract different consumer groups. **It is not just the functions and
services that sell phones** — physical design variations meeting **psychological needs** matter too.

### Environmental psychology

**Definition.** The study of the **relationship between an environment and how it affects its
inhabitants**.

Typical factors for an indoor office: **lighting, acoustics, air quality, temperature, worker
densities**.

### Thermal comfort — the number to quote

**Air temperature, such as radiant temperature, has the strongest influence on humans** because of
the **absorptive nature of the human body**. Higher temperatures lead to **heat stress and
eventually heat exhaustion**.

> **"It is generally considered that when 80% of a given population feels comfortable then a
> situation of 'reasonable comfort' is achieved."**

**Air temperature alone is not a valid indicator of reasonable comfort.** Four grouped factors:

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Air quality** | Usually **air conditioning**, controlling **ventilation, air movement and humidity**, filtering pollutants — but AC itself can create **air movement, sub-optimal temperatures and noise** |
| **Building acoustics** | **Quiet spaces** encourage concentration by minimising distraction |
| **Lighting** | **Natural light and views outside**; **adjustable task lighting** lets individuals control conditions for the task |
| **Worker densities** | **Adjustable and adaptable spaces**, flexible enough to be personalised to a work style |

### Open-plan offices

Developed for **greater worker density** and **unrestricted space**, by reducing interior walls and
replacing them with partitions, or reducing partition size.

- **Aims:** remove **barriers to communication**, give a **perception of space**, and allow **freer
  air movement and light distribution** from windows.
- **Disadvantages:** **reduced barriers to noise transfer**, **reduced personal privacy**, **greater
  visual distraction**.

Standard space allocations are often based on **job-specific tasks, seniority or status**.

### Defensible space

Personal space requirements **vary and may be influenced by culture or upbringing**. This comfort
zone is called **"defensible space"** — a term **coined by John Calhoun in the 1940s**.

It is built into office design to overcome the **sense of overcrowding** and the negative behaviours
it creates. **Judicious use of barriers or partitions** creates personal space while keeping an open
plan, letting employees **customise their space** and improving their **feeling of comfort, safety
and control**.

**PWESQ.** Designers may use the **Physical Work Environment Satisfaction Questionnaire** to measure
worker satisfaction — covering environmental factors relative to **occupational health and safety**,
plus **physical demands and work systems**.

## Section 1.9 — B 1.1.1 Understanding needs

> **B 1.1.1** User-centred design (UCD) requires a plan to structure an inquiry using user-centred
> research methods. *(© IBO 2025)*

**Definition (learn this wording).**

> "User-centred design (UCD) focuses on understanding the needs, preferences and limitations of
> end-users throughout the product development process. It involves actively engaging users in an
> **iterative process**, gathering feedback and using that information to prioritise the user when
> creating products and services that are **usable, accessible and enjoyable**."

### The four question sets

The book gives four groups of questions designers ask — in surveys, focus groups or interviews.
These are highly quotable in an extended response.

**Understanding user needs**
- Who is the target market?
- What are the critical functions users need to achieve with this product?
- What problems are an issue for users of current product solutions?
- What are the product expectations of users?

**Context of use**
- What environmental or contextual considerations apply when users interact with this product?
- What products are currently available that meet the needs of the design brief?
- What physical, social or cultural factors need to be taken into account?

**User experience**
- What are users' expectations for this product?
- Is this a new problem?
- How do current products solve the issues being addressed?
- What do users consider product-critical features?

**Usability and accessibility**
- What user accessibility issues need consideration?
- What usability issues are dealt with by existing products?
- How can the product be made intuitive and easy to learn?

### Three critical planning considerations

1. Determining **optimal methods for gathering and incorporating user feedback**.
2. Selecting appropriate techniques for **data collection, analysis and presentation**.
3. Ensuring **insights, decisions and iterations** derived from user data are **effectively
   integrated into the overall design process**.

**Worked example — the cockpit.** The traditional aeroplane cockpit has become the modern **"glass
cockpit"**, adding information such as weather radar — but the **traditional information (attitude,
altitude, airspeed) remains mainly in the nearest panel**. New capability, without displacing what
the pilot reaches for first.

## Section 1.10 — User-centred research methods

### Field research

**Definition.** Observing people in their **natural environment** to understand their needs and
normal behaviour, **typically carried out early in the design process**.

**Techniques:** field trials, **ethnographic** interviews, observation of everyday activities.
*(Ethnographic = the scientific description of peoples and cultures with their customs, habits and
mutual differences.)*

Because it happens in the natural environment:
- data is obtained **in the context of use**
- **previously unrecognised issues** are discovered
- there are **no artificial effects** of the kind laboratory testing generates

### Method of extremes

**Definition.** Looks at the **extremes of the user population distribution**; these positions,
along with the mean, are used to design equipment for general use.

| Design element | Percentile used |
|---|---|
| **Doorways, ladders, step heights, escape hatches** | **95th percentile of males** |
| **Forces to operate control panel buttons** | **5th percentile of females** |

"By adopting these extremes the greatest number of users is accommodated" — but by its nature this
**excludes members of the population beyond the chosen extremes**.

**Link to disability.** Designing for extremes "often includes, and even prioritises, considering
the needs of people with disabilities, as they represent a group facing unique challenges that, when
addressed, can lead to better designs for everyone."

**Universal design — know the contrast.** Because people with disabilities can have needs outside
the able-bodied 95th percentile population, designs including them are called **universal design**.
It **differs from designing for extremes** by aiming to create designs **usable by all people to the
greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialised design**.

### Observation

**Definition.** Watching users as they use a product, in a **controlled laboratory** or in the
**field**. The purpose is to gather information on **how a product is used** and **any usability
problems** encountered.

The **observer effect** must be recognised and minimised — the presence of the observer changes what
the user does.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Can uncover **previously unrecognised** usability problems | Data can be **complex to analyse** |
| Products tested under **actual conditions of use** | **Environmental noise** may disguise small effects |
| | Usually only performed on **finished** products |

### Interviews — three forms

Interviews can be **structured, semi-structured or unstructured**.

**Unstructured** use predominantly **open-ended questions** — requiring more than one or two words,
letting the interviewee answer in their own words. Examples the book gives:
- What did you find were the most useful features?
- Were there other features you would have liked?
- In what situations do you think this product would be most useful?

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Interviewee can **ask for clarification** if intent is unclear | **Time-consuming** |
| | Small groups **may not represent all usability issues** |
| | Respondents **are not anonymous**, so answers may be influenced by a wish to please the interviewer |

**Structured (standardised)** interviews — also called **researcher-administered** — ask **closed
questions** with fixed responses or yes/no answers. Their purpose is a **fair, objective and
consistent evaluation** through a standardised question set and evaluation criteria. Example:
*"Did you like the positioning of the sound controls?"* Follow-up questions are still allowed to
clarify ambiguity.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Research suggests they are **better at predicting actual user experiences** than unstructured | The **predetermined nature limits flexibility**, restricting follow-up or nuance |
| **Consistency** across participants → more reliable, valid assessments | |
| Standardised format makes **data collection and comparison easier** | |

### Likert scale

Developed by the American social psychologist **Rensis Likert in 1932**, it measures attitudes and
opinions on a scale **typically 1 to 5**. Likert surveys are **structured questionnaires**; they can
be used **within** a structured interview or **standalone**.

*Figure B 1.1.2a* is a five-point **"Website User Survey"** running **strongly agree → strongly
disagree**.

### Focus groups

**Definition.** An evaluation tool used in **market research and usability testing** to gauge the
opinions and experiences of the public on a specific topic.

**Size: no more than 8–12 participants**, facilitated by a leader.

**The leader's duties:** introduce the topic; maintain the discussion if it stalls; ensure all
participants can express an opinion; redirect discussion on topic **without stifling interaction**.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Uncover **previously unrecognised** usability problems | Data is predominantly **qualitative** |
| Usable throughout the process, **especially useful early** | **Group dynamics** can be disruptive |
| | Small groups may not represent all usability issues |

### Questionnaires

**Definition.** Self-administered questionnaires are a printed list of questions completed by the
respondent **without outside interference**. Two question groups: **fixed-response** and
**open-ended**.

**Fixed-response** aim at **quantitative data** — a selection of responses or a rating scale,
commonly a **Likert scale**, with numerical values assigned for analysis. Responses are summed and
averaged, then analysed statistically by age, gender and so on.

**Reliability and validity — a likely definition question.**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | The **repeatability** of the response to the question |
| **Validity** | The degree to which the question **measures what is intended** |

Such instruments take **considerable time to construct and validate**, because producing a single
overall number requires the questions to be **of equal value**.

**Open-ended** questionnaires let respondents answer in their own words. They can uncover
**unexpected information** but take **considerably more time to analyse**, and gather **qualitative
data**. They are useful in the **early stages**; fixed-response questionnaires are used in **later
evaluations**.

| Advantages of questionnaires | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Cover a **wide geographic area** | Without guaranteed **anonymity**, answers may be inaccurate |
| **Interviewer observation bias eliminated** | Only a **fraction are returned** |
| A **large sample** can be surveyed | Returns may be a **biased population** — only the interested respond |

### System Usability Scale (SUS)

A standard fixed-response questionnaire evaluated for reliability and validity, **developed by John
Brooke in 1986**. Ten statements; **odd-numbered questions score highly for good usability, even
numbered score low** *(Figure B 1.1.2b)*.

**Scoring** — converts raw scores to a value between **0 and 100**:

1. Odd questions (1, 3, 5, 7, 9): **subtract 1** from the user's response.
2. Even questions (2, 4, 6, 8, 10): **subtract the response from 5**.
3. Add the adjusted scores.
4. **Multiply by 2.5**.

$$
\text{SUS} = 2.5\Big[\textstyle\sum_{\text{odd}}(Q_i - 1) + \sum_{\text{even}}(5 - Q_i)\Big]
$$

**The benchmark: the average SUS score across industries and products is around 68.** Higher means
better usability; the book's worked example scores **40**, indicating usability problems.

> [!warning] The book's worked example does not add up
> Its bracket terms are printed as `3+0+3+3+2+0+0+3+2+1`, which totals **17**, but it then states
> the sum as **16** and multiplies to get 40. One term is a misprint. Use the **method**, not that
> arithmetic.

## Section 1.11 — B 1.1.3 Personae

> **B 1.1.3** Data collected from user-centred research is used to determine personae that represent
> attributes of user populations. *(© IBO 2025)*

### User population

Large groups are defined by common characteristics — **age, gender, physical condition, handedness,
ethnicity** — but user-research classification is defined by **common goals or needs**. A group
defined that way is a **user population**.

### Persona

**Definition.** Personae are **fictional constructs derived from ethnographic research and field
trials** that let researchers focus on the behaviour of a user group with common goals. **While
fictional, they are not hypothetical** — they embody extensive ethnographic research.

A persona embodies what was seen during **observation**, rather than what group members **say** they
would do in focus groups and surveys.

### What a persona lets designers do

- Prioritise design activities
- Focus development on user goals
- Understand customer motivations
- Identify opportunities and shortcomings within a market
- Test and validate design concepts against user requirements
- Evaluate designs **without numerous usability tests**
- Avoid costly, time-consuming research across the whole user community
- Create a **standardised reference** for consistency, reducing disagreements

### Primary, secondary and anti-personae

| Type | Who it represents |
|---|---|
| **Primary persona** | The **primary user population** — the design must satisfy these requirements |
| **Secondary persona** | Secondary user populations — usability is **extended to them where possible** |
| **Anti-persona** | Someone the design is **not** intended for |

**Making them real.** A physical and social context is produced, often including a **photograph** to
put a face to the persona, building empathy in the team. A profile may include: **age, health,
gender, interests, activities, life goals, education, motivation, employment, expectations, marital
status, organisations/affiliations**.

### Anti-personae — the book's definition and cases

The **Nielsen Norman Group**, who have worked in UCD for more than 25 years, define an anti-persona
as **"a representation of a user group that could misuse a product in ways that negatively impact
target users and the business"**.

They are developed to test for **safety issues, security loopholes, risk of product damage and
marketing**.

| Case | What the anti-persona does |
|---|---|
| **McDonald's** | Establishes marketing practices ensuring they **do not target or misinform vegans**, who would not want to buy a hamburger |
| **Trainline** | Allows **non-purchasers to plan a journey**, ensuring a positive experience even when the user is not a paying client |
| **Systems / cyber-security** | Develops defences against **hackers** attempting **data breaches, identity theft or denial-of-service attacks** |

### Use case

**Definition.** A **written description of how a user will interact with a product or design, seen
from the user's perspective**, giving insight into usability as experienced by the user.

## Section 1.12 — C 1.1.1 Responsibility

> **C 1.1.1** A designer has a responsibility to the needs of clients, their community and the
> environment when designing and creating products. *(© IBO 2025)*

Note the three parties: **client, community, environment** — and that the book explicitly calls them
**"sometimes competing interests"**. That tension is what an evaluation question wants.

### The designer's role

Designers translate **user needs and business goals** into tangible, usable, aesthetically pleasing
products, fostering innovation through **research, prototyping and iteration**, and ensuring success
**throughout the product's lifecycle**. They are a **bridge between creativity and technical
feasibility**.

### What they are responsible for

The book's phrase is the **"rational utilisation of"**:

1. materials
2. manufacturing processes
3. energy usage
4. recyclability
5. reusability

Discharged by **maintaining a critical contemporary understanding of the science and technologies
necessary to create designs** — and this is **not a one-off activity** but a commitment to continued
innovation. That commitment has a name: **Kaizen**.

### Kaizen and the PDCA cycle

**Kaizen** is Japanese for **"continuous improvement"** or **"change for the better"** — the idea
that **small, gradual changes over time lead to significant improvements**. It emphasises
understanding customer needs and **testing improvements on a small scale before updating
company-wide procedures**.

| Who | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **W. Edward Deming** | American business theorist who introduced it to **Japan in the 1950s**, during **post-war industrial reconstruction** |
| **Walter A. Shewhart** | American engineer whose work Deming championed; emphasised **using statistics to study the effects of changes** |
| **The Shewhart Cycle** | **PDSA — Plan, Do, Study, Act** |
| **Through Deming's work in Japan** | Became **PDCA — Plan, Do, Check, Act** |

*Figure C1.1.1a — the Deming PDCA cycle of continuous improvement.*

### Environmental sustainability

**The Brundtland Report**, also known as **"Our Common Future"**, was published in **1987** by the
**United Nations World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED)**, chaired by **Gro Harlem
Brundtland**. It addressed **environmental degradation and its relationship to development**, and
**popularised the term "sustainable development"**.

**Sustainable products** are made from **renewable resources**, use **minimal energy**, and are
**recyclable or reusable** — often called **eco-friendly**.

| Example | Why it qualifies |
|---|---|
| **Reusable bags** | Organic cotton; cost-effective alternative to plastic |
| **Recycled plastic bottles** | **PET** is first made from fossil fuels, but can be **repeatedly recycled**, reducing virgin plastic and landfill |
| **Solar energy devices** | Renewable source; reduce reliance on finite fossil fuels; minimal operating impact |
| **Biodegradable products** | Break down naturally or are digested by **fungi and bacteria**, or compost |
| **Recycled clothing** | Reduces the **carbon footprint of the supply chain**; diverts clothing from landfill |

**Other sustainable choices:** low-impact shipping materials and methods; natural, organic or
plant-based ingredients; brands donating proceeds to charity; **buying locally** to cut
long-distance shipping pollution.

### Negative impacts — the book's list

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Microplastics** | Products containing them |
| **PFAS, PCBs, dioxin** | **"Forever chemicals"** — resist degradation, accumulate in groundwater, cause health problems. PFAS widely used in **firefighting foam** |
| **CFCs and HCFCs** | Chemical aerosol emissions reaching the stratosphere and **affecting the ozone layer** |
| **Disposable plastic containers** | A significant proportion reaches the oceans, hazardous to marine life |
| **Fast fashion** | Rapid production of cheap trend-mimicking clothing, then discarded. Contributes **up to 8–10% of global CO₂**; **≈87% of textile waste goes to landfill each year** |
| **Mercury** | From **gold mining** and chemical processes. Causes **kidney damage, impaired hearing, vision and balance, coma and death** |
| **Lead, sulphur, NOₓ, PM2.5** | From vehicle fuel — aggravate **asthma**, cause **cardiovascular issues and neurological damage** |

**Minamata disease** arose in the **1960s**, named after the **Japanese village of Minamata**, where
residents were poisoned by eating **fish contaminated with methylmercury discharged from a chemical
factory into coastal water**.

**The counter-example.** *"Not all mining operations are environmentally irresponsible"* — the
**Kalgoorlie "Super Pit"** in **Western Australia** is cited as an operator mindful of its
responsibilities. Useful for a balanced evaluation answer.

## Section 1.13 — C 1.1.2 Safety

> **C 1.1.2** It is a designer's responsibility to ensure their products are safe to use.
> *(© IBO 2025)*

### Standardisation

**Definition.** The establishment of **common practices, methods or formats to ensure consistency**,
occurring at levels from **international to country, state or company-specific**.

The prime example is **weights and measures**. Before standardisation, *"a 'foot' could be different
lengths in different places"*, causing **confusion, difficulties in trade and inaccuracies**.
Standardisation created a **common language for quantifying things**.

### Product standards

**Definition.** **Documented guidelines specifying technical requirements for a product, ensuring
its safety, quality, consistency and suitability for its intended use** — a **benchmark for product
quality and performance**.

**Named standards — children's toys:**

| Standard | Scope |
|---|---|
| **ASTM F963** (USA) | *Standard Consumer Safety Specification for Toy Safety* — **material properties** (toxicology, flammability) and **labelling of age range and functional hazards for children under 14** |
| **ISO 8124.1** (international) | *Safety of Toys, Part 1: mechanical and physical properties*; subsequent parts cover **flammability and toxicology** |

**Benefit to designers:** a **clear framework for ensuring safety, reducing design risks and
promoting consumer confidence**.

### Why standards help designers

- Set criteria for **design, materials, manufacturing, performance and testing**
- **A feature of mature markets** — assist **quality assurance, component supply**, and the costs of
  **expensive R&D**, giving companies confidence to develop for world markets
- Act as a **common language** in a globalised world, enabling international collaboration
- Strategically, companies well placed in the standards process may **favourably influence the
  standard** itself

*Figure C 1.1.2b — Standardisation Space (Sanders, ISO, 1972),* an orthogonal three-axis model:

| Axis | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **X — Domains** | The subjects of standardisation |
| **Y — Aspects** | Terminology; specification; sampling and inspection; tests and analysis; limitation of variety; grading; code of practice; packaging, conservation, transport |
| **Z — Levels** | **Company → National → Regional → International** — from company standards voluntarily adopted as indicators of good practice, to those **mandated and regulated by governments** |

### Enforcement authorities

Products are **affixed with a mark indicating compliance** *(Figure C 1.1.2c)*. Countries maintain
authorities enforcing **mandatory product safety and information standards**:

- **ACCC** — Australian Competition & Consumer Commission
- **CPSC** — United States Consumer Product Safety Commission
- **PROSAFE** — Product Safety Enforcement Forum of Europe

### Interchangeability

**Component standardisation** reduces variability, allowing **interchangeability of components
across products and markets**, and improving manufacturing efficiency through **economy of scale and
simplified processes**.

### Case study — the flintlock musket

The book's set-piece on interchangeable parts. Know the names and dates.

| Year | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| **1776** | **Lt Gen Jean-Baptiste Vaquette de Gribeauval** (French inspector of artillery) | Introduced mass production of interchangeable parts — the **système Gribeauval** — for **cannons and cannonballs**, making them **lighter and more mobile** with interchangeable **carriages and ammunition**, allowing **faster deployment and more accurate firing** |
| **1778** | **Honoré Blanc** | Extended it to the **manufacture and repair of the flintlock musket**. Components were **still handmade**, but **standardised jigs and gauges** made them precise enough to be interchangeable |
| **1816–1818** | **John Hancock Hall (1816)**, **Thomas Blanchard (1817)**, **Simon North (1818)** | American engineers who independently introduced machinery allowing **mechanised mass production** and **true interchangeability** |

**The problem it solved.** Repair previously required a **skilled armourer or gunsmith**, because
each weapon had **fragile, oddly shaped handmade components**. Damaged parts could not be swapped in
the field — weapons returned to the armoury, causing **long delays**.

**Modern equivalent — bolts.** Standardised bolt design gives confidence in interchangeability
between manufacturers worldwide, and standardised **grade (Property Class) classifications** give
confidence in **material properties such as strength**, marked **on the bolt head**.

## Section 1.14 — C 1.1.3 Obsolescence

> **C 1.1.3** Products can become obsolete due to a number of factors and this can be planned.
> *(© IBO 2025)*

**Four types: planned, style (fashion), functional, technological.**

> [!warning] The book has a hole here
> Sample questions 1 and 9 — and a linking question — ask about **social obsolescence**, but the
> chapter **never defines it**. If it comes up, treat it as obsolescence driven by **changing social
> norms and expectations** rather than by the product, and say you are reasoning by analogy.

### 1. Planned obsolescence

**Definition.** Also called **built-in obsolescence** — a **deliberate tactic to create products
with a limited lifespan**, designed to **force consumers to purchase repeatedly**, boosting sales
while maintaining a competitive edge. It creates a **desire for replacement or update rather than
repair**.

**Case study — General Electric, 1930s.** GE improved the **efficiency of flashlight lamps** through
a corresponding **decrease in their lifespan**. Engineers were **encouraged to build items with a
limited lifespan to increase sales and boost the economy**; proponents accepted this *"new ethical
paradigm with almost patriotic vigour"*.

**Design features of planned obsolescence:**
- low durability
- style obsolescence
- not easily or cheaply repaired
- difficult to disassemble, restricting maintenance
- regular introduction of newer technologies or "improved" models

**Value engineering.** These features let manufacturers **cut costs and boost sales** — but as
markets mature and competition increases, **pressure builds to increase product lifespans** as
consumers migrate to more reliable, durable brands.

| Advantages for consumers | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Opportunities to **upgrade as new technologies arrive** | **Constant need for replacement** and the inconvenience |
| Access to the **latest fashion trends** | **Lack of repairability** forces purchase before ready, or replacement of a model whose features suited them |
| **Volume sales** strengthen the market, with knock-on effects on **national economies and local jobs** | New models bring **new ways of operating**, which can be **disconcerting** |

**Environmental impact:** more **waste, pollution, energy consumption and resource consumption** as
consumers **replace rather than repair**.

**The book's paired image.** A **1950s computer with vacuum tubes — unreliable but repairable**,
against a **2000s computer with integrated circuits — reliable but unrepairable.** That trade-off in
one line is worth quoting.

### 2. Style (fashion) obsolescence

**Fashion** is a **currently popular style or practice**, subject to trends in **shape, form, colour,
style, materials and finish**, across **vehicles, architecture, food, clothing, jewellery, footwear**.

**Perceived value versus cost.** **Value for money** relates to **cost and performance**; **perceived
value** also includes **prestige, rarity and other social implications**.

**The Veblen effect.** Named after **Thorstein Bunde Veblen (1857–1929)**, describing
**counter-intuitive consumer behaviour** — the **prestige of owning a similar item branded
differently but priced at a premium** still draws customers, because **pricing is taken as an
indication of quality** and **conspicuous consumption** confers **social prestige**.

**Fads** are trends lasting **merely months** rather than years. They arise from **social influence,
the desire to fit in, novelty seeking, the power of celebrities and influencers on TikTok, YouTube
and Instagram**, and **rapid dissemination through marketing and popular culture**.

| Case study | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Zoot suits (1930s–40s)** | **High-waisted baggy trousers with a long watch chain**, a **long coat with wide lapels**, often a **pork pie hat**. Popular among **African American, Mexican American, Filipino American, Italian American and Japanese American men**. Bandleader **Cab Calloway** called them *"totally and truly American"*; critics called them **unpatriotic for wasteful use of material during WWII** |
| **Paisley print (1960s)** | The tear-drop pattern is **centuries old**, but became **key to hippie culture** in the mid-late 1960s, representing **rebellion and non-conformity**, tied to the **psychedelic style and free love movement** |
| **Bell-bottom jeans (1960s–70s)** | **Flared at the knee**; fell out of fashion in the **late 1970s** as **1950s straight jeans** returned — then **came back in the mid-1990s to 2000s as "boot-cut"**. A useful counter-example: obsolescence is **not always one-way** |
| **Shoulder pads (1930s–40s, 1980s–early 90s)** | First reflected the **military styling** of the time; returned in the 1980s as **"power dressing"**, a **broader, more commanding silhouette** letting women project authority in male-dominated workplaces. Popularised by **Dynasty**, **Dallas**, **Princess Diana**, **Margaret Thatcher**, **Michelle Pfeiffer**, and **Joan Crawford (1946)** |

**Named fads:** **Troll Dolls (1960s, 1990s)**, **Pet Rocks (1975)**, **Beanie Babies (mid-1990s)**,
**Tamagotchi (mid-1990s to early 2000s)**.

### 3. Functional obsolescence

**Definition.** When something becomes **less useful or desirable due to an outdated design or
functionality, even if the technology itself still works**.

Applies to **real estate, technology and other assets**, and leads to **loss of value or
depreciation**. It is **difficult to correct because the outdated design is not easily updated** —
though a **comeback is possible**, e.g. a company redesigning furniture to match current trends.

**Examples:**
- A property needing you to **walk through one bedroom to reach another**, or with **bedrooms and
  bathrooms on separate floors**, is less desirable and appraises lower
- Dwellings with **inadequate electrical systems** for modern appliances
- Absence of modern conveniences such as **indoor plumbing**
- **New smartphone models make older ones less desirable**
- **Manufacturers no longer support updates or repairs**
- **Changes in electronics causing incompatibility**

### 4. Technological obsolescence

**Definition.** Shares many features with functional obsolescence — when a technology is **no longer
useful or efficient compared to newer alternatives**. Crucially, **technological obsolescence can
occur well before functional obsolescence**. Applies to **hardware and software**.

| Domain | The chain |
|---|---|
| **Timekeeping** | **Hourglass** and **sundial** → **clocks and electronic timers** (the old forms survive **ornamentally**) |
| **Refrigeration** | **Ice chests** → **refrigerator**, early 20th century |
| **Displays** | **CRT** → **Plasma** → **LCD** and **OLED** |
| **Sound recording** | **Edison's cylinders (1877)** → **shellac 78s (1898)** → **polyvinyl 45 and 33 rpm (1948)** → **magnetic tape (1960s)** → **Compact Disc (1980)** → **digital files and streaming** |
| **Electronics** | **Vacuum tubes** → **transistors**, introducing **miniaturisation** |
| **Steel making** | **Bessemer Converter (1856)** → **Open Hearth Furnace (1860s)** → **Basic Oxygen Furnace (1948)** and **Electric Arc Furnace** (commercial 1901, widespread from the **1960s** as **steel scrap** became available) |
| **Data storage** | **8", 5" and 3½" floppy discs (1970s–1990s)** → **CD-ROM, DVD, USB flash drives** |

The sound recording and steel making chains are the two most quotable — each shows **several
generations of replacement**, which is what an "explain technological obsolescence" question wants.
